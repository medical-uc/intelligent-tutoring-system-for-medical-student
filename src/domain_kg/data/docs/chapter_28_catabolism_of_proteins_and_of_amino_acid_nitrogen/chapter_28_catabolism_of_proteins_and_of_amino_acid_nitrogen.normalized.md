# PROTEIN TURNOVER

The continuous degradation and synthesis, or turnover, of cellular proteins occur in all forms of life. Humans turn over 1 to 2% of their total body protein each day, principally from muscle. High rates of protein degradation occur in tissues undergoing structural rearrangement, such as uterine tissue during pregnancy. High rates of protein degradation also occur in skeletal muscle during starvation. High rates of protein degradation occur in tadpole tail tissue during metamorphosis. Approximately 75% of the amino acids liberated by protein degradation are reutilized. The remaining excess free amino acids are rapidly degraded. The major portion of the carbon skeletons of the amino acids is converted to amphibolic intermediates. In humans, the amino nitrogen is converted to urea and excreted in the urine.

Protein half-lives of liver proteins can range from under 30 minutes to over 150 hours. Ubiquitin is a small polypeptide, 8.5 kDa in size and consisting of 76 residues, present in all eukaryotic cells. The primary structure of ubiquitin is highly conserved, with only 3 of 76 residues differing between yeast and human ubiquitin. # ANIMALS CONVERT α-AMINO NITROGEN TO VARIED END PRODUCTS

Teleostean fish are ammonotelic and excrete ammonia into their aqueous environment. Ammonia is highly toxic, but the aquatic environment provides a large sink for its dispersion. Birds are uricotelic and excrete nitrogen-rich uric acid as semisolid guano. Birds need to conserve water and maintain low weight, which is addressed by excreting uric acid. Many land animals, including humans, are ureotelic and excrete nontoxic, highly water-soluble urea. Urea is nontoxic to humans. High blood levels of urea are associated with renal disease. High blood urea levels are a consequence, not a cause, of impaired kidney function.

## Transamination Transfers α-Amino Nitrogen to α-Ketoglutarate, Forming Glutamate

Transamination reactions interconvert pairs of α-amino acids and α-keto acids (Figure 28--8). Transamination reactions are freely reversible. Transamination reactions also function in amino acid biosynthesis. All common amino acids except lysine, threonine, proline, and hydroxyproline participate in transamination. Transamination is not restricted to α-amino groups. The δ-amino group of ornithine readily undergoes transamination. The ε-amino group of lysine does not undergo transamination.

[FIGURE:fig-28-8]

Transamination. The reaction is freely reversible with an equilibrium constant close to unity.

Alanine-pyruvate aminotransferase catalyzes the transfer of amino groups to pyruvate, forming alanine. Glutamate-α-ketoglutarate aminotransferase catalyzes the transfer of amino groups to α-ketoglutarate, forming glutamate. Each aminotransferase is specific for one pair of substrates. Each aminotransferase is nonspecific for the other pair of substrates. Alanine is a substrate for glutamate aminotransferase. The α-amino nitrogen from amino acids that undergo transamination can be concentrated in glutamate. L-glutamate is the only amino acid that undergoes oxidative deamination at an appreciable rate in mammalian tissues. The formation of ammonia from α-amino groups occurs mainly via the α-amino nitrogen of L-glutamate.

Transamination occurs via a 'ping-pong' mechanism. The 'ping-pong' mechanism is characterized by the alternate addition of a substrate and release of a product. Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) is present at the catalytic site of all aminotransferases. PLP plays a key role in catalysis. PLP serves as a 'carrier' of amino groups during transamination. An enzyme-bound Schiff base is formed between the oxo group of enzyme-bound PLP and the α-amino group of an α-amino acid (Figure 28--9). The Schiff base can rearrange in various ways. In transamination, the shifting of the N=C double bond by hydration-dehydration produces an α-keto acid and enzyme-bound pyridoxamine phosphate. After removal of its α-amino nitrogen by transamination, the remaining carbon 'skeleton' of an amino acid is degraded by pathways discussed in Chapter 29.

## Ammonia Intoxication Is Life-Threatening

Enteric bacteria produce ammonia, which is absorbed into portal venous blood.

Tissues also produce ammonia.

The liver rapidly removes ammonia from circulation and converts it to urea.

Normally, only traces of ammonia (10-20 μg/dL) are present in peripheral blood.

Ammonia is toxic to the central nervous system.

If portal blood bypasses the liver, systemic blood ammonia may reach toxic levels.

Severely impaired hepatic function can lead to elevated systemic blood ammonia levels.

The development of collateral links between the portal and systemic veins in cirrhosis can lead to elevated systemic blood ammonia levels.

Symptoms of ammonia intoxication include tremor, slurred speech, blurred vision, coma, and ultimately death.

Ammonia may be toxic to the brain because it reacts with α-ketoglutarate to form glutamate.

The depletion of α-ketoglutarate impairs the function of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in neurons.

# GENERAL FEATURES OF METABOLIC DISORDERS

Metabolic disorders associated with the enzymes of urea biosynthesis are rare but well-characterized and medically devastating. Metabolic disorders illustrate general principles of inherited metabolic diseases. Similar or identical clinical signs and symptoms can accompany various genetic mutations in a gene that encodes a given enzyme. Similar symptoms can also accompany mutations in enzymes that catalyze successive reactions in a metabolic pathway. Rational therapy for these disorders is based on an understanding of the relevant biochemical enzyme-catalyzed reactions in both normal and impaired individuals. The identification of intermediates and ancillary products that accumulate prior to a metabolic block provides the basis for metabolic screening tests. Metabolic screening tests can implicate the reaction that is impaired. Definitive diagnosis of these disorders involves quantitative assay of the activity of the enzyme suspected to be defective. The DNA sequence of the gene that encodes a given mutant enzyme is compared to that of the wild-type gene to identify the specific mutation(s) that cause the disease. The exponential increase in DNA sequencing of human genes has identified dozens of mutations of an affected gene. Some of these mutations are innocuous, or 'silent,' while others are associated with symptoms of varying severity of a given metabolic disorder.

# METABOLIC DISORDERS ARE ASSOCIATED WITH EACH REACTION OF THE UREA CYCLE

Five well-documented diseases represent defects in the biosynthesis of enzymes of the urea cycle. Urea cycle disorders are characterized by hyperammonemia, encephalopathy, and respiratory alkalosis. Four of the five metabolic diseases are deficiencies of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, ornithine carbamoyl transferase, argininosuccinate synthetase, and argininosuccinate lyase. Deficiencies of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, ornithine carbamoyl transferase, argininosuccinate synthetase, and argininosuccinate lyase result in the accumulation of precursors of urea, principally ammonia and glutamine. Ammonia intoxication is most severe when the metabolic block occurs at reaction 1 or 2. If citrulline can be synthesized, some ammonia has already been removed by being covalently linked to an organic metabolite.

Clinical symptoms common to all urea cycle disorders include vomiting, avoidance of high-protein foods, intermittent ataxia, irritability, lethargy, and severe mental retardation. The most dramatic clinical presentation occurs in full-term infants who initially appear normal. Full-term infants then exhibit progressive lethargy, hypothermia, and apnea due to high plasma ammonia levels. The clinical features and treatment of all five disorders are similar. A low-protein diet ingested as frequent small meals can lead to significant improvement and minimization of brain damage. A low-protein diet helps to avoid sudden increases in blood ammonia levels. The goal of dietary therapy is to provide sufficient protein, arginine, and energy to promote growth and development while simultaneously minimizing the metabolic perturbations.

## Argininosuccinate Lyase

Argininosuccinic aciduria is associated with elevated levels of argininosuccinate in blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and urine. Argininosuccinic aciduria is associated with friable, tufted hair known as trichorrhexis nodosa. There are both early-onset and late-onset types of argininosuccinic aciduria. The metabolic defect in argininosuccinic aciduria is in argininosuccinate lyase. The reaction involving argininosuccinate lyase is reaction 4 in Figure 28--13. Diagnosis of argininosuccinic aciduria can be made by measuring erythrocyte argininosuccinate lyase activity. Erythrocyte argininosuccinate lyase activity can be measured in umbilical cord blood. Erythrocyte argininosuccinate lyase activity can also be measured in amniotic fluid cells.

## Arginase

Hyperargininemia is an autosomal recessive defect in the gene for arginase.

The first symptoms of hyperargininemia typically do not appear until age 2 to 4 years.

Blood levels of arginine are elevated in hyperargininemia.

Cerebrospinal fluid levels of arginine are elevated in hyperargininemia.

The urinary amino acid pattern in hyperargininemia resembles that of lysine-cystinuria.

Arginine competes with lysine and cysteine for reabsorption in the renal tubule.

## Analysis of Neonate Blood by Tandem Mass Spectrometry Can Detect Metabolic Diseases

Metabolic diseases can be devastating when caused by the absence or functional impairment of metabolic enzymes.

Early dietary intervention can ameliorate the effects of metabolic diseases in many instances.

Early detection of metabolic diseases is of primary importance.

Tandem mass spectrometry (MS) is a powerful and sensitive technique that can detect numerous analytes significant in identifying metabolic disorders in a few minutes.

Exome sequencing can elucidate the specific genetic defect responsible for metabolic diseases.

Researchers have used gene editing to correct deficiencies in argininosuccinate lyase in experimental animals in recent trials.
