# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Cholesterol is present in tissues and in plasma either as free cholesterol or combined with a long-chain fatty acid as cholesteryl ester, the storage form. In plasma, both forms are transported in lipoproteins. Cholesterol is an amphipathic lipid. Cholesterol is an essential structural component of membranes. Cholesterol is important for the maintenance of the correct permeability and fluidity of membranes. Cholesterol is an important component of the outer layer of plasma lipoproteins. Cholesterol is synthesized in many tissues from acetyl-CoA. Cholesterol is the precursor of all other steroids in the body, including corticosteroids, sex hormones, bile acids, and vitamin D. Cholesterol occurs in foods of animal origin such as egg yolk, meat, liver, and brain. Plasma low-density lipoprotein (LDL) supplies cholesterol and cholesteryl ester to many tissues. Free cholesterol is removed from tissues by plasma high-density lipoprotein (HDL). HDL transports free cholesterol to the liver. The liver eliminates cholesterol from the body either unchanged or after conversion to bile acids. Cholesterol plays a central role in pathologic processes. Cholesterol is a factor in the development of atherosclerosis of vital arteries. Atherosclerosis causes cerebrovascular, coronary, and peripheral vascular disease. Cholesterol is a major constituent of gallstones.

# CHOLESTEROL IS BIOSYNTHESIZED FROM ACETYL-CoA

Cholesterol is biosynthesized from acetyl-CoA. About 700 mg of cholesterol is synthesized daily in the body. The liver accounts for approximately 10% of total cholesterol synthesis in humans. The intestine accounts for approximately 10% of total cholesterol synthesis in humans. The remainder of the body's cholesterol is provided by the average diet. Virtually all tissues containing nucleated cells are capable of cholesterol synthesis. Cholesterol synthesis occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum. Cholesterol synthesis occurs in the cytosolic compartments.

## Acetyl-CoA Is the Source of All Carbon Atoms in Cholesterol

Cholesterol is a 27-carbon compound consisting of four rings and a side chain. Cholesterol is synthesized from acetyl-CoA by a lengthy pathway that may be divided into five steps. The first step is the synthesis of mevalonate. The second step is the formation of isoprenoid units. The third step is the condensation of six isoprenoid units to form squalene. The fourth step is the synthesis of lanosterol from squalene. The fifth step is the formation of cholesterol from lanosterol.

HMG-CoA (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA) is formed by the reactions used in mitochondria to synthesize ketone bodies. Two molecules of acetyl-CoA condense to form acetoacetyl-CoA, catalyzed by cytosolic thiolase. Acetoacetyl-CoA condenses with a further molecule of acetyl-CoA to form HMG-CoA, catalyzed by HMG-CoA synthase. HMG-CoA is reduced to mevalonate by NADPH in a reaction catalyzed by HMG-CoA reductase. HMG-CoA is reduced to mevalonate by NADPH in a reaction catalyzed by HMG-CoA reductase is the principal regulatory step in the pathway of cholesterol synthesis. Statins are HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. Statins are the most effective class of cholesterol-lowering drugs.

Mevalonate is phosphorylated sequentially using ATP by three kinases. After decarboxylation, the active isoprenoid unit, isopentenyl diphosphate, is formed.

Isopentenyl diphosphate is isomerized by a shift of the double bond to form dimethylallyl diphosphate. Dimethylallyl diphosphate is condensed with another molecule of isopentenyl diphosphate to form the 10-carbon intermediate geranyl diphosphate. A further condensation with isopentenyl diphosphate forms farnesyl diphosphate. Two molecules of farnesyl diphosphate condense at the diphosphate end to form squalene. Farnesyl diphosphate is an intermediate in the formation of other important compounds that contain isoprenoid units such as dolichol and ubiquinone.

Squalene can fold into a structure that closely resembles the steroid nucleus. Before ring closure occurs, squalene is converted to squalene 2,3-epoxide by squalene epoxidase. Squalene epoxidase is a mixed-function oxidase in the endoplasmic reticulum. Cyclization is catalyzed by oxidosqualene-lanosterol cyclase.

## Farnesyl Diphosphate Gives Rise to Dolichol & Ubiquinone

Dolichol and ubiquinone are polyisoprenoids formed from farnesyl diphosphate by the addition of isopentenyl diphosphate residues.

Dolichol is formed by the addition of up to 16 isopentenyl diphosphate residues.

Ubiquinone is formed by the addition of 3 to 7 isopentenyl diphosphate residues.

GTP-binding proteins in the cell membrane are prenylated with farnesyl or geranylgeranyl residues.

Farnesyl residues are 15 carbon long.

Geranylgeranyl residues are 20 carbon long.

Protein prenylation facilitates the anchoring of proteins into lipid membranes.

Protein prenylation may be involved in protein-protein interactions.

Protein prenylation may be involved in membrane-associated protein trafficking.

# HMG-CoA ACTIVITY IS TIGHTLY CONTROLLED

Cholesterol synthesis is tightly controlled by regulation at the HMG-CoA reductase step. The activity of the enzyme is inhibited by mevalonate, the immediate product of the reaction. The activity of the enzyme is inhibited by cholesterol, the main product of the pathway. Increased intake of cholesterol from the diet leads to a decrease in de novo synthesis, especially in the liver. Cholesterol and metabolites repress transcription of HMG-CoA reductase mRNA via inhibition of a sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) transcription factor. SREBPs are a family of proteins that regulate gene transcription. SREBP activation is inhibited by insulin-induced gene (Insig), which is present in the endoplasmic reticulum. Insig promotes degradation of HMG-CoA reductase. Short-term changes in enzyme activity are brought about by posttranslational modification (Figure 26-4).

## The LDL Receptor Plays an Important Role in the Maintenance of Intracellular Cholesterol Balance

LDL receptors are located on the cell surface in pits coated with clathrin on the cytosolic side of the cell membrane. The LDL receptor is a glycoprotein that spans the membrane, with the B-100 binding region at the exposed amino terminal end. LDL binds to the receptor and is taken up by the cell through endocytosis. After binding, LDL is hydrolyzed in lysosomes, where the apoprotein and cholesteryl ester are broken down, and cholesterol is translocated into the cell. The receptors are recycled back to the cell surface after the process.

The influx of cholesterol into the cell inhibits the transcription of genes encoding enzymes such as HMG-CoA synthase and HMG-CoA reductase, which are involved in cholesterol synthesis. The inhibition also affects the transcription of the LDL receptor itself, via the SREBP pathway, suppressing both cholesterol synthesis and uptake. ACAT activity is stimulated, promoting cholesterol esterification.

Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) regulates the recycling of the LDL receptor to the cell surface by targeting it for degradation. This regulation helps maintain the cholesterol requirement for membranes, steroid hormones, or bile acid synthesis. The free cholesterol content within the cell is kept within narrow limits by these mechanisms.

# CHOLESTEROL IS TRANSPORTED BETWEEN TISSUES IN PLASMA LIPOPROTEINS

Cholesterol is transported in plasma in lipoproteins. The greater part of cholesterol in plasma is in the form of cholesteryl ester. In humans, the highest proportion of cholesterol is found in LDL.

Dietary cholesterol equilibrates with plasma cholesterol in days and with tissue cholesterol in weeks. Cholesteryl ester in the diet is hydrolyzed to cholesterol. Hydrolyzed cholesterol is absorbed by the intestine together with dietary unesterified cholesterol and other lipids.

Cholesterol synthesized in the intestines is incorporated into chylomicrons. Of the cholesterol absorbed, 80 to 90% is esterified with long-chain fatty acids in the intestinal mucosa. Ninety-five percent of the chylomicron cholesterol is delivered to the liver in chylomicron remnants.

Most of the cholesterol secreted by the liver in very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) is retained during the formation of intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL) and ultimately LDL. LDL is taken up by the LDL receptor in liver and extrahepatic tissues.

## Cholesteryl Ester Transfer Protein Facilitates Transfer of Cholesteryl Ester From HDL to Other Lipoproteins

Cholesteryl ester transfer protein is associated with HDL in the plasma of humans and many other species.

Cholesteryl ester transfer protein facilitates the transfer of cholesteryl ester from HDL to VLDL, IDL, and LDL in exchange for triacylglycerol.

In humans, much of the cholesteryl ester carried in lipoproteins is delivered to the liver via VLDL remnants (IDL) or LDL.

# CHOLESTEROL IS EXCRETED FROM THE BODY IN THE BILE AS CHOLESTEROL OR AS BILE ACIDS

Cholesterol is excreted from the body via the bile either in the unesterified form or after conversion into bile acids in the liver.

Coprostanol is the principal sterol in the feces.

Coprostanol is formed from cholesterol by the bacteria in the lower intestine.

## Bile Acids Are Formed From Cholesterol

The primary bile acids are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol. The primary bile acids are cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid. Cholic acid is found in the largest amount in most mammals. The first and principal regulatory step in the biosynthesis of bile acids is the 7α-hydroxylation of cholesterol. The 7α-hydroxylation of cholesterol is catalyzed by cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase. Cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase is a microsomal cytochrome P450 enzyme designated CYP7A1. CYP7A1 requires oxygen, NADPH, and cytochrome P450. Subsequent hydroxylation steps in bile acid biosynthesis are also catalyzed by monooxygenases. The pathway of bile acid biosynthesis divides early into one subpathway leading to cholyl-CoA, characterized by an extra α-OH group on position 12, and another pathway leading to chenodeoxycholyl-CoA. A secondary pathway in mitochondria involves the 27-hydroxylation of cholesterol as the first step. The secondary pathway involves hydroxylation of cholesterol by sterol 27-hydroxylase (Figure 26-7).

## Most Bile Acids Return to the Liver in the Enterohepatic Circulation

Primary and secondary bile acids are absorbed almost exclusively in the ileum.

98 to 99% of bile acids are returned to the liver via the portal circulation.

The process of 98 to 99% of bile acids being returned to the liver via the portal circulation is known as the enterohepatic circulation.

Lithocholic acid is not reabsorbed to any significant extent due to its insolubility.

A small fraction of bile acids is eliminated in the feces.

Fecal elimination represents a major pathway for the elimination of cholesterol.

The pool of bile acids, about 3-5 grams, is cycled through the intestine 6 to 10 times each day.

An amount of bile acid equivalent to that lost in the feces is synthesized from cholesterol daily.

A system of feedback controls maintains a constant size of the bile acid pool.

## Bile Acid Synthesis Is Regulated at the CYP7A1 Step

The principal rate-limiting step in the biosynthesis of bile acids is at the CYP7A1 reaction. The activity of the enzyme CYP7A1 is feedback regulated via the nuclear bile acid-binding receptor, farnesoid X receptor (FXR). When the size of the bile acid pool in the enterohepatic circulation increases, FXR is activated. Activation of FXR suppresses the transcription of the CYP7A1 gene. Chenodeoxycholic acid is particularly important in activating FXR. CYP7A1 activity is enhanced by cholesterol of endogenous origin. CYP7A1 activity is also enhanced by cholesterol of dietary origin. CYP7A1 activity is regulated by insulin. CYP7A1 activity is regulated by glucagon. CYP7A1 activity is regulated by glucocorticoids. CYP7A1 activity is regulated by thyroid hormone.

## Serum Cholesterol Is Correlated With the Incidence of Atherosclerosis & Coronary Heart Disease

Atherosclerosis is an inflammatory disease characterized by the deposition of cholesterol and cholesteryl ester from the plasma lipoproteins into the artery wall. Atherosclerosis is a major cause of heart disease. Elevated plasma cholesterol levels greater than 5mmol/L are one of the most important factors in promoting atherosclerosis. Elevated blood triacylglycerol is an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis. Diseases with prolonged elevation of VLDL, IDL, chylomicron remnants, or LDL in the blood often accompany premature or more severe atherosclerosis. Diabetes mellitus is a condition that can lead to elevated levels of VLDL, IDL, chylomicron remnants, or LDL. Lipid nephrosis is a condition that can lead to elevated levels of VLDL, IDL, chylomicron remnants, or LDL. Hypothyroidism is a condition that can lead to elevated levels of VLDL, IDL, chylomicron remnants, or LDL. Other conditions of hyperlipidemia can lead to elevated levels of VLDL, IDL, chylomicron remnants, or LDL. There is an inverse relationship between HDL concentrations and coronary heart disease. The LDL:HDL cholesterol ratio is a good predictive parameter for coronary heart disease. HDL functions in reverse cholesterol transport. Susceptibility to atherosclerosis varies widely among species. Humans are one of the few species in which atherosclerosis can be induced by diets high in cholesterol.

## Diet and Lifestyle Can Play an Important Role in Reducing Serum Cholesterol

Hereditary factors play the most important role in determining plasma cholesterol concentrations. Dietary, environmental, and lifestyle factors also influence plasma cholesterol concentrations. Substituting polyunsaturated and monounsaturated fatty acids for saturated fatty acids in the diet is beneficial. Plant oils such as corn oil and sunflower seed oil contain a high proportion of ω6 polyunsaturated fatty acids. Olive oil contains a high concentration of monounsaturated fatty acids. ω3 fatty acids found in fish oils are also beneficial. Butter fat, beef fat, and palm oil contain a high proportion of saturated fatty acids. Sucrose and fructose have a greater effect in raising blood lipids, particularly triacylglycerols, compared to other carbohydrates.

Unsaturated fatty acids lower blood cholesterol levels by upregulating LDL receptors on the cell surface. Unsaturated fatty acids increase the catabolic rate of LDL, the main atherogenic lipoprotein. ω3 fatty acids have anti-inflammatory and triacylglycerol-lowering effects. Saturated fatty acids cause the formation of smaller VLDL particles that contain relatively more cholesterol. Smaller VLDL particles are utilized by extrahepatic tissues at a slower rate than larger particles, which is considered atherogenic.

High blood pressure is associated with raised plasma cholesterol levels. Smoking is associated with raised plasma cholesterol levels. Male gender is associated with raised plasma cholesterol levels. Obesity, particularly abdominal obesity, is associated with raised plasma cholesterol levels. Lack of exercise is associated with raised plasma cholesterol levels. Drinking soft water instead of hard water is associated with raised plasma cholesterol levels. Elevation of plasma-free fatty acids (FFAs) followed by increased output of triacylglycerol and cholesterol into the circulation in VLDL may be related to emotional stress. Elevation of plasma-free fatty acids (FFAs) followed by increased output of triacylglycerol and cholesterol into the circulation in VLDL may be related to coffee drinking. Premenopausal women appear to be protected against many of these deleterious factors. Estrogen is thought to contribute to this protection in premenopausal women. Moderate alcohol consumption is associated with a lower incidence of coronary heart disease. Moderate alcohol consumption may be due to elevation of HDL concentrations. Regular exercise lowers plasma LDL. Regular exercise raises HDL. Regular exercise reduces triacylglycerol concentrations. Increased insulin sensitivity enhances the expression of lipoprotein lipase, which likely reduces triacylglycerol concentrations.

## Hypolipidemic Drugs Can Reduce Serum Cholesterol & Triacylglycerol

Statins are a family of drugs that have proved highly efficacious in lowering plasma cholesterol and preventing heart disease. Statins act by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase. Statins upregulate LDL receptor activity. Atorvastatin is an example of a statin currently in use. Simvastatin is an example of a statin currently in use. Fluvastatin is an example of a statin currently in use. Pravastatin is an example of a statin currently in use.

Ezetimibe reduces blood cholesterol levels. Ezetimibe inhibits the absorption of cholesterol by the intestine. Ezetimibe blocks uptake by the Niemann-Pick C-like 1 protein.

Fibrates are drugs used to lower plasma triacylglycerols. Clofibrate is an example of a fibrate. Gemfibrozil is an example of a fibrate. Fibrates decrease the secretion of triacylglycerol and cholesterol-containing VLDL by the liver.

PCSK9 reduces the number of LDL receptors exposed on the cell membrane. PCSK9 raises blood cholesterol levels. Drugs that inhibit PCSK9 activity are potentially antiatherogenic. Alirocumab is a drug approved for inhibiting PCSK9. Evolocumab is a drug approved for inhibiting PCSK9.
