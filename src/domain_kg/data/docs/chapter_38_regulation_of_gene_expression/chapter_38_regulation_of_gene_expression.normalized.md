# REGULATED EXPRESSION OF GENES IS REQUIRED FOR DEVELOPMENT, DIFFERENTIATION, & ADAPTATION

The genetic information in each normal somatic cell of a metazoan organism is practically identical. Specialized cellular functions in a few cells involve amplified or rearranged genes. In disease states, chromosome integrity can be altered, such as in cancer and other diseases. Sometimes, chromosome integrity is altered at the whole chromosome level, like trisomy 21 causing Down syndrome. The expression of genetic information must be regulated during the ontogeny and differentiation of the organism and its cellular components. The organism must adapt to its environment and conserve energy and nutrients by responding to extrinsic signals only when necessary. As organisms have evolved, more sophisticated regulatory mechanisms have appeared, providing the organism and its cells with the responsiveness necessary for survival in a complex environment. Mammalian cells possess about 1000 times more genetic information than the bacterium Escherichia coli. Much of this additional genetic information is likely involved in the regulation of gene expression during the differentiation of tissues and biologic processes in the multicellular organism. This genetic information also ensures that the organism can respond to complex environmental challenges.

There are two types of gene regulation: positive regulation and negative regulation. Positive regulation occurs when the expression of genetic information is quantitatively increased by the presence of a specific regulatory element. Negative regulation occurs when the expression of genetic information is diminished by the presence of a specific regulatory element. The element or molecule mediating negative regulation is called a negative regulator. The element or molecule mediating negative regulation is called a silencer. The element or molecule mediating negative regulation is called a repressor. The element mediating positive regulation is called a positive regulator. The element mediating positive regulation is called an enhancer. The element mediating positive regulation is called an activator. A double negative can act as a positive. An effector that inhibits the function of a negative regulator will appear to bring about positive regulation. Many regulated systems that appear to be induced are in fact derepressed at the molecular level. (See Chapter 9 for additional discussion of these terms.)

Effects of Positive & Negative Regulation on Gene Expression

In the presence of a regulator, negative regulation decreases the rate of gene expression.
In the presence of a regulator, positive regulation increases the rate of gene expression.
In the absence of a regulator, negative regulation increases the rate of gene expression.
In the absence of a regulator, positive regulation decreases the rate of gene expression.

# BIOLOGIC SYSTEMS EXHIBIT THREE TYPES OF TEMPORAL RESPONSES TO A REGULATORYSIGNAL

A type A response is characterized by an increased extent of gene expression that is dependent on the continued presence of the inducing signal. When the inducing signal is removed, the amount of gene expression diminishes to its basal level. The amount of gene expression repeatedly increases in response to the reappearance of the specific signal. A type A response is commonly observed in prokaryotes in response to sudden changes of the intracellular concentration of a nutrient. A type A response is also observed in many higher organisms after exposure to inducers such as hormones, nutrients, or growth factors (see Chapter 42).

A type B response exhibits an increased amount of gene expression that is transient even in the continued presence of the regulatory signal. After the regulatory signal has terminated and the cell has been allowed to recover, a second transient response to a subsequent regulatory signal may be observed. Response-desensitization recovery characterizes the action of many pharmacologic agents. Response-desensitization recovery is also a feature of many naturally occurring processes. A type B response commonly occurs during development of an organism, when only the transient appearance of a specific gene product is required, although the signal persists.

The type C response pattern exhibits an increased extent of gene expression in response to the regulatory signal. The increased gene expression persists indefinitely even after termination of the signal. The signal acts as a trigger in the type C response pattern. Once expression of the gene is initiated in the cell, it cannot be terminated even in the daughter cells. The type C response pattern is an irreversible and inherited alteration. The type C response pattern typically occurs during the development of differentiated function in a tissue or organ.

## Simple Unicellular & Multicellular Organisms Serve as Valuable Models for the Study of Gene Expression in Human Cells

Prokaryotic cells helped establish the principle that information flows from the gene to a messenger RNA to a specific protein molecule. Advanced genetic analyses could be performed in prokaryotes. Simpler model eukaryotic organisms such as baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), the nematode (Caenorhabditis elegans), and the fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) aided these studies. The principles established in these studies have led to remarkable progress in the analysis of gene regulation in higher eukaryotic organisms, including humans. Advancements in physical optical, biochemical, informatic, and molecular biological techniques have contributed to this progress. The initial discussion in this chapter will center on prokaryotic systems. The physiology of gene expression will be discussed in this chapter. Nearly all conclusions about this physiology have been derived from genetic studies. Conclusions about the physiology of gene expression have been confirmed by molecular biological and biochemical experiments.

## Some Features of Prokaryotic Gene Expression Are Unique

In prokaryotes, genes involved in a single metabolic pathway are often present in a linear array called an operon. An operon can be regulated by a single promoter or regulatory region. The cistron is the smallest unit of genetic expression. A single mRNA that encodes more than one separately translated protein is referred to as a polycistronic mRNA. The polycistronic lac operon mRNA is translated into three separate proteins. Operons are common in bacteria but not in eukaryotes. Polycistronic mRNAs are common in bacteria but not in eukaryotes.

An inducible gene is one whose expression increases in response to an inducer or activator. Inducible genes have relatively low basal rates of transcription. Genes with high basal rates of transcription are often subject to downregulation by repressors.

The expression of some genes is constitutive, meaning that they are expressed at a reasonably constant rate. Constitutive genes are often referred to as housekeeping genes. As a result of mutation, some inducible gene products become constitutively expressed. A mutation resulting in constitutive expression of what was formerly a regulated gene is called a constitutive mutation.

## Analysis of Lactose Metabolism in E. coli Led to the Discovery of the Basic Principles of Gene Transcription Activation & Repression

Jacob and Monod described their operon model in a classic paper published in 1961. The operon model was largely based on observations of the regulation of lactose metabolism by the intestinal bacterium E. coli. The molecular mechanisms responsible for the regulation of the genes involved in lactose metabolism are among the best-understood in any organism.

β-Galactosidase hydrolyzes the β-galactoside lactose to galactose and glucose. The gene encoding β-galactosidase is called lacZ. The lacZ gene is clustered with the genes encoding lactose permease and thiogalactoside transacetylase. Lactose permease is encoded by lacY. Thiogalactoside transacetylase is encoded by lacA. The genes lacZ, lacY, and lacA are physically linked and constitute the lac operon. The lac operon includes the lac promoter and lac operator, which is a regulatory region. The lac operon also includes the lacI gene, which encodes the LacI repressor. The genetic arrangement of the lac operon allows for coordinate expression of the three enzymes concerned with lactose metabolism.

Each of the linked operon genes is transcribed into one large polycistronic mRNA molecule. Each of the linked operon genes contains multiple independent translation start (AUG) and stop (UAA) codons for each of the three cistrons. Each protein is translated separately and they are not processed from a single large precursor protein. The transcription start site for lac operon transcription is shown in Figure 38-2. The binding site for the LacI protein, the lac operator, overlaps the lac promoter. Immediately upstream of the lac operon promoter is the binding site for the cAMP-binding protein, CAP, which is the positive regulator of lac operon transcription. See Figure 38–3for more details.

## A diagram shows the structure of the lac operon.

A gene includes regulatory sequences as well as the region that encodes the primary transcript. A gene is generally italicized in lower case, and the encoded protein, when abbreviated, is expressed in roman type with the first letter capitalized. The gene lacI encodes the repressor protein LacI. When E. coli is presented with lactose or specific lactose analogs under appropriate nonrepressing conditions, the expression of β-galactosidase, galactoside permease, and thiogalactoside transacetylase increases 100 to 1000 times. This is a type A response, as depicted in Figure 38–1. The kinetics of induction can be quite rapid; lac-specific mRNAs are fully induced within ~5 minutes after addition of lactose to a culture, and β-galactosidase protein is maximal within 10 minutes. Under fully induced conditions, there can be up to 5000 β-galactosidase molecules per cell, an amount about 1000 times greater than the basal, uninduced level. Upon removal of the inducer, the synthesis of these three enzymes declines. When E. coli is exposed to both lactose and glucose, the cells first metabolize glucose before temporarily stopping growth until the lac operon becomes induced to metabolize lactose. Although lactose is present from the beginning of the bacterial growth phase, the cell does not induce the enzymes necessary for catabolism of lactose until glucose has been exhausted. This phenomenon was first thought to be attributable to repression of the lac operon by some catabolite of glucose, termed catabolite repression. Catabolite repression is in fact mediated by a catabolite activator protein (CAP) in conjunction with 3',5' cyclic Adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). The expression of many inducible enzyme systems or operons in E. coli and other prokaryotes is sensitive to catabolite repression. The physiology of induction of the lac operon is well understood at the molecular level. Expression of the normal lacI gene of the lac operon is constitutive, resulting in formation of the subunits of the lac repressor. Four identical subunits with molecular weights of 38,000 assemble into a tetrameric Lac repressor molecule. The LacI repressor protein molecule has a very high affinity for the operator locus, with a dissociation constant of about 10^-13 mol/L. The operator locus is a region of double-stranded DNA that exhibits a twofold rotational symmetry and an inverted palindrome in a region that is 21-bp long. At any one time, only two of the four subunits of the repressor tetramer appear to bind to the operator. Within the 21-base-pair operator region, nearly every base of each base pair is involved in LacI recognition and binding. Binding occurs mostly in the major groove without interrupting the basepaired, double-helical nature of the operator DNA. The operator locus is between the promoter and the transcription initiation site of the lacZ gene. When bound to the operator locus, the LacI repressor molecule prevents transcription of the distal structural genes, lacZ, lacY, and lacA by interfering with the binding of RNA polymerase to the promoter. RNA polymerase and LacI repressor cannot be effectively bound to the lac operon at the same time. The LacI repressor molecule is a negative regulator, and in its presence, expression from the lacZ, lacY, and lacA genes is very, very low. There are normally about 30 repressor tetramer molecules in the cell, a concentration sufficient to effect more than 95% occupancy of the one lac operator element in a bacterium, thus ensuring low basal lac operon gene transcription in the absence of inducing signals. A lactose analog that can induce the lac operon while not itself serving as a substrate for β-galactosidase is an example of a gratuitous inducer. An example is isopropylthiogalactoside (IPTG). The addition of lactose or a gratuitous inducer such as IPTG to bacteria growing on a poorly utilized carbon source results in prompt induction of the lac operon enzymes. Small amounts of the gratuitous inducer or lactose are able to enter the cell even in the absence of permease. The LacI repressor molecules have a high affinity for the inducer. Binding of the inducer to the repressor molecule induces a conformational change in the structure of the repressor that causes a decrease in operator DNA occupancy because its affinity for the operator is now 10^4 times lower, with a dissociation constant of about 10^-9 mol/L. DNA-dependent RNA polymerase can now more efficiently compete with LacI and bind to the promoter, and transcription will begin. An inducer derepresses the lac operon and allows transcription of the genes encoding β-galactosidase, galactoside permease, and thiogalactoside transacetylase. Translation of the polycistronic mRNA can occur even before transcription is completed. Derepression of the lac operon allows the cell to synthesize the enzymes necessary to catabolize lactose as an energy source. IPTG-induced expression of transfected plasmids bearing the lac operator-promoter ligated to appropriate bioengineered constructs is commonly used to express mammalian recombinant proteins in E. coli. In order for RNA polymerase to form a PIC at the promoter site most efficiently, the cAMP-CAP complex must also be present in the cell. The bacterium accumulates cAMP only when it is starved for a source of carbon. In the presence of glucose or glycerol in concentrations sufficient for growth, the bacteria will lack sufficient cAMP to bind to CAP because glucose inhibits adenylyl cyclase, the enzyme that converts ATP to cAMP. In the presence of glucose or glycerol, cAMP-saturated CAP is lacking, so that the DNA-dependent RNA polymerase cannot initiate transcription of the lac operon at the maximal rate. In the presence of the CAP-cAMP complex, which binds to CAP Response Element (CRE) DNA just upstream of the promoter site, transcription occurs at maximal levels. Studies indicate that a region of CAP directly contacts the RNA polymerase α subunit, and these protein-protein interactions facilitate the binding of RNAP to the promoter. The CAP-cAMP regulator acts as a positive regulator because its presence is required for optimal gene expression. The lac operon is therefore controlled by two distinct, ligand-modulated DNA-binding trans-factors; one that acts positively (cAMP-CRP complex) to facilitate productive binding of RNA polymerase to the promoter and one that acts negatively (LacI repressor) that antagonizes RNA polymerase promoter binding. Maximal activity of the lac operon occurs when glucose levels are low and lactose is present. When the lacI gene has been mutated so that its product, LacI, is not capable of binding to operator DNA, the organism will exhibit constitutive expression of the lac operon. An organism with a lacI gene mutation that produces a LacI protein which prevents the binding of lactose to the repressor will remain repressed, even in the presence of the inducer molecule. Bacteria harboring mutations in their lac operator locus such that the operator sequence will not bind the normal repressor will constitutively express the lac operon genes. Mechanisms of positive and negative regulation comparable to those described for the lac system have been observed in eukaryotic cells. The genetic switch of bacteriophage lambda (λ) provides another paradigm for understanding the role of conditional regulatory protein-DNA interactions in transcriptional control in eukaryotic cells. Like some eukaryotic viruses, certain bacterial viruses can either reside in a dormant state within the host chromosomes or can replicate within the bacterium and eventually lead to lysis and killing of the bacterial host. Some E. coli harbor such a “temperate” virus, bacteriophage lambda (λ). When lambda infects an organism of that species, it injects its 48,490-bp, double-stranded, linear DNA genome into the cell. Depending on the nutritional state of the cell, the lambda DNA will either integrate into the host genome and remain dormant until activated, or it will commence replicating until it has made about 100 copies of complete, protein-packaged virus, at which point it causes lysis of its host. Poor growth conditions favor lysogeny while good growth conditions promote the lytic pathway of lambda growth. When integrated into the host genome in its dormant state, lambda will remain in that state until activated by exposure of its bacterial host to DNA-damaging agents. In response to such a noxious stimulus, the dormant bacteriophage becomes 'induced' and begins to transcribe and subsequently translate those genes of its own genome that are necessary for its excision from the host chromosome, its DNA replication, and the synthesis of its protein coat and lysis enzymes. This event acts like a trigger or type C response; once dormant lambda has committed itself to induction, there is no turning back until the cell is lysed and the replicated bacteriophage released. The lytic/lysogenic genetic switching event in lambda is centered around an 80-bp region in its double-stranded DNA genome referred to as the 'right operator' (OR). The right operator is flanked on its left side by the gene for the lambda repressor protein, cI, and on its right side by the gene encoding another regulatory protein, the cro gene. When lambda is in its prophage state, integrated into the host genome, the cI repressor gene is the only lambda gene that is expressed. When the bacteriophage is undergoing lytic growth, the cI repressor gene is not expressed, but the cro gene—as well as many other lambda genes—is expressed. Thus, when the cI repressor gene is on, the cro gene is off, and when the cro gene is on the cI repressor gene is off. These two genes regulate each other's expression and thus, ultimately, the decision between lytic and lysogenic growth of lambda. The 80-bp lambda right operator, OR, can be subdivided into three discrete, evenly spaced, 17-bp cis-active DNA elements that represent the binding sites for either of two bacteriophage lambda regulatory proteins. The nucleotide sequences of these three tandemly arranged sites are similar but not identical. The three related cis-elements, termed operators OR1, OR2, and OR3, can be bound by either cI or cro proteins. However, the relative affinities of cI and cro for each of the sites vary, and this differential binding affinity is central to the appropriate operation of the lambda phage lytic or lysogenic 'molecular switch.' The DNA region between the cro and repressor genes also contains two promoter sequences that direct the binding of RNA polymerase in a specified orientation, where it commences transcribing adjacent genes. One promoter directs RNA polymerase to transcribe in the rightward direction and, thus, to transcribe cro and other distal genes, while the other promoter directs the transcription of the cI repressor gene in the leftward direction. The product of the cI repressor gene, the 236-amino-acid λ cI repressor protein, is a two-domain molecule with an amino terminal DNA-binding domain and a carboxyl-terminal dimerization domain. Association of one repressor protein with another forms a dimer. cI repressor dimers bind to operator DNA much more tightly than do monomers. The product of the cro gene, the 66-amino-acid, 9-kDa cro protein, has a single domain but also binds the operator DNA more tightly as a dimer. The cro protein's single domain mediates both operator binding and dimerization. In a lysogenic bacterium, the lambda repressor dimer binds preferentially to OR1 but in so doing, by a cooperative interaction, enhances the binding of another repressor dimer to OR2. The affinity of repressor for OR3 is the least of the three operator subregions. The binding of repressor to OR1 has two major effects. First, occupancy of OR1 by repressor blocks the binding of RNA polymerase to the rightward promoter and in that way prevents expression of cro. Second, repressor dimer bound to OR1 enhances the binding of RNA polymerase to the leftward promoter that overlaps OR3 and thereby enhances transcription and subsequent expression of the repressor gene. This enhancement of transcription is mediated through direct protein-protein interactions between promoter-bound RNA polymerase and OR2-bound repressor. Thus, the λ cI protein is both a negative regulator, by preventing transcription of cro, and a positive regulator, by enhancing transcription of its own gene, cI. This dual effect of the repressor is responsible for the stable state of the dormant lambda bacteriophage. If intracellular repressor protein concentration becomes very high, the excess repressor will bind to OR3 and by so doing diminish transcription of the repressor gene from the leftward promoter by blocking RNAP binding to the cI promoter until the repressor concentration drops and repressor dissociates from OR3. Similar examples of repressor proteins also having the ability to activate transcription have been observed in eukaryotes. The mechanism of repression, derepression, and activation of the lac operon is shown in Figure 38-3. When no inducer is present, the constitutively synthesized lacI gene products form a repressor tetramer that binds to the operator. Repressor-operator binding prevents the binding of RNA polymerase and consequently prevents transcription of the lacZ, lacY, and lacA genes into a polycistronic mRNA. When inducer is present, but glucose is also present in the culture medium, the tetrameric repressor molecules are conformationally altered by inducer, and cannot efficiently bind to the operator locus. However, RNA polymerase will not efficiently bind the promoter and initiate transcription because positive protein-protein interactions between CRE-bound CAP protein and RNA polymerase fail to occur; thus, the lac operon is not efficiently transcribed. When inducer is present, and glucose is depleted from the medium, adenylyl cyclase is activated and cAMP is produced. This cAMP binds with high affinity to its binding protein, the cyclic AMP activator protein, or CAP. The CAP-cAMP complex binds to its recognition sequence (CRE) at lac operon nucleotide coordinate -50. Direct protein-protein contacts between the CRE-bound CAP and the RNA polymerase increases promoter binding more than 20-fold; hence RNAP will efficiently transcribe the lac operon and the polycistronic lacZ-lacY-lacA mRNA molecule formed can be translated into the corresponding protein molecules β-galactosidase, permease, and transacetylase. This protein production enables cellular catabolism of lactose as the sole carbon source for growth. The alternate lytic and lysogenic lifestyles of bacteriophage lambda are shown in Figure 38-4. Infection of the bacterium E. coli by phage lambda begins when a virus particle attaches itself to specific receptors (maltose transporters) on the bacterial cell surface and injects its DNA into the cell, where the phage genome then circularizes. Infection can take either of two courses depending on which two sets of viral genes is turned on. In the lysogenic pathway, the viral DNA becomes integrated into the bacterial chromosome, where it is replicated passively as part of the bacterial DNA during E. coli cell division. This dormant, bacterial genome-integrated virus is called a prophage, and the cell that harbors it is called a lysogen. In the alternative, lytic mode of infection, the viral DNA excises from the E. coli chromosome and replicates itself in order to direct the synthesis of viral proteins. About 100 new virus particles are formed. The proliferating viruses induce lysis of the cell. A prophage can be 'induced' by a DNA damaging agent such as ultraviolet radiation. The inducing agent throws a switch, so that a different set of viral genes is turned on. Viral DNA loops out and is excised from the E. coli chromosome and replicates; the virus then proceeds along the lytic pathway. The operator is a region of the viral DNA some 80-bp long. To its left lies the gene encoding lambda repressor (cI), to its right the gene (cro) encoding the regulator protein Cro. When the operator region is enlarged, it is seen to include three subregions termed operators: OR1, OR2, and OR3, each 17-bp long. These three DNA elements are recognition sites to which both λ cI repressor and Cro proteins can bind. The recognition sites overlap two divergent promoters—sequences of bases to which RNA polymerase binds in order to transcribe these genes into mRNA. Site OR1 is enlarged to show its base sequence. The schematic molecular structures of lambda regulatory proteins cI and Cro are shown in Figure 38-6. The lambda repressor protein is a 236-amino-acid polypeptide. The chain folds itself into a dumbbell shape with two substructures: an amino terminal (NH₂) domain and a carboxyl-terminal (COOH) domain. The two domains are linked by a region of the chain that is less structured and susceptible to cleavage by proteases. Single repressor molecules (monomers) tend to reversibly associate to form dimers. A dimer is held together mainly by contact between the carboxyl-terminal domains. cI repressor dimers bind to the recognition sites in the operator region; they display differential affinities for the three operator sites, OR1 > OR2 > OR3. The DNA-binding domains (DBD) of the repressor molecule make contact with DNA. Cro is a single globular protein that contains both a DNA binding domain and a cro-cro dimerization domain, which promotes binding of cro-cro dimers to target operator DNA. It is important that cro exhibits the highest affinity for OR3, opposite the sequence binding preference of the cI protein. The configuration of the lytic/lysogenic switch is shown at four stages of the lambda phage 'life' cycle in Figure 38-7. The lysogenic pathway is selected when a repressor dimer binds to OR1, thereby making it likely that OR2 will be bound immediately by another dimer due to the cooperative nature of cI-OR DNA binding. In the prophage, the repressor dimers bound at OR1 and OR2 prevent RNA polymerase from binding to the rightward cro promoter and so block the synthesis of cro (negative control). Simultaneously, these DNA-bound cI proteins enhance the binding of polymerase to the leftward promoter (positive control), with the result that the repressor gene is transcribed into RNA and more repressor is synthesized, maintaining the lysogenic state. The prophage is induced when ultraviolet radiation activates the protease recA, which cleaves cI repressor monomers. The equilibrium of free monomers, free cI dimers, and bound dimers is thereby shifted by mass action, and cI dimers thus dissociate from the operator sites. RNA polymerase is no longer stimulated to bind to the leftward promoter, so that repressor is no longer synthesized. As induction proceeds, all the operator sites become vacant, thus polymerase can bind to the rightward promoter and cro is synthesized. During early lytic growth, a single cro dimer binds to OR3, the site for which it has the highest affinity, thereby occluding the cI promoter. Consequently, RNA polymerase cannot bind to the leftward promoter, but the rightward promoter remains accessible. Polymerase continues to bind there, transcribing cro and other early lytic genes. Lytic growth ensues.

## The Chromatin Template Contributes Importantly to Eukaryotic Gene Transcription Control

Chromatin structure provides an additional level of control of gene transcription. Large regions of chromatin are transcriptionally inactive while others are either active or potentially active. Each cell contains the same complement of genes, and the development of specialized organs, tissues, and cells depends on the differential expression of genes. Differential expression is achieved by having different regions of chromatin available for transcription in cells from various tissues. The DNA containing the β-globin gene cluster is in “active” chromatin in the reticulocyte but in 'inactive' chromatin in muscle cells. The presence of nucleosomes and complexes of histones and DNA provides a barrier against the ready association of most transcription factors with specific DNA regions. The dynamics of the formation, disruption, and physical state of nucleosome structure are an important part of eukaryotic gene regulation. Histone covalent modification, known as the histone code, is a key determinant of gene activity. Histones are subjected to a wide range of specific posttranslational modifications. These modifications are dynamic and reversible. Histone acetylation and deacetylation are the best understood. Histone acetylation occurs on lysine residues in the amino terminal tails of histone molecules and has been correlated with either active transcription or transcriptional potential. Histone acetylation reduces the positive charge of these tails and likely decreases the binding affinity of histones for the negatively charged DNA. Covalent modification of histones creates new binding sites for additional proteins such as ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes. These complexes increase accessibility of adjacent DNA sequences by removing or altering nucleosomal histones. Coregulators, including chromatin modifiers and chromatin remodelers, can 'open up' gene promoters and regulatory regions, facilitating binding of transcriptional activator proteins, RNA polymerase II, and the GTFs. Histone deacetylation, catalyzed by transcriptional corepressors, has the opposite effect. Different proteins with specific acetylase and deacetylase activities are associated with various components of the transcription apparatus. Proteins that catalyze histone PTMs are sometimes referred to as 'code writers', while proteins that recognize and bind these PTMs are termed 'code readers', and enzymes that remove PTMs are called 'code erasers'. Histone PTMs represent a dynamic, potentially information-rich source of regulatory information. The exact rules and mechanisms defining the specificity of these processes are under investigation. Methylation of deoxycytidine residues, 5meC, in the sequence 5'-meCpG-3' in DNA has important effects on chromatin, often leading to a decrease in gene transcription. In mouse liver, only the unmethylated ribosomal RNA encoding genes can be expressed. Many animal viruses are not transcribed when their DNA is methylated. Acute demethylation of 5meC residues in specific regions of steroid hormone inducible genes has been associated with an increased rate of transcription. However, it is not possible to generalize that methylated DNA is transcriptionally inactive, that all inactive chromatin is methylated, or that active DNA is not methylated. The binding of specific transcription factors to cognate DNA elements may result in disruption of nucleosomal structure. Most eukaryotic genes have multiple protein-binding DNA elements. The serial binding of transcription factors to these elements may either directly disrupt the structure of the nucleosome, prevent its reformation, or recruit multiprotein coregulator complexes that can covalently modify and/or remodel nucleosomes. These reactions result in chromatin-level structural changes that increase or decrease DNA accessibility to other factors and the transcription machinery. Eukaryotic DNA that is in an 'active' region of chromatin can be transcribed. In mammalian cells, a promoter dictates where the RNA polymerase will initiate transcription. Additional complexity is added by elements or factors that enhance or repress transcription, define tissue-specific expression, and modulate the actions of many effector molecules. Gene activation and repression might occur when particular genes move into or out of different subnuclear compartments or locations, affecting biomolecular condensate formation that can stimulate or inhibit transcription. The β-interferon gene enhancer is well understood in mammalian systems. The β-interferon gene enhancer is activated upon viral infection of mammalian cells. The β-interferon gene is induced to help the organism mount an antiviral response. Interferon proteins are secreted by virally infected cells to inhibit viral replication in neighboring cells. The β-interferon gene enhancer is located between nucleotides -110 and -45 relative to the transcription start site (+1). ## The Helix-Turn-Helix Motif

The helix-turn-helix motif was the first described. The lambda cro transcription regulator consists of three antiparallel β sheets and three α helices in each monomer. The rest of the molecule stabilizes these structures. The average diameter of an α helix is 1.2 nm, matching the width of the major groove in the B form of DNA.

The DNA recognition domain of each cro monomer interacts with 5 base pairs. The dimer-binding sites span 3.4 nm, allowing the protein to fit into successive half turns of the major groove on the same DNA surface (Figure 38-14).

## The Zinc Finger Motif

The zinc finger was the second DNA-binding motif whose atomic structure was elucidated. The eukaryotic protein TElIIA, a positive regulator of 5S RNA gene transcription, required zinc for activity. Structural and biophysical analyses revealed that each TEIIIA molecule contains nine zinc ions in a repeating coordination complex formed by closely spaced cysteine-cysteine residues followed 12 to 13 amino acids later by a histidine-histidine pair (Figure 38-15). In some zinc finger proteins, notably the steroid-thyroid nuclear hormone receptor family, the His-His doublet is replaced by a second Cys-Cys pair. The zinc finger motifs of the protein lie on one face of the DNA helix, with successive fingers positioned in one turn in the major groove. Each TElllA zinc finger contacts about 5 bp of DNA.

Zinc fingers are a series of repeated domains (two to nine) in which each is centered on a tetrahedral coordination with zinc. In the case of the DNA-binding transcription factor TFIllA, the coordination is provided by a pair of cysteine residues (C) separated by 12 to 13 amino acids from a pair of histidine (H) residues. In other zinc finger proteins, the second pair also consists of C residues. Zinc fingers bind in the major groove, where adiacent Zn-fingers make contact with 5 bp of DNA along the same face of the helix.

## The Leucine Zipper Motif

A 30-amino-acid sequence in the carboxyl-terminal region of the mammalian enhancer-binding protein C/EBP revealed a novel structure known as the leucine zipper motif. The leucine zipper motif forms an α helix with a periodic repeat of leucine residues at every seventh position. This repeat occurs for eight helical turns and includes four leucine repeats. Similar structures have been identified in other proteins involved in the regulation of transcription in all tested eukaryotes. The leucine zipper structure enables two identical or nonidentical monomers, such as Jun-Jun or Fos-Jun, to form a tight dimeric complex by 'zipping together' in a coiled coil. This protein-protein interaction enhances the association of the separate DNA-binding domains (DBDs) with their target DNA sites.

# THE DNA BINDING & TRANSACTIVATION DOMAINS OF MOST REGULATORY PROTEINS ARE SEPARATE

The DNA binding and transcription activation functions in regulatory proteins are typically served by separate domains. Domain swap experiments in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae provided critical tests for this concept. The GAL1 gene in yeast is part of the galactose metabolism pathway. Transcription of GAL1 is regulated by the Gal4 protein. The Gal4 protein binds to a 17bp enhancer element known as the upstream activator sequence (UAS). The upstream activator sequence (UAS) is located upstream of the GAL1 promoter. The Gal4 protein has an N-terminal DNA binding domain (DBD) consisting of amino acids 1-73. Gal4 activates GAL1 transcription through a C-terminal 34 amino acid activation domain (AD).

To test the contributions of the Gal4 AD and DBD to GAL1 transcription activation, domain swap experiments were conducted. The N-terminal 73-amino-acid DBD of Gal4 was replaced with the DBD of LexA, an E. coli DNA-binding protein. The resulting chimeric molecule (lexA DBD-Gal4 AD) did not bind to the GAL1 UAS. The resulting chimeric molecule (lexA DBD-Gal4 AD) failed to activate GAL1 transcription. When the lexA operator, the DNA sequence normally bound by the LexA DBD, was inserted upstream of the GAL1 promoter to replace the GAL1 enhancer, the hybrid LexA-Gal4 AD fusion protein bound to the substituted lexA operator. When the lexA operator was inserted upstream of the GAL1 promoter, the hybrid LexA-Gal4 AD fusion protein activated GAL1 transcription. This experiment has been replicated with various DBDs and ADs across different cell types and organisms. The replication of this experiment demonstrated that the DBDs and ADs of many transcription factors can function independently (Figure 38-17).

## ncRNAs Modulate Gene Expression

Non-protein-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) contribute importantly to the control of gene expression in eukaryotes. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have a broad range of functions. LncRNAs can serve as scaffolds for the formation of ribonucleoprotein complexes. LncRNAs are involved in the formation of ribosomes. LncRNAs are involved in the formation of P-bodies. LncRNAs are involved in the formation of telomerase. LncRNAs are involved in X-chromosome inactivation through Xist. The molecular mechanisms of lncRNA biology are still not fully understood. Future research will likely reveal more about how lncRNAs modulate human biology.

Small miRNAs and siRNAs are better understood in terms of their mechanism of action. MiRNAs and siRNAs are approximately 22 nucleotides in length. MiRNAs and siRNAs regulate the function and expression of specific mRNAs. MiRNAs and siRNAs can inhibit translation or induce mRNA degradation. In some cases, miRNAs can stimulate mRNA function. MiRNA action can lead to significant changes in protein production and gene expression. Small ncRNAs have been implicated in human diseases such as heart disease, cancer, muscle wasting, viral infection, and diabetes.

MiRNAs are transactive molecules. SiRNAs are transactive molecules. Once synthesized and processed, miRNAs interact with specific proteins and bind to target mRNAs. Once synthesized and processed, siRNAs interact with specific proteins and bind to target mRNAs. The binding of miRNAs to mRNA targets is guided by basepairing rules. If miRNA-mRNA base pairing has mismatches, translation of the target mRNA is inhibited. If miRNA-mRNA base pairing is perfect, the corresponding mRNA is degraded. Many scientists and biotechnology companies are studying miRNA biogenesis, transport, and function to potentially cure human diseases. The importance of miRNAs is growing. The role of miRNAs in gene regulation is being actively investigated.

## Eukaryotic Genes Can Be Amplified or Rearranged During Development or in Response to Drugs

During early development of metazoans, there is an abrupt increase in the need for specific molecules such as ribosomal RNA and messenger RNA molecules for proteins that make up specific cell or tissue types. One way to increase the rate at which such molecules can be formed is to increase the number of genes available for transcription of these specific molecules. Among the repetitive DNA sequences within the genome are hundreds of copies of ribosomal RNA-encoding genes. Ribosomal RNA-encoding genes preexist repetitively in the DNA of the gametes and thus are transmitted in high copy numbers from generation to generation. In some specific organisms such as the fruit fly (Drosophila), there occurs during oogenesis an amplification of a few preexisting genes such as those for the chorion (eggshell) proteins. Subsequently, these amplified genes provide multiple sites for gene transcription. Amplified genes are presumably generated by a process of repeated initiations during DNA synthesis. ## Alternative RNA Processing Is Another Control Mechanism

Eukaryotic cells use alternative RNA processing to control gene expression. Alternative RNA processing can involve alternative promoters, intron-exon splice sites, or polyadenylation sites. Different tissues often process the same primary transcript differently.

Alternative transcription start sites lead to different 5' exons in mRNAs for mouse amylase and myosin light chain, rat glucokinase, and Drosophila alcohol dehydrogenase and actin.

Alternative polyadenylation sites in the μ immunoglobulin heavy-chain primary transcript result in different carboxyl-terminal regions of the encoded proteins.

Alternative splicing and processing create seven unique α-tropomyosin mRNAs in seven different tissues. The mechanisms for these processing-splicing decisions are not yet fully understood.

## Regulation of Messenger RNA Stability Provides Another Control Mechanism

Most mRNAs in mammalian cells are very stable, with half-lives measured in hours. Some mRNAs turn over very rapidly, with half-lives of 10-30 minutes. The stability of mRNA can be regulated in certain instances. Changes in mRNA stability can have major effects on biologic processes. There is usually a direct relationship between mRNA amount and the translation of that mRNA into its cognate protein.

Messenger RNAs exist in the cytoplasm as ribonucleoprotein particles (RNPs). Some proteins protect mRNA from digestion by nucleases. Some proteins may promote nuclease attack under certain conditions. mRNAs are stabilized by the interaction of proteins with various structures or sequences. mRNAs are destabilized by the interaction of proteins with various structures or sequences. Hormones may regulate mRNA stability by increasing the amount of mRNA-binding proteins. Hormones may regulate mRNA stability by decreasing the amount of mRNA-binding proteins.

The ends of mRNA molecules are involved in mRNA stability. The 5'-cap structure in eukaryotic mRNA prevents attack by 5' exonucleases. The poly(A) tail minimizes the action of 3' exonucleases. In mRNA molecules with these structures, a single endonucleolytic cut allows exonucleases to attack and digest the entire molecule. Structures in the 5' untranslated region (5' UTR), the coding region, and the 3' UTR are thought to promote or prevent this initial endonucleolytic action.

A number of mechanisms are used to regulate mRNA stability and hence function. Mechanisms used to regulate mRNA stability are similar to those used to regulate the synthesis of mRNA and mRNA translation. Coordinate regulation of these processes confers on the cell remarkable adaptability. The typical eukaryotic mRNA has a 5' noncoding sequence (NCS), or untranslated exonic region (5' UTR), a coding region, and a 3' exonic untranslated NCS region (3' UTR). Essentially all mRNAs are capped at the 5' end, and most have a 100 to 200 nucleotide polyadenylate sequence at their 3' end. The 5' cap and 3' poly(A) tail protect the mRNA against exonuclease attack and are bound by specific proteins that interact to facilitate translation (Figure 38-20). Stem-loop structures in the 5' and 3' NCS, and the AU-rich region in the 3' NCS represent the binding sites for specific proteins that modulate mRNA stability (Figure 38-20).
