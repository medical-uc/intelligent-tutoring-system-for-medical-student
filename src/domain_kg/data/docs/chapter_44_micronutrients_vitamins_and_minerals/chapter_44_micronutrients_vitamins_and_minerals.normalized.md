# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Vitamins are organic nutrients required in small quantities for various biochemical functions. The body generally cannot synthesize vitamins, so they must be supplied through the diet. Lipid-soluble vitamins are hydrophobic compounds that are absorbed efficiently only when fat absorption is normal. Lipid-soluble vitamins are transported in the blood in lipoproteins or attached to specific binding proteins. Vitamin A is involved in vision and cell differentiation. Vitamin D regulates calcium and phosphate metabolism and also aids in cell differentiation. Vitamin E functions as an antioxidant. Vitamin K is crucial for blood clotting. Deficiency syndromes can arise from conditions affecting the digestion and absorption of lipid-soluble vitamins. A very-low-fat diet can lead to deficiency syndromes. Steatorrhea can lead to deficiency syndromes. Disorders of the biliary system can lead to deficiency syndromes. Night blindness is a deficiency syndrome associated with vitamin A. Xerophthalmia is a deficiency syndrome associated with vitamin A. Rickets is a deficiency syndrome associated with vitamin D in young children. Osteomalacia is a deficiency syndrome associated with vitamin D in adults. Neurologic disorders can result from vitamin E deficiency. Hemolytic anemia of the newborn can result from vitamin E deficiency. Hemorrhagic disease of the newborn can result from vitamin K deficiency. Toxicity can occur from excessive intake of vitamins A and D. Vitamin A and carotenes are precursors of vitamin A. Vitamin A, carotenes, and vitamin E are antioxidants. Antioxidants have possible roles in preventing atherosclerosis and cancer. In excess, vitamins A and E may act as pro-oxidants.

Water-soluble vitamins include vitamins B and C, folic acid, biotin, and pantothenic acid. Water-soluble vitamins mainly function as enzyme cofactors. Folic acid acts as a carrier of one-carbon units. Deficiency of a single vitamin of the B complex is rare. Poor diets are most often associated with multiple deficiency states. Beriberi is a deficiency syndrome associated with thiamin. Cheilosis, glossitis, and seborrhea are deficiency syndromes associated with riboflavin. Pellagra is a deficiency syndrome associated with niacin. Megaloblastic anemia, methylmalonic aciduria, and pernicious anemia are deficiency syndromes associated with vitamin B12. Megaloblastic anemia is a deficiency syndrome associated with folic acid. Scurvy is a deficiency syndrome associated with vitamin C.

Inorganic mineral elements must be provided in the diet to function in the body. Insufficient intake of these minerals can lead to deficiency signs. Anemia is a deficiency sign associated with iron. Cretinism and goiter are deficiency signs associated with iodine. Excessive intake of these minerals may be toxic.

## The Determination of Micronutrient Requirements Depends on the Criteria of Adequacy Chosen

For any nutrient, there is a range of intakes between that which is clearly inadequate, leading to clinical deficiency disease, and that which is so much in excess of the body's metabolic capacity that there may be signs of toxicity. Between these two extremes is a level of intake that is adequate for normal health and the maintenance of metabolic integrity. Requirements are determined in depletion/repletion studies, in which people are deprived of the nutrient until there is a metabolic change, then repleted with the nutrient until the abnormality is normalized. Individuals do not all have the same requirement for nutrients, even when calculated on the basis of body size or energy expenditure. There is a range of individual requirements of up to 25% around the mean. Therefore, in order to assess the adequacy of diets, it is necessary to set a reference level of intake high enough to ensure that no one either suffers from deficiency or is at risk of toxicity. Reference and recommended intakes of vitamins and minerals published by different national and international authorities differ because of different interpretations of the available data, and the availability of new experimental data in more recent publications.

# VITAMINS ARE A DISPARATE GROUP OF COMPOUNDS WITH A VARIETY OF METABOLIC FUNCTIONS

A vitamin is an organic compound required in the diet in small amounts for maintaining normal physiologic and metabolic integrity. Deficiency of a vitamin causes a specific disease, which is cured or prevented only by restoring the vitamin to the diet. Vitamin D is formed in the skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol on exposure to sunlight. Niacin can be formed from the essential amino acid tryptophan. Vitamin D and niacin do not strictly comply with the definition of a vitamin.

Vitamin A, known as retinol or β-carotene, functions in the formation of visual pigments in the retina and regulates gene expression and cell differentiation. β-carotene acts as an antioxidant. Deficiency of Vitamin A causes night blindness, xerophthalmia, and keratinization of the skin.

Deficiency of Vitamin D leads to rickets, which is poor mineralization of bone in children, and osteomalacia, which is bone demineralization in adults.

Vitamin E, which includes tocopherols and tocotrienols, acts as an antioxidant, especially in cell membranes, and plays roles in cell signaling. Deficiency of Vitamin E is extremely rare but can cause serious neurologic dysfunction.

Vitamin K, known as phylloquinone or menaquinones, functions as a coenzyme in the formation of γ-carboxyglutamate in enzymes of blood clotting and bone matrix. Deficiency of Vitamin K results in impaired blood clotting and hemorrhagic disease.

Niacin, known as nicotinic acid or nicotinamide, serves as a coenzyme in oxidation and reduction reactions, is a functional part of NAD and NADP, and plays a role in intracellular calcium regulation and cell signaling. Deficiency of niacin causes pellagra, characterized by photosensitive dermatitis and depressive psychosis.

Folic acid functions as a coenzyme in the transfer of one-carbon fragments. Deficiency of folic acid causes megaloblastic anemia.

Pantothenic acid is a functional part of CoA and acyl carrier protein, involved in fatty acid synthesis and metabolism. Deficiency of pantothenic acid causes peripheral nerve damage, known as nutritional melalgia or 'burning foot syndrome'.

Vitamin H, known as biotin, functions as a coenzyme in carboxylation reactions in gluconeogenesis and fatty acid synthesis and plays a role in the regulation of the cell cycle. Deficiency of Vitamin H causes impaired fat and carbohydrate metabolism and dermatitis.

Vitamin C, known as ascorbic acid, functions as a coenzyme in the hydroxylation of proline and lysine in collagen synthesis, acts as an antioxidant, and enhances the absorption of iron. Deficiency of Vitamin C causes scurvy, characterized by impaired wound healing, loss of dental cement, and subcutaneous hemorrhage.

# TWO GROUPS OF COMPOUNDS HAVE VITAMIN A ACTIVITY

Retinoids include retinol, retinaldehyde, and retinoic acid. Carotenoids are found in plants and include a variety of carotenes and related compounds. Many carotenoids are precursors of vitamin A, as they can be cleaved to yield retinaldehyde. The alpha-, beta-, and gamma-carotenes and cryptoxanthin are the most important provitamin A carotenoids. Beta-carotene and other provitamin A carotenoids are cleaved in the intestinal mucosa by carotene dioxygenase, producing retinaldehyde. Retinaldehyde is reduced to retinol, esterified, and secreted in chylomicrons along with esters from dietary retinol. Carotene dioxygenase exists in two isoenzyme forms. One isoenzyme cleaves the central bond of beta-carotene. The other isoenzyme performs asymmetric cleavage, forming 8'-, 10'-, and 12'-apo-carotenals, which are oxidized to retinoic acid but cannot be used to form retinol or retinaldehyde. The intestinal activity of carotene dioxygenase is low, so a significant portion of ingested beta-carotene may remain unchanged in the circulation. Although theoretically one molecule of beta-carotene should yield two molecules of retinol, in practice, 6 micrograms of beta-carotene is equivalent to 1 microgram of preformed retinol. The vitamin A content in foods is expressed as micrograms of retinol equivalents, calculated as micrograms of preformed vitamin A plus 1/6 times micrograms of beta-carotene plus 1/12 times micrograms of other provitamin A carotenoids. Before pure vitamin A was available for chemical analysis, the vitamin A content of foods was measured by biological assay and expressed in international units (IU). 1 IU equals 0.3 micrograms of retinol, and 1 microgram of retinol equals 3.33 IU. Although obsolete, IU is sometimes still used in food labeling. The term retinol activity equivalent accounts for the incomplete absorption and metabolism of carotenoids. 1 RAE equals 1 microgram of all-trans-retinol, 12 micrograms of beta-carotene, or 24 micrograms of alpha-carotene or beta-cryptoxanthin. On this basis, 1 IU of vitamin A activity is equal to 3.6 micrograms of beta-carotene or 7.2 micrograms of other provitamin A carotenoids. Beta-carotene is cleaved symmetrically by carotene dioxygenase to yield retinaldehyde (Figure 44-1).

## Vitamin A Has a Function in Vision

In the retina, retinaldehyde functions as the prosthetic group of the light-sensitive opsin proteins. Retinaldehyde forms rhodopsin in rods. Retinaldehyde forms iodopsin in cones. Each cone cell contains only one type of opsin. Each cone cell is sensitive to only one color. In the pigment epithelium of the retina, all-trans-retinol is isomerized to 11-cis-retinol. In the pigment epithelium of the retina, 11-cis-retinol is oxidized to 11-cis-retinaldehyde. 11-cis-retinaldehyde reacts with a lysine residue in opsin to form the holoprotein rhodopsin. Absorption of light by rhodopsin causes isomerization of retinaldehyde from 11-cis to all-trans. Absorption of light by rhodopsin causes a conformational change in opsin. Absorption of light by rhodopsin results in the release of retinaldehyde from the protein. Absorption of light by rhodopsin initiates a nerve impulse. The formation of the initial excited form of rhodopsin, bathorhodopsin, occurs within picoseconds of illumination. There is a series of conformational changes leading to the formation of metarhodopsin II. Metarhodopsin II initiates a guanine nucleotide amplification cascade. Metarhodopsin II initiates a nerve impulse. The final step is hydrolysis to release all-trans-retinaldehyde and opsin. The key to initiation of the visual cycle is the availability of 11-cis-retinaldehyde, and hence vitamin A. In deficiency, both the time taken to adapt to darkness and the ability to see in poor light are impaired (Figure 44-2).

## Retinoic Acid Has a Role in the Regulation of Gene Expression and Tissue Differentiation

A major role of vitamin A is the control of cell differentiation and turnover. All-trans-retinoic acid and 9-cis-retinoic acid regulate growth, development, and tissue differentiation. All-trans-retinoic acid and 9-cis-retinoic acid have different actions in different tissues. Like thyroid and steroid hormones and vitamin D, retinoic acid binds to nuclear receptors that bind to response elements of DNA and regulate the transcription of specific genes. There are two families of nuclear retinoid receptors: the retinoic acid receptors (RARs) bind all-trans-retinoic acid or 9-cis-retinoic acid, and the retinoid X receptors (RXR) bind 9-cis-retinoic acid. RXRs also form heterodimers with vitamin D, thyroid, and other nuclear acting hormone receptors. Deficiency of vitamin A impairs vitamin D and thyroid hormone function because of lack of 9-cis-retinoic acid to form active receptor dimers. Unoccupied RXRs form dimers with occupied vitamin D and thyroid hormone receptors. Unoccupied RXRs are unable to activate gene expression and may repress it. Consequently, vitamin A deficiency has a more severe effect on vitamin D and thyroid hormone function than simply interfering with gene expression. Excessive vitamin A also impairs vitamin D and thyroid hormone function, because of formation of RXR homodimers. There are not enough RXR available to form heterodimers with the vitamin D and thyroid hormone receptors due to excessive vitamin A.

## Vitamin A Deficiency Is a Major Public Health Problem Worldwide

Vitamin A deficiency is the most important preventable cause of blindness.

Vitamin A deficiency leads to a loss of sensitivity to green light.

Vitamin A deficiency causes impairment in adapting to dim light.

Vitamin A deficiency results in night blindness, an inability to see in the dark.

Prolonged Vitamin A deficiency leads to xerophthalmia.

Xerophthalmia involves keratinization of the cornea.

Xerophthalmia can lead to blindness.

Vitamin A plays a crucial role in the differentiation of immune system cells.

Mild Vitamin A deficiency increases susceptibility to infectious diseases.

The synthesis of retinol-binding protein is reduced in response to infection.

Retinol-binding protein is required to transport Vitamin A in the bloodstream.

Retinol-binding protein is a negative acute phase protein.

Reduced synthesis of retinol-binding protein decreases the circulating concentration of Vitamin A.

Decreased circulating Vitamin A concentration further impairs immune responses.

## Vitamin A Is Toxic in Excess

Humans have a limited capacity to metabolize vitamin A.

Excessive intakes of vitamin A lead to accumulation beyond the capacity of intracellular binding proteins.

Unbound vitamin A causes membrane lysis and tissue damage.

Symptoms of vitamin A toxicity affect the central nervous system, including headache, nausea, ataxia, and anorexia, all associated with increased cerebrospinal fluid pressure.

Vitamin A toxicity affects the liver, causing hepatomegaly with histological changes and hyperlipidemia.

Vitamin A toxicity disrupts calcium homeostasis, leading to thickening of the long bones, hypercalcemia, and calcification of soft tissues.

Vitamin A toxicity affects the skin, causing excessive dryness, desquamation, and alopecia.

# VITAMIN D IS REALLY A HORMONE

Vitamin D is not strictly a vitamin since it can be synthesized in the skin.

Under most conditions, the skin is the major source of vitamin D.

A dietary source of vitamin D is required only when sunlight exposure is inadequate.

Vitamin D's main function is to regulate calcium absorption and homeostasis.

Most of vitamin D's actions are mediated by nuclear receptors that regulate gene expression.

Vitamin D has a role in regulating cell proliferation and differentiation.

Intakes of vitamin D higher than those required to maintain calcium homeostasis reduce the risk of insulin resistance, obesity, and metabolic syndrome.

High intakes of vitamin D also reduce the risk of various cancers.

Deficiency in vitamin D leads to rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults.

Deficiency in vitamin D continues to be a problem in northern latitudes, where sunlight exposure is inadequate.

## Vitamin D Metabolism Is Both Regulated by and Regulates Calcium Homeostasis

The main function of vitamin D is to control calcium homeostasis. Vitamin D metabolism is regulated by factors that respond to plasma concentrations of calcium and phosphate. Calcitriol reduces its own synthesis by inducing the 24-hydroxylase and repressing the 1-hydroxylase in the kidney. The principal function of vitamin D is to maintain the plasma calcium concentration. Calcitriol increases intestinal absorption of calcium. Calcitriol reduces excretion of calcium by stimulating reabsorption in the distal renal tubules. Calcitriol mobilizes bone mineral. Calcitriol is involved in insulin secretion. Calcitriol is involved in the synthesis and secretion of parathyroid and thyroid hormones. Calcitriol inhibits the production of interleukin by activated T-lymphocytes. Calcitriol inhibits the production of immunoglobulin by activated B-lymphocytes. Calcitriol is involved in the differentiation of monocyte precursor cells. Calcitriol modulates cell proliferation. In most of these actions, calcitriol acts like a steroid hormone. Calcitriol binds to nuclear receptors. Calcitriol enhances gene expression. Calcitriol has a rapid effect to mobilize calcium transporters in the intestinal mucosa.

## Higher Intakes of Vitamin D May or May Not Be Beneficial

Higher vitamin D status is protective against various cancers, including prostate and colorectal cancer.

Higher vitamin D status is protective against prediabetes and metabolic syndrome.

Randomized clinical trial data suggest no benefit with vitamin D supplementation.

Low vitamin D status is common and increases risk.

Supplementing with vitamin D if status is low may be an option.

Increased sunlight exposure would meet the need for vitamin D.

Increased sunlight exposure carries the risk of developing skin cancer.

## Vitamin D Deficiency Affects Children & Adults

In the vitamin D deficiency disease rickets, the bones of children are undermineralized. Poor absorption of calcium causes the bones of children to be undermineralized in rickets. Vitamin D deficiency during the adolescent growth spurt causes similar problems to rickets. Osteomalacia in adults results from the demineralization of bone. Osteomalacia particularly affects women who have little exposure to sunlight. Osteomalacia especially affects women after several pregnancies. Vitamin D is essential for the prevention and treatment of osteomalacia in the elderly. There is less evidence that vitamin D is beneficial in treating osteoporosis.

## Vitamin D Is Toxic in Excess

Some infants are sensitive to intakes of vitamin D as low as 50 µ g/day. An elevated plasma concentration of calcium can result from such sensitivity. An elevated plasma concentration of calcium can lead to contraction of blood vessels. An elevated plasma concentration of calcium can lead to high blood pressure. An elevated plasma concentration of calcium can lead to calcinosis, the calcification of soft tissues. Hypercalcemia in response to low intakes of vitamin D is due to genetic defects of calcidiol 24-hydroxylase in at least a few cases. Calcidiol 24-hydroxylase is the enzyme that leads to inactivation of vitamin D. Excess dietary vitamin D is toxic. Excessive exposure to sunlight does not lead to vitamin D poisoning. There is a limited capacity to form the precursor, 7-dehydrocholesterol. Prolonged exposure of previtamin D to sunlight leads to formation of inactive compounds.

# VITAMIN E DOES NOT HAVE A PRECISELY DEFINED METABOLIC FUNCTION

No unequivocal unique function for vitamin E has been defined. Vitamin E acts as a lipid-soluble antioxidant in cell membranes. Vitamin E maintains the fluidity of cell membranes. Vitamin E has a role in cell signaling, although this role is relatively poorly defined. Vitamin E is the generic descriptor for two families of compounds: the tocopherols and the tocotrienols (Figure 44-5). The different vitamers of vitamin E have different biologic potencies. D-α-tocopherol is the most active vitamer. Vitamin E intake is usually expressed in terms of milligrams D-α-tocopherol equivalents. Synthetic DL-α-tocopherol does not have the same biologic potency as the naturally occurring D-α-tocopherol. In α-tocopherol and tocotrienol, R1, R2, and R3 are all -CH3 groups. In the β-vitamers, R2 is H. In the γ-vitamers, R1 is H. In the δ-vitamers, R1 and R2 are both H (Figure 44-5).

## Vitamin E Is the Major Lipid-Soluble Antioxidant in Cell Membranes & Plasma Lipoproteins

The main function of vitamin E is to act as a chain-breaking, free radical–trapping antioxidant in cell membranes and plasma lipoproteins.

Vitamin E reacts with lipid peroxide radicals formed by peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids.

The tocopheroxyl radical is relatively unreactive and ultimately forms nonradical compounds.

The tocopheroxyl radical is commonly reduced back to tocopherol by reaction with vitamin C from plasma.

The resultant, stable, monodehydroascorbate radical undergoes enzymic or nonenzymic reaction to yield ascorbate and dehydroascorbate.

Neither ascorbate nor dehydroascorbate is a radical.

## Vitamin E Deficiency

In experimental animals, vitamin E deficiency results in resorption of fetuses. In experimental animals, vitamin E deficiency causes testicular atrophy.

Dietary deficiency of vitamin E in human beings is unknown. Patients with severe fat malabsorption suffer vitamin E deficiency. Patients with cystic fibrosis suffer vitamin E deficiency. Patients with some forms of chronic liver disease suffer vitamin E deficiency. Patients with severe fat malabsorption, cystic fibrosis, or some forms of chronic liver disease are unable to absorb or transport vitamin E. This inability leads to nerve and muscle membrane damage.

Premature infants are born with inadequate reserves of vitamin E. Erythrocyte membranes become abnormally fragile due to lipid peroxidation. Erythrocyte membrane fragility leads to hemolytic anemia.

# VITAMIN K IS REQUIRED FOR SYNTHESIS OF BLOOD-CLOTTING PROTEINS

Vitamin K was discovered as a result of investigations into the cause of a bleeding disorder known as hemorrhagic disease of cattle and chickens fed on a fat-free diet. The missing factor in the diet of the chickens was vitamin K. The cattle feed contained dicumarol, an antagonist of the vitamin. Antagonists of vitamin K are used to reduce blood coagulation in patients at risk of thrombosis. The most widely used antagonist of vitamin K is warfarin.

Three compounds have the biological activity of vitamin K: phylloquinone, the normal dietary source, found in green vegetables; menaquinones, synthesized by intestinal bacteria, with differing lengths of side chain; and menadione and menadiol diacetate, synthetic compounds that can be metabolized to phylloquinone. Phylloquinone is the normal dietary source of vitamin K and is found in green vegetables. Menaquinones are synthesized by intestinal bacteria and have differing lengths of side chain. Menadione and menadiol diacetate are synthetic compounds that can be metabolized to phylloquinone. Menaquinones are absorbed to some extent. It is not clear to what extent menaquinones are biologically active. It is possible to induce signs of vitamin K deficiency simply by feeding a phylloquinone-deficient diet, without inhibiting intestinal bacterial action.

Vitamin K hydroquinone is oxidized to the epoxide, which activates a glutamate residue in the protein substrate to a carbanion. The carbanion reacts nonenzymically with carbon dioxide to form GLA. Vitamin K epoxide is reduced to the quinone by a warfarin-sensitive reductase. The quinone is reduced to the active hydroquinone by either the same warfarin-sensitive reductase or a warfarin-insensitive quinone reductase. Warfarin prevents recycling of vitamin K. In the presence of warfarin, vitamin K epoxide cannot be reduced. Vitamin K epoxide accumulates and is excreted in the presence of warfarin. A high dose of vitamin K can be reduced to the active hydroquinone by the warfarin-insensitive enzyme. Carboxylation can continue with stoichiometric utilization of vitamin K and excretion of the epoxide in the presence of a high dose of vitamin K. A high dose of vitamin K is the antidote to an overdose of warfarin.

## Vitamin K Is Also Important in Synthesis of Bone & Other Calcium-Binding Proteins

Vitamin K is important in the synthesis of bone and other calcium-binding proteins. Osteocalcin undergoes vitamin K-dependent carboxylation of glutamate to GLA. The matrix Gla protein in bone also undergoes this carboxylation. Nephrocalcin in the kidney undergoes vitamin K-dependent carboxylation. The product of the growth arrest specific gene Gas6 undergoes vitamin K-dependent carboxylation. Gas6 is involved in the regulation of differentiation and development in the nervous system. Gas6 controls apoptosis in other tissues. All these GLA-containing proteins bind calcium. Calcium binding causes a conformational change in these proteins. The conformational change allows these proteins to interact with membrane phospholipids. A low carboxylation state of osteocalcin in plasma is a biomarker of low Vitamin K status. A low carboxylation state of osteocalcin indicates inadequate bone health.

# VITAMIN B₁ (THIAMIN) HAS A KEY ROLE IN CARBOHYDRATE METABOLISM

Thiamin has a central role in energy-yielding metabolism. Thiamin has a central role in the metabolism of carbohydrates (Figure 44--8).

Thiamin is transported across the plasma membrane by specific transporters. The specific transporters belong to the SLC19A transporter family.

Thiamin is rapidly phosphorylated to thiamin diphosphate by thiamine pyrophosphokinase once inside the cell.

[FIGURE:fig-44-8]

Thiamin.

In some cells, thiamin diphosphate can be directly transported.

In the enterocyte, phosphatases rapidly dephosphorylate thiamin diphosphate. The absorbed thiamine is delivered to the blood.

Thiamine is delivered to all cells. Thiamine is delivered to red blood cells. Thiamine is delivered to leukocytes. Thiamine is delivered to platelets.

In target cells, thiamine is taken up. In target cells, thiamine is phosphorylated in the cytosol to thiamin diphosphate.

Thiamin diphosphate is the coenzyme for pyruvate dehydrogenase in carbohydrate metabolism. Thiamin diphosphate is the coenzyme for the branched-chain keto acid dehydrogenase involved in the metabolism of leucine, isoleucine, and valine.

Thiamin diphosphate provides a reactive carbon on the thiazole moiety. A carbanion forms on the reactive carbon provided by thiamin diphosphate. The carbanion adds to the carbonyl group, for example, pyruvate.

The addition compound is then decarboxylated.

For mitochondrial-associated enzymes, thiamin diphosphate is transported into the mitochondria. Pyruvate dehydrogenase is a mitochondrial-associated enzyme.

Thiamin diphosphate is the coenzyme for transketolase. Transketolase is involved in the pentose phosphate pathway.

Thiamin triphosphate has a role in nerve conduction. Thiamin triphosphate phosphorylates, and so activates, a chloride channel in the nerve membrane.

## Thiamin Deficiency Affects the Nervous System & the Heart

Thiamin deficiency can result in three distinct syndromes: a chronic peripheral neuritis, beriberi, and Wernicke encephalopathy with Korsakoff psychosis.

Beriberi may or may not be associated with heart failure and edema.

Acute pernicious (fulminating) beriberi, also known as Shoshin beriberi, is characterized by heart failure and metabolic abnormalities, without peripheral neuritis.

Wernicke encephalopathy with Korsakoff psychosis is associated especially with alcohol and narcotic abuse.

Thiamin diphosphate plays a role in pyruvate dehydrogenase, and in thiamin deficiency, there is impaired conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA.

In subjects on a relatively high-carbohydrate diet, thiamin deficiency results in increased plasma concentrations of lactate and pyruvate.

Increased plasma concentrations of lactate and pyruvate due to thiamin deficiency may cause life-threatening lactic acidosis.

## Thiamin Nutritional Status Can Be Assessed by Erythrocyte Transketolase Activation

The activation of apotransketolase in erythrocyte lysate by thiamin diphosphate added in vitro has become the accepted index of thiamin nutritional status.

A greater than 25% increase in activity in response to thiamin diphosphate is considered abnormal.

# VITAMIN B₂ (RIBOFLAVIN) HAS A CENTRAL ROLE IN ENERGY-YIELDING METABOLISM

Riboflavin provides the reactive moieties of the coenzymes flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD).

FMN is formed by ATP-dependent phosphorylation of riboflavin.

FAD is synthesized by further reaction with ATP in which the AMP moiety is transferred onto FMN.

The main dietary sources of riboflavin are milk and dairy products.

Riboflavin is widely used as a food additive because of its intense yellow color.

## Flavin Coenzymes Are Electron Carriers in Oxidoreduction Reactions

Flavin coenzymes are electron carriers in oxidoreduction reactions. The mitochondrial respiratory chain involves flavin coenzymes. Key enzymes in fatty acid oxidation involve flavin coenzymes. Key enzymes in amino acid oxidation involve flavin coenzymes. The citric acid cycle involves flavin coenzymes.

Reoxidation of the reduced flavin in oxygenases and mixed-function oxidases proceeds by the formation of the flavin radical. Reoxidation of the reduced flavin in oxygenases and mixed-function oxidases proceeds by the formation of flavin hydroperoxide. Intermediate generation of superoxide occurs during reoxidation. Intermediate generation of perhydroxyl radicals occurs during reoxidation. Intermediate generation of hydrogen peroxide occurs during reoxidation.

Flavin oxidases contribute significantly to the total physiologic oxidant stress in the body. Glutathione reductase is an FAD-dependent enzyme. Glutathione reductase helps in the recycling of glutathione. Glutathione is an essential player in protection from physiologic oxidative stress. Glutathione is an essential player in protection from pathophysiologic oxidative stress.

## Riboflavin Deficiency Is Widespread but Not Fatal

Riboflavin is centrally involved in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism.

Riboflavin deficiency occurs in many countries.

Riboflavin deficiency is not fatal because there is very efficient conservation of tissue riboflavin.

The gold standard test for measuring riboflavin status is the measurement of erythrocyte glutathione reductase activity in red blood cells.

Erythrocyte glutathione reductase is an FAD-dependent enzyme.

Riboflavin deficiency can be caused by inadequate intake or endocrine abnormalities.

Alcohol interferes with both the digestion and absorption of riboflavin.

Riboflavin released by the catabolism of enzymes is rapidly incorporated into newly synthesized enzymes.

Riboflavin deficiency is characterized by cheilosis, desquamation and inflammation of the tongue, and seborrheic dermatitis.

Riboflavin nutritional status is assessed by measurement of the activation of erythrocyte glutathione reductase by FAD added in vitro.

# NIACIN IS NOT STRICTLY A VITAMIN

Niacin was discovered as a nutrient during studies of pellagra. Niacin is not strictly a vitamin. Niacin can be synthesized in the body from the essential amino acid tryptophan. Two compounds, nicotinic acid and nicotinamide, have the biological activity of niacin. The metabolic function of niacin is as the nicotinamide ring of the coenzymes NAD and NADP in oxidation/reduction reactions. Some 60 mg of tryptophan is equivalent to 1 mg of dietary niacin. The niacin content of foods is expressed as mg niacin equivalents = mg preformed niacin + 1/60 × mg tryptophan. Since most of the niacin in cereals is biologically unavailable, this is discounted.

## NAD Is the Source of ADP-Ribose

NAD is the source of ADP-ribose for the ADP-ribosylation of proteins.

NAD is the source of ADP-ribose for the poly-ADP-ribosylation of nucleoproteins involved in the DNA repair mechanism.

Cyclic ADP-ribose is formed from NAD.

Cyclic ADP-ribose acts to increase intracellular calcium in response to neurotransmitters and hormones.

Nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide is formed from NAD.

Nicotinic acid adenine dinucleotide acts to increase intracellular calcium in response to neurotransmitters and hormones.

## Pellagra Is Caused by Deficiency of Tryptophan & Niacin

Pellagra is characterized by photosensitive dermatitis. Pellagra leads to depressive psychosis as it progresses. Pellagra may cause diarrhea. Untreated pellagra is fatal. Pellagra is caused by a deficiency of tryptophan or niacin. Tryptophan prevents pellagra. Niacin prevents pellagra. Riboflavin deficiency may be important in pellagra. In outbreaks of pellagra, twice as many women as men are affected. Estrogen metabolites inhibit tryptophan metabolism, affecting the prevalence of pellagra in women.

## Pellagra Can Result from Disease Despite an Adequate Intake of Tryptophan & Niacin

Hartnup disease is a rare genetic condition that affects tryptophan metabolism. Hartnup disease causes a defect in the membrane transport mechanism for tryptophan. Hartnup disease leads to large losses of tryptophan due to intestinal malabsorption. Hartnup disease also results in the failure of renal reabsorption of tryptophan. Pellagra can develop in Hartnup disease despite an adequate intake of tryptophan and niacin.

Carcinoid syndrome involves the metastasis of a primary liver tumor originating from enterochromaffin cells. Carcinoid syndrome leads to the overproduction of 5-hydroxytryptamine. Carcinoid syndrome can divert up to 60% of the body's tryptophan metabolism towards 5-hydroxytryptamine production. Carcinoid syndrome can cause pellagra by reducing NAD synthesis.

## Niacin Is Toxic in Excess

Nicotinic acid is used to treat hyperlipidemia at doses ranging from 1 to 6 g/day.

Nicotinic acid causes dilation of blood vessels and flushing at these doses.

Nicotinic acid causes skin irritation at these doses.

Intakes of nicotinic acid in excess of 500 mg/day cause liver damage.

Intakes of nicotinamide in excess of 500 mg/day cause liver damage.

Niacin at high doses can harm an unborn fetus.

Niacin can pass into breast milk.

Nursing mothers should avoid taking niacin.

## Vitamin B 12 Deficiency Causes Pernicious Anemia

Functional folate deficiency disturbs one carbon metabolism and DNA synthesis. ## Inhibitors of Folate Metabolism Provide Cancer Chemotherapy, Antibacterial, & Antimalarial Drugs

The accumulation of methyltetrahydrofolate creates a 'folate trap'.

## Folate Deficiency Causes Megaloblastic Anemia

Folate deficiency causes megaloblastic anemia. Rapidly dividing cells are affected by folic acid deficiency. Rapidly dividing cells require a large amount of thymidine for DNA synthesis. The bone marrow is clinically affected by this deficiency. The bone marrow being affected by this deficiency leads to megaloblastic anemia.

Supplements of 400 µg/day of folic acid reduce the incidence of spina bifida and other neural tube defects if begun before conception. Many countries have mandatory enrichment of flour with folic acid due to its role in reducing neural tube defects. Elevated blood homocysteine is a significant risk factor for atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and hypertension. Impaired ability to form methyltetrahydrofolate by methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase causes functional folate deficiency. Impaired ability to form methyltetrahydrofolate by methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase results in the failure to remethylate homocysteine to methionine. People with an abnormal variant of methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase, occurring in 5 to 10% of the population, do not develop hyperhomocysteinemia if they have a relatively high intake of folate. Placebo-controlled trials of folate supplements, often combined with vitamins B_{6} and B_{12}, have shown a lowering of plasma homocysteine. Apart from a reduced incidence of stroke, there has been no effect on death from cardiovascular disease in these trials.

Low folate status results in impaired methylation of CpG islands in DNA. Impaired methylation of CpG islands in DNA is a factor in the development of colorectal and other cancers. Studies suggest that folic acid supplementation or food enrichment may reduce the risk of developing some cancers. Evidence also suggests that folate supplements increase the rate of transformation of preneoplastic colorectal polyps into cancers. People with such polyps may be at increased risk of developing colorectal cancer if they have a high folate intake.

## Vitamin C Is the Coenzyme for Two Groups of Hydroxylases

Ascorbic acid has specific roles in the copper-containing hydroxylases. Ascorbic acid increases the activity of a number of other enzymes in vitro. # MINERALS ARE REQUIRED FOR BOTH PHYSIOLOGIC & BIOCHEMICAL FUNCTIONS

Many essential minerals are widely distributed in foods, and most people eating a mixed diet are likely to receive adequate intakes. The amounts required for minerals vary from grams per day for sodium and calcium, through milligrams per day for iron and zinc, to micrograms per day for the trace elements. Mineral deficiencies occur when foods come from one region where the soil may be deficient in some minerals, such as iodine and selenium. When foods come from a variety of regions, mineral deficiency is less likely to occur. Iron deficiency is an important problem worldwide because if iron losses from the body are relatively high, such as from heavy menstrual blood loss or intestinal parasites, it is difficult to achieve an adequate intake to replace losses. However, 10% of the population, and more in some areas, are genetically at risk of iron overload. Iron overload leads to the formation of free radicals as a result of nonenzymic reactions of iron ions in free solution when the capacity of iron-binding proteins has been exceeded. Foods grown on soil containing high levels of selenium cause toxicity. Excessive intakes of sodium cause hypertension in susceptible people.
