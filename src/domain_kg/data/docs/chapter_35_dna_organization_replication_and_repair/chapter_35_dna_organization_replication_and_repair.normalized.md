# Overview

Appreciate that the 3 × 10º base pairs of DNA that compose the haploid genome of humans are divided uniquely between 23 linear DNA units, the chromosomes. Humans, being diploid, have 23 pairs of these linear chromosomes: 22 autosomes and two sex chromosomes, X and Y.
Understand that human genomic DNA, if extended end-to-end, would be meters in length, yet still fits within the nucleus of the cell, an organelle that is only microns (μ; 10⁶ meters) in diameter. Such condensation in DNA length, in part, is induced following its association with the highly positively charged histone proteins resulting in the formation of a unique DNA-histone complex termed the nucleosome. Nucleosomes have DNA wrapped around the surface of an octamer of histones.
Explain that strings of nucleosomes form along the linear sequence of genomic DNA to form chromatin, which itself can be more tightly packaged and condensed, this ultimately leads to the formation of the chromosomes.
Appreciate that while the chromosomes are the macroscopic functional units for DNA transcription, replication, recombination, gene assortment, and cellular division, it is DNA function at the level of the individual nucleotides that composes regulatory sequences linked to specific genes that are essential for life.
Describe the steps, phase of the cell cycle, and the molecules responsible for the replication, repair, and recombination of DNA, and understand the negative effects that errors in any of these processes can have on cellular, and thus, organismal integrity and health

# CHROMATIN IS THE CHROMOSOMAL MATERIAL IN THE NUCLEI OF CELLS OF EUKARYOTIC ORGANISMS

Chromatin consists of very long double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) molecules. Chromatin contains a nearly equal mass of small basic proteins termed histones. Chromatin includes a smaller amount of nonhistone proteins, most of which are acidic and larger than histones. Chromatin also contains a small quantity of RNA. The nonhistone proteins in chromatin include enzymes involved in DNA replication and repair. Nonhistone proteins are involved in RNA synthesis, processing, and transport to the cytoplasm. Nonhistone proteins regulate various processes in chromatin. The dsDNA helix in each chromosome has a length that is thousands of times the diameter of the cell nucleus. One purpose of the molecules that comprise chromatin, particularly the histones, is to condense the DNA. Histones also participate integrally in gene regulation. Histones contribute importantly to all DNA-directed molecular transactions. Electron microscopic studies of chromatin have demonstrated dense spherical particles called nucleosomes. Nucleosomes are approximately 10 nm in diameter. Nucleosomes are connected by DNA filaments. Nucleosomes are composed of DNA wound around an octameric complex of histone molecules. Biochemical, biophysical, and X-ray crystallography data corroborate this model of nucleosome structure. (Figure 35-1)

## Histones Are the Most Abundant Chromatin Proteins

Histones are a small family of closely related basic proteins. H1 histones are the least tightly bound to chromatin (Figures 35-1. 35-2, and 35-3). H1 histones can be easily removed with a salt solution. After removing H1 histones, chromatin becomes more soluble. The organizational unit of this soluble chromatin is the nucleosome. Nucleosomes contain four major types of histones: H2A, H2B, H3, and H4. The sequence and structures of these four histones, known as core histones, have been highly conserved between species. Variants of these core histones exist in various organisms for specialized purposes. The extreme conservation of core histone sequences suggests that their overall functions are identical in all eukaryotes. The entire histone molecule is involved in carrying out these functions. The carboxyl terminal two-thirds of the histone molecules are hydrophobic. The amino terminal thirds of the histone molecules are particularly rich in basic amino acids.

The four core histones are subject to at least six major types of covalent modification or posttranslational modifications (PTMs): acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, ADP-ribosylation, monoubiquitylation, and sumoylation. Histone modifications play important roles in chromatin structure and function (Table 35-1).

Chapters 38 and 42 provide a more detailed discussion of the role of histone PTMs in cellular biology.

Acetylation of histones H3 and H4 is associated with the activation of gene transcription. Acetylation of histones H3 and H4 is associated with the inactivation of gene transcription. Acetylation of core histones is associated with chromosomal assembly during DNA replication. Phosphorylation of histone H1 is associated with the condensation of chromosomes during the replication cycle. ADP-ribosylation of histones is associated with DNA repair. Methylation of histones is correlated with activation of gene transcription. Methylation of histones is correlated with repression of gene transcription. Monoubiquitylation is associated with gene activation. Monoubiquitylation is associated with gene repression. Monoubiquitylation is associated with heterochromatic gene silencing. Sumoylation of histones (SUMO; small ubiquitin-related modifier) is associated with transcription repression. Replacement of H2A with alternative H2AZ within nucleosomes is associated with transcriptional activation. Alternative acylations of histones (propionylation, butyrylation, crotonylation, succinylation, malonylation, and 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation) likely link histone modifications with intracellular metabolism. Alternative acylations of histones (propionylation, butyrylation, crotonylation, succinylation, malonylation, and 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation) correlate with gene activity.

## The Nucleosome Contains Histone & DNA

The histone octamer mixed with purified dsDNA under appropriate ionic conditions forms the same x-ray diffraction pattern as freshly isolated chromatin. Biochemical studies confirm the existence of reconstituted nucleosomes in vitro. Electron microscopic studies confirm the existence of reconstituted nucleosomes in vitro. The reconstitution of nucleosomes from DNA and histones H2A, H2B, H3, and H4 is independent of the organismal or cellular origin of the components. Nucleosome formation is an ancient process. Nucleosome formation is evolutionarily conserved. Neither histone H1 nor nonhistone proteins are necessary for the reconstitution of the nucleosome core.

In the nucleosome, DNA is supercoiled in a left-handed helix over the surface of the disk-shaped histone octamer. Core histone proteins interact with the DNA on the inside of the supercoil. The amino terminal tails of all histones extend outside the structure. The amino terminal tails of all histones are available for regulatory PTMs.

The (H3-H4)2 tetramer confers nucleosome-like properties on DNA. The (H3-H4)2 tetramer has a central role in nucleosome formation. Two H2A-H2B dimers stabilize the primary particle. Two H2A-H2B dimers bind two additional half-turns of DNA to the (H3-H4)2 tetramer.

1.75 superhelical turns of DNA are wrapped around the histone octamer. The histone octamer protects 145 to 150 bp of DNA. The nucleosome core particle is formed by the DNA wrapped around the histone octamer. In chromatin, adjacent core particles are separated by a roughly 30-bp region of DNA termed 'linker'. Most DNA is in a repeating series of these structures. A repeating series of these structures gives chromatin a 'beads-on-a-string' appearance under electron microscopy.

In vivo, the assembly of nucleosomes is mediated by nuclear chromatin assembly factors. Histone chaperones facilitate the assembly of nucleosomes. Histone chaperones exhibit high affinity for binding histones. As nucleosomes are assembled, histones are released from the histone chaperones. Nucleosomes exhibit preference for certain regions on specific DNA molecules. Nucleosome distribution is termed phasing. Phasing is related to the physical flexibility of particular nucleotide sequences. The presence of other DNA-bound factors limits nucleosome deposition sites.

# HIGHER-ORDER STRUCTURES PROVIDE FOR THE COMPACTION OF CHROMATIN

Electron microscopy of chromatin reveals the 10-nm fibril. Electron microscopy of chromatin reveals the 30-nm chromatin fiber. The disk-like nucleosome structure has a 10-nm diameter. The disk-like nucleosome structure has a height of 5 nm. The 10-nm fibril consists of nucleosomes arranged with their edges separated by a small distance. The 10-nm fibril consists of nucleosomes with 30 bp of linker DNA. The flat faces of nucleosomes in the 10-nm fibril are parallel to the fibril axis. The 10-nm fibril is probably further supercoiled to form the 30-nm chromatin fiber. The supercoiled structure of the 10-nm fibril has six or seven nucleosomes per turn. Each turn of the supercoil in the 30-nm chromatin fiber is relatively flat. The faces of the nucleosomes in successive turns of the 30-nm fiber are nearly parallel to each other. H1 histones appear to stabilize the 30-nm fiber. The position of H1 histones is not clear. The position of the variable length linker DNA is not clear. It is probable that nucleosomes can form a variety of packed structures. In order to form a mitotic chromosome, the 30-nm fiber must be compacted in length another 100-fold.

In interphase chromosomes, chromatin fibers appear to be organized into 30,000 to 100,000 bp loops or domains anchored in a scaffolding, or supporting matrix structure within the nucleus, known as the nuclear matrix. Within these domains, referred to as Topologically Associated Domains, or TADs, some DNA sequences are located nonrandomly. It is thought that at least some of the looped domains of chromatin correspond to one or more separate genetic functions. These looped domains of chromatin contain both coding and noncoding regions of a particular gene or genes. This nuclear architecture is dynamic and has important regulatory effects on gene regulation. Recent data suggest that certain genes or gene regions are mobile within the nucleus, moving obligatorily to discrete loci within the nucleus on activation. Future work in the area of the 3-D organization of nuclear chromatin, and further characterization of regulatory TADs, will elucidate the exact biological functions and molecular mechanisms responsible.

# SOME REGIONS OF CHROMATIN ARE “ACTIVE”& OTHERS ARE “INACTIVE”

Every cell in a metazoan organism contains the same genetic information. The differences between cell types are due to differential expression of this genetic information. Active chromatin contains genes that are transcriptionally or potentially transcriptionally active. Active chromatin differs from inactive chromatin in several ways. The nucleosome structure of active chromatin is altered, sometimes extensively, in highly active regions. Active chromatin is more sensitive to digestion by DNase I. DNase I digests DNA that is not protected by protein. Sensitivity to DNase I in active chromatin reflects potential for transcription rather than actual transcription. Active chromatin often has a relative lack of 5-methyldeoxycytidine in the DNA. Active chromatin also has particular histone variants and histone post-translational modifications like phosphorylation and acetylation. Within active chromatin, shorter stretches of 100 to 300 nucleotides exhibit greater sensitivity to DNase I. Hypersensitive sites result from structural conformations that favor nuclease access to the DNA. Hypersensitive sites are often located upstream of active genes. Nonhistone regulatory transcription factor proteins can cause interrupted nucleosomal structure, leading to hypersensitive sites. Hypersensitive sites often indicate the presence and location of transcription control elements. Transcriptionally inactive chromatin is densely packed and referred to as heterochromatin. Transcriptionally active chromatin is less densely packed and referred to as euchromatin. Euchromatin is generally replicated earlier than heterochromatin in the mammalian cell cycle. Inactive chromatin often has high levels of 5-methyldeoxycytidine and lower levels of certain 'activating' histone modifications. Inactive chromatin has higher levels of 'repressing' histone post-translational modifications. There are two types of heterochromatin: constitutive and facultative. Constitutive heterochromatin is always highly condensed and inactive. Constitutive heterochromatin is found near chromosomal centromeres and telomeres. Facultative heterochromatin can be condensed or actively transcribed. In mammalian females, one of the two X chromosomes is almost completely inactive transcriptionally and is heterochromatic. The inactive X chromosome decondenses during gametogenesis and becomes transcriptionally active during early embryogenesis, making it facultative heterochromatin. Insect cells, such as those of Chironomus and Drosophila, contain giant chromosomes replicated multiple times without separation. Polytene chromosomes have regions of condensed and extended chromatin. Active regions of polytene chromosomes are decondensed into 'puffs'. Puffs contain enzymes responsible for transcription and are sites of RNA synthesis. Fluorescence in situ hybridization techniques can map specific gene sequences within human cell nuclei without polytene chromosome formation.

During the metaphase phase of the mitotic cycle, the duplicated chromosomes condense and can readily be visualized. Condensed chromosomes possess a twofold symmetry. The identical duplicated sister chromatids are connected at a chromosomal structure termed the centromere. The relative position of the centromere is characteristic for a given chromosome. Metazoan centromeres are bound by nucleosomes containing the special histone H3 variant protein CENP-A and other specific centromere-binding proteins. The kinetochore, called the kinetochore, provides the anchor for the mitotic spindle, upon which chromosomal segregation occurs during mitosis (Figure 35-4).

## Coding Regions Are Often Interrupted by Intervening Sequences

The protein coding regions of DNA are usually interrupted in the eukaryotic genome by large intervening sequences of nonprotein-coding DNA. The primary transcripts, or mRNA precursors, are originally termed hnRNA because these RNAs are quite heterogeneous in size and are restricted to the nucleus. Primary transcripts contain nonprotein coding intervening sequences that must be removed to form the mature mRNA. Most coding sequences for a single mRNA are interrupted by at least one—and in some cases as many as 50—noncoding intervening sequences termed introns. In most cases, the introns are much longer than the coding regions termed exons. The processing of the primary transcript involves precise removal of introns and splicing of adjacent exons. The function of the intervening sequences, or introns, is not totally clear. However, mRNA precursor molecules can be differentially spliced, thereby increasing the number of distinct proteins produced by a single gene. Introns may also serve to separate functional domains of coding information in a form that permits genetic rearrangement by recombination to occur more rapidly. Such an enhanced rate of genetic rearrangement might allow more rapid evolution of biologic function. In some instances, other protein-coding or noncoding RNAs are localized within the intronic DNA of certain genes. # THE EXACT FUNCTION OF MUCH OF THE MAMMALIAN GENOME IS NOT WELL UNDERSTOOD

The haploid genome of each human cell consists of 3 × 10º bp of DNA subdivided into 23 chromosomes. The entire haploid genome contains sufficient DNA to code for nearly 1.5 million average-sized protein coding genes. Early studies of mutation rates and complexities of higher organisms' genomes suggested that humans have fewer than 100,000 proteins. About 1% of the human genome is composed of exonic DNA. Current estimates based on sequencing of the human genome and the collection of mRNA species suggest there are about 25,000 protein-coding genes in humans. Most genomic DNA is nonprotein coding. Some of the excess DNA sequences regulate gene expression during development, differentiation, and adaptation to the environment. Some excess DNA makes up introns that split the coding regions of genes. Another portion of the excess DNA is composed of many families of repeated sequences, for which clear functions have yet to be defined. Some small RNAs transcribed from these repeats can modulate transcription by interacting with the transcription machinery or by affecting the activity of the chromatin template. The ENCODE Project Consortium has shown that most of the genomic sequence is transcribed in at least some human cell types, albeit at a low level. A large fraction of such transcription appears to generate lncRNAs. Further research will elucidate the physiological roles played by such transcripts.

The DNA in the eukaryotic genome can be divided into two broad 'sequence classes'. The two broad 'sequence classes' of the eukaryotic genome are unique-sequence DNA and repetitive-sequence DNA. Unique-sequence DNA generally includes single copy genes that code for proteins. Repetitive DNA includes sequences that vary in copy number from 2 to as many as 10 copies per cell.

## More Than Half the DNA in Eukaryotic Organisms Is in Unique or Nonrepetitive Sequences

In brewers' yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), about two-thirds of its 6200 genes are expressed. In brewers' yeast, approximately one-fifth of its 6200 genes are required for viability under laboratory growth conditions. In typical tissues of higher eukaryotes, such as mammalian liver and kidney, between 10,000 and 15,000 genes are actively expressed. Different combinations of genes are expressed in each tissue of higher eukaryotes. The mechanism of how different combinations of genes are expressed in each tissue remains one of the major unanswered questions in biology.

## In Human DNA, at Least 30% of the Genome Consists of Repetitive Sequences

Repetitive-sequence DNA can be classified as moderately repetitive. Repetitive-sequence DNA can be classified as highly repetitive. Highly repetitive sequences consist of 5 to 500 base pair lengths repeated many times in tandem. Highly repetitive sequences are often clustered in centromeres and telomeres of the chromosome. Some highly repetitive sequences are present in about 1 to 10 million copies per haploid genome. The majority of these sequences are transcriptionally inactive. Some highly repetitive sequences play a structural role in the chromosome.

Moderately repetitive sequences are defined as being present in numbers of less than 10¶ copies per haploid genome. Moderately repetitive sequences are not clustered but are interspersed with unique sequences. Long interspersed repeats are often transcribed by RNA polymerase II. The RNAs produced from long interspersed repeats contain 5'-Cap structures indistinguishable from those on mRNA.

Moderately repetitive sequences are classified as long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs). Moderately repetitive sequences are classified as short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs). Both LINEs and SINEs are retroposons. LINEs and SINEs arose from movement through an RNA intermediate by the action of reverse transcriptase. Mammalian genomes contain 20,000 to 50,000 copies of the 6 to 7 kbp LINEs. LINEs represent species-specific families of repeat elements. SINEs are shorter, ranging from 70 to 300 bp. There may be more than 100,000 copies of SINEs per genome.

In the human genome, the Alu family of SINEs is present in about 500,000 copies per haploid genome. The Alu family accounts for approximately 10% of the human genome. Members of the human Alu family can be transcribed as integral components of mRNA precursors. Members of the human Alu family can be transcribed as discrete RNA molecules. Closely related analogs of the human Alu family in other animals can be transcribed as integral components of mRNA precursors. Closely related analogs of the human Alu family in other animals can be transcribed as discrete RNA molecules. Closely related analogs of the human Alu family in other animals include the well-studied 4.5S RNA and 7S RNA.

Alu family members are highly conserved within a species. Alu family members are highly conserved between mammalian species. Short-interspersed repeats, including the Alu family, may be mobile elements capable of jumping into and out of various sites within the genome. Transposition events can have disastrous results. Alu sequences can insert into a gene causing neurofibromatosis. Alu B1 and B2 SINE RNAs regulate mRNA production at the levels of transcription. Alu B1 and B2 SINE RNAs regulate mRNA production at the level of mRNA splicing.

## Microsatellite Repeat Sequences

Microsatellite repeat sequences consist of 2 to 6 base pairs repeated up to 50 times. Microsatellite repeat sequences are commonly found as dinucleotide repeats of AC on one strand and TG on the opposite strand. Other forms of these repeats include CG, AT, and CA. AC repeat sequences occur at 50,000 to 100,000 locations in the genome. The number of repeats at any locus may vary between the two chromosomes, providing heterozygosity in an individual. The number of repeats at any locus is a heritable trait. The ease of detecting these repeats using PCR makes them useful in constructing genetic linkage maps. Most genes are associated with one or more microsatellite markers. Microsatellite markers help assess the relative position of genes on chromosomes. Microsatellite markers also help associate a gene with a disease. PCR or directed DNA sequences methods can rapidly screen a large number of family members for a specific microsatellite polymorphism. The association of a specific polymorphism with a gene in affected family members may provide the first clue about the genetic basis of a disease. The lack of this association in unaffected members may provide the first clue about the genetic basis of a disease.

Trinucleotide sequences that increase in number, known as microsatellite instability, can cause disease. Huntington chorea is associated with the CAG trinucleotide repeat. Myotonic dystrophy is associated with the CTG trinucleotide repeat. Spinobulbar muscular atrophy is associated with the CAG trinucleotide repeat. Kennedy disease is associated with the CAG trinucleotide repeat. Next-generation, high-throughput DNA sequencing technologies have improved the speed, accuracy, and precision of analyzing human genome structure. Many clinical tests now involve targeted genomic DNA sequencing prepared from tissues or serum samples.

# ONE PERCENT OF CELLULAR DNA IS IN MITOCHONDRIA

The majority of the polypeptides in mitochondria, about 54 out of 67, are encoded by nuclear genes. The remaining polypeptides are coded by genes found in mitochondrial (mt) DNA. Human mitochondria contain 2 to 10 copies of a small circular 16,569 bp dsDNA molecule. A small circular 16,569 bp dsDNA molecule makes up approximately 1% of total cellular DNA. Mitochondrial (mt) DNA codes for mt-specific ribosomal and transfer RNAs. Mitochondrial (mt) DNA also codes for 13 proteins that play key roles in the respiratory chain (see Chapter 13).

An important feature of human mitochondrial mtDNA is its transmission by maternal nonmendelian inheritance. All mitochondria are contributed by the ovum during zygote formation. In diseases resulting from mutations of mtDNA, an affected mother would in theory pass the disease to all of her children. However, only her daughters would transmit the trait. In some cases, deletions in mtDNA occur during oogenesis and thus are not inherited from the mother. A number of diseases have been shown to be due to mutations of mtDNA. These include a variety of myopathies, neurologic disorders, and some forms of diabetes mellitus.

The primary function of DNA replication is to provide progeny with the genetic information of the parent. DNA replication must be complete. DNA replication maintains genetic stability within the organism and the species. DNA replication is a complex process involving many cellular functions. DNA replication involves several verification procedures to ensure replication fidelity. About 30 proteins are involved in the replication of the Escherichia coli chromosome. The replication process in eukaryotic organisms is significantly more complex than in Escherichia coli.

In eukaryotic cells, an additional step is required where the dsDNA must reform the chromatin structure, including nucleosomes that existed prior to replication. Although this entire process is not completely understood in eukaryotic cells, replication has been precisely described in prokaryotic cells. ## The Origin of Replication

At the origin of replication, sequence-specific dsDNA-binding proteins associate with direct repeat DNA sequences. In E. coli, the origin of DNA synthesis is termed oriC. The protein dnaA binds to oriC in E. coli. The complex formed by dnaA at oriC consists of 150 to 250 bp of DNA. This complex includes multimers of the single-strand DNA-binding protein. The binding of dnaA leads to the local denaturation and unwinding of an adjacent A+T-rich region of DNA.

Functionally similar autonomously replicating sequences (ARS) or replicators have been identified in yeast cells. The ARS in yeast contains a somewhat degenerate 11-bp sequence called the origin replication element (ORE). The ORE binds a set of proteins analogous to the dnaA protein of E. coli. This group of proteins is collectively called the origin recognition complex (ORC). ORC homologs have been found in all eukaryotes examined. The ORE is located adjacent to an approximately 80-bp A+T-rich sequence that is easy to unwind. This sequence is called the DNA unwinding element (DUE). The DUE is the origin of replication in yeast. The DUE is bound by the MCM protein complex.

Consensus sequences similar in structure to oriC or ARS have not been precisely defined in mammalian cells. Several proteins that participate in ori recognition and function have been identified in human cells. Several proteins that participate in ori recognition and function appear quite similar to their yeast counterparts in both amino acid sequence and function. This similarity suggests that ORE-like elements exist in humans at the functional level.

## Formation of the Replication Fork

A replication fork consists of four components: DNA helicase, SSBs, primase, and DNA polymerase. DNA helicase unwinds a short segment of the parental duplex DNA. SSBs bind to single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) to prevent premature reannealing into double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). Primase initiates the synthesis of an RNA molecule essential for priming DNA synthesis. DNA polymerase initiates nascent daughter-strand synthesis on the free 3'-OH of the RNA primer generated by primase.

The DNA polymerase III enzyme, the product of the dnaE gene in E. coli, binds to template DNA as part of a multiprotein complex. This complex includes several polymerase accessory factors: β', γ, δ, δ', and τ. DNA polymerases synthesize DNA in the 5' to 3' direction. Only one type of polymerase is involved at the replication fork. The DNA strands are antiparallel. The polymerase functions asymmetrically on the leading and lagging strands.

On the leading strand, DNA is synthesized continuously. On the lagging strand, DNA is synthesized in short fragments called Okazaki fragments. Okazaki fragments are typically 1-5 kilobases in length. Several Okazaki fragments, up to 1000, must be sequentially synthesized for each replication fork. Helicase unwinds dsDNA in a 5' to 3' direction on the lagging strand. Helicase associates with primase to provide proper access to the template. The association of helicase and primase allows the RNA primer to be made, enabling the polymerase to begin replicating the DNA. DNA polymerases cannot initiate DNA synthesis de novo. The complex of helicase and primase is called a primosome.

As the synthesis of an Okazaki fragment is completed and the polymerase is released, a new primer has been synthesized. The same polymerase molecule remains associated with the replication fork and proceeds to synthesize the next Okazaki fragment.

## The DNA Polymerase Complex

A number of different DNA polymerase molecules engage in DNA replication. A number of different DNA polymerase molecules share the property of chain elongation. A number of different DNA polymerase molecules share the property of processivity. A number of different DNA polymerase molecules share the property of proofreading. Chain elongation accounts for the rate at which polymerization, or phosphodiester bond formation, occurs, measured in nucleotides per second (nt/s). Processivity is an expression of the number of nucleotides added to the nascent chain before the polymerase disengages from the template. The proofreading function identifies copying errors. The proofreading function corrects copying errors.

In E. coli, DNA polymerase III (pol III) functions at the replication fork. Of all polymerases, DNA polymerase III (pol III) catalyzes the highest rate of chain elongation. DNA polymerase III (pol III) is the most processive of all polymerases. DNA polymerase III (pol III) is capable of polymerizing 0.5 Mb of DNA during one cycle on the leading strand. The replication of the circular bacterial chromosome is completed in about 30 minutes. If a mammalian genome replicated at the same rate as bacteria from a single origin, it would take over 150 hours. Metazoan organisms use two strategies to overcome this: replication is bidirectional, and it proceeds from multiple origins in each chromosome. In humans, there can be up to 100 origins. The S phase is separated by the G1 and G2 phases. The G1 phase occurs before the S phase. The G2 phase occurs after the S phase. The G1 phase prepares the cell for DNA synthesis. The G2 phase prepares the cell for mitosis. DNA synthesis is regulated to occur only once per cell cycle. DNA synthesis occurs specifically during the S phase.

Replication licensing involves the dissociation and phosphorylation of origin binding proteins. Replication origins fire only once per cell cycle. Chromosomes replicate simultaneously within a fixed portion of the S phase. The regulation of chromosome replication is an intrinsic property of each chromosome.

Cell cycle checkpoints monitor DNA, chromosome, and chromosome segregation integrity throughout the cell cycle. If defects are detected in G1 or G2 phases, cells halt progression until the issues are resolved. If damage cannot be repaired, cells may undergo apoptosis. ## All Organisms Contain Elaborate Evolutionarily Conserved Mechanisms to Repair Damaged DNA

Repair of damaged DNA is critical for maintaining genomic integrity and thereby preventing the propagation of mutations, either horizontally (somatic cells), or vertically (germ cells). DNA is subjected to a huge array of chemical, physical, and biologic assaults on a daily basis (Table 35-7).

Eukaryotic cells contain five major DNA repair pathways. These pathways include nucleotide excision repair (NER), mismatch repair (MMR), base excision repair (BER), homologous recombination (HR), and nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) repair pathways (Figure 35-20).

DNA repair proteins typically have orthologues in prokaryotes. Eukaryotic cells contain five major DNA repair pathways contain multiple, sometimes shared proteins.

Mutations in a large number of DNA repair genes lead to human disease (Table 35-8). Systematic gene-directed 'knockout' experiments with laboratory mice and cells in culture have ascribed critical genome integrity maintenance functions to these genes. Targeted mutations within these genes induce defects in DNA repair. Mutations in DNA repair genes often dramatically increase susceptibility to cancer.

## Types of Damage to DNA

Single-base alteration includes depurination. Single-base alteration includes deamination of cytosine to uracil. Single-base alteration includes deamination of adenine to hypoxanthine. Single-base alteration includes alkylation of base. Single-base alteration includes base-analog incorporation.

Two-base alteration involves UV light-induced thymine-thymine (pyrimidine) dimer. Two-base alteration involves bifunctional alkylating agent cross-linkage.

Chain breaks can be caused by ionizing radiation. Chain breaks can be caused by radioactive disintegration of backbone element. Chain breaks can be caused by oxidative free-radical formation.

Cross-linkage can occur between bases in the same strand. Cross-linkage can occur between bases in the opposite strands. Cross-linkage can occur between DNA and protein molecules, such as histones.

DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are highly mutagenic. DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are repaired by two pathways: homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ).

The choice between HR and NHEJ depends on the phase of the cell cycle. The choice between HR and NHEJ depends on the type of DSBs to be repaired.

Evolutionarily conserved molecules catalyze all steps of DNA damage repair. DNA damage sensors are part of the evolutionarily conserved molecules. Transducers are part of the evolutionarily conserved molecules. Damage repair mediators are part of the evolutionarily conserved molecules.

The cellular outcomes of DNA damage range from cell-cycle delay to allow for DNA repair. The cellular outcomes of DNA damage include cell-cycle arrest. The cellular outcomes of DNA damage include apoptosis or senescence.

Recognition of damage-specific histone modifications, such as dimethylated lysine 20 histone H4 (H4K20me2), is involved in the DNA damage response. Incorporation of histone isotype variants like the histone variant H2AX is involved in the DNA damage response.

Poly ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) is a key molecule in the DNA damage response. The MRN protein complex, consisting of Mre11-Rad50-NBS1 subunits, is a key molecule in the DNA damage response. DNA damage-activated kinases, including ATM, ATR, DNA-PK, and checkpoint kinases CHK1, CHK2, are key molecules in the DNA damage response.

DNA damage-activated kinases, including ATM, ATR, DNA-PK, and checkpoint kinases CHK1, CHK2, phosphorylate numerous proteins involved in DNA repair, checkpoint control, and cell-cycle control. DNA damage-activated kinases, including ATM, ATR, DNA-PK, and checkpoint kinases CHK1, CHK2, modulate the activities of proteins such as CDC25A, B, C, Wee1, p21, p16, and p19.

Exonucleases are involved in DNA repair processes. Endonucleases are involved in DNA repair processes. DNA single-strand-specific DNA-binding proteins (RPA) are involved in DNA repair processes. PCNA is involved in DNA repair processes. Specific DNA polymerases (DNA pol δ and n) are involved in DNA repair processes.

DNA repair pathways are crucial in cell biology. DNA repair pathways have potential implications in cancer generation and prevention.

## DNA & Chromosome Integrity Is Monitored Throughout the Cell Cycle

Eukaryotic cells have developed mechanisms to monitor the integrity of DNA and chromosomes. Complex enzyme systems have evolved to repair damaged DNA at the nucleotide level. DNA mishaps at the chromosome level are also monitored and repaired. DNA and chromosomal integrity are continuously monitored throughout the cell cycle. Checkpoint controls are the specific steps where this monitoring occurs. If problems are detected at any of these checkpoints, the cell cycle progression is interrupted until the damage is repaired.

P53 is a DNA-binding transcription factor that is stabilized in response to DNA damage. P53 is subject to a variety of regulatory post-translational modifications. Increased levels of p53 activate the transcription of genes that delay transit through the cell cycle. One of these induced proteins is p21, which is a potent CDK-cyclin inhibitor. Inhibition of CDKs halts progression through the cell cycle. If DNA damage is too extensive to repair, cells undergo apoptosis in a p53-dependent manner. P53 induces the activation of genes that promote apoptosis. Cells lacking functional p53 fail to undergo apoptosis in response to high levels of radiation or DNA-active chemotherapeutic agents. P53 is one of the most frequently mutated genes in human cancers. Over 80% of human cancers carry p53 loss-of-function mutations. Elephants, which have 20 copies of the p53-encoding gene, are highly resistant to developing cancer. Additional research into checkpoint control and genome maintenance will be valuable for developing anticancer therapies.

DNA in eukaryotic cells is associated with a variety of proteins, resulting in a structure termed chromatin.
Much of the DNA is associated with histone proteins to form a structure called the nucleosome. Nucleosomes are composed of an octamer of histones around which about 150 bp of DNA is wrapped.
Histones are subject to an extensive array of dynamic covalent modifications that have important regulatory consequences—both positive and negative.
Nucleosomes and higher-order structures formed therefrom serve to compact the DNA.
DNA in transcriptionally active regions is relatively more sensitive to nuclease attack in vitro. This property can be used as a proxy for a relative increase in DNA accessibility; some such genomic regions, so-called hypersensitive sites are exceptionally sensitive, and such DNA regions are often found to contain transcription control sites.
Highly transcriptionally active DNA (genes) is often clustered in regions of each chromosome. Within these regions, genes may be separated by inactive DNA in nucleosomal structures. In many eukaryotic transcription units (ie, the portion of a gene that is copied by DNA-dependent RNA polymerase) often consists of protein-coding regions of DNA (exons) interrupted by intervening seguences of nonprotein-coding DNA (introns) This is particularly true for mRNA-encoding genes.
After transcription, during RNA processing, introns are removed and the exons are ligated together to form the mature RNAs that appear in the cytoplasm; this process is termed RNA splicing.
DNA in each chromosome is exactly replicated according to the rules of base pairing during the S phase of the cell cycle.
Each strand of the double helix is replicated simultaneously but by somewhat different mechanisms. A complex of proteins, including DNA polymerase, replicates the leading strand continuously in the 5' to 3' direction. The lagging strand is replicated discontinuously, in short pieces of 100 to 250 nucleotides by DNA polymerase synthesizing in the 5' → 3' direction.
•DNA replication is initiated at special sites termed origins to generate replication bubbles. Each eukaryotic chromosome contains multiple origins The entire process takes about 9 hours in a typical human cell and only occurs once, and only once, during the S phase of the cell cycle.
A variety of mechanisms that employ different enzyme systems repair damaged cellular DNA after exposure of cells to chemical and physical mutagens.
Normal cells containing DNA that cannot be repaired either undergo senescence or programmed cell death via apoptosis.
