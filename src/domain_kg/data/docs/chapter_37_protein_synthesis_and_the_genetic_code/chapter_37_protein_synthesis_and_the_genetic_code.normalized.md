# Protein Synthesis & the Genetic Code

Understand that the genetic code is a three-letter nucleotide code (composed of triplets of A, G, C, and T) within the linear array of the exons of DNA of the protein coding genes. Further, that once transcribed into mRNA, this three-letter code, now composed of triplets of A, G, C, and U, specifies the linear order of amino acid addition during protein synthesis via the process of translation.
Appreciate that the universal genetic code is degenerate, unambiguous, nonoverlapping, and punctuation free.
Explain that the genetic code is composed of 64 codons, 61 of which encode amino acids while 3 specific codons induce the termination of protein synthesis.
Describe how the transfer RNAs (tRNAs) serve as the ultimate informational agents that decode the genetic code of mRNAs.
Understand the mechanism of the highly energy-intensive process of protein synthesis that involves the steps of initiation, elongation, and termination, and occurs on RNA-protein complexes termed ribosomes.
Appreciate that protein synthesis, like DNA replication and transcription, is precisely controlled through the action of multiple accessory factors that are responsive to multiple extra- and intracellular regulatory signaling inputs.

# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

The letters A, G, T, and C correspond to the nucleotides found in DNA. Within the protein-coding genes, these nucleotides are organized into three-letter code words called codons. The collection of these codons, once transcribed into mRNA, makes up the genetic code. The deciphering of the genetic code was essential to understand protein synthesis. The deciphering of the genetic code was necessary to explain the molecular effects of gene mutations. The genetic code provides a foundation for explaining how protein defects may cause genetic disease. The genetic code aids in the diagnosis and possible treatment of genetic disorders. The pathophysiology of many viral infections is related to these agents' ability to disrupt host cell protein synthesis. The pathophysiology of many viral infections is related to these agents' ability to co-opt host cell protein synthesis. Many antibacterial drugs are effective because they selectively disrupt protein synthesis in bacterial cells. Many antibacterial drugs do not affect protein synthesis in eukaryotic cells.

# GENETIC INFORMATION FLOWS FROM DNA TO RNA TO PROTEIN

The genetic information within the nucleotide sequence of DNA is transcribed in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells into the specific nucleotide sequence of an mRNA molecule. The sequence of nucleotides in the RNA transcript is complementary to the nucleotide sequence of the template strand of its gene in accordance with Watson-Crick base-pairing rules. Several different classes of RNA combine to direct the synthesis of proteins.

In prokaryotes there is a linear correspondence between the gene and the messenger RNA transcribed from the gene. In prokaryotes there is a linear correspondence between the messenger RNA and the polypeptide product. In higher eukaryotic cells, the primary transcript is much larger than the mature mRNA. The large mRNA precursors contain coding regions (exons) that will form the mature mRNA. The large mRNA precursors contain long intervening sequences (introns) that separate the exons. The mRNA is processed within the nucleus. The introns are removed from the mRNA during processing. The introns typically make up much more of the RNA than the exons. Exons are spliced together to form mature mRNAs. Mature mRNAs are transported to the cytoplasm. Mature mRNAs are translated into protein in the cytoplasm.

The cell must possess the machinery necessary to translate information accurately and efficiently from the nucleotide sequence of an mRNA into the sequence of amino acids of the corresponding specific protein. The process of translating the information in the mRNA nucleotide sequence into the amino acid sequence of a protein is termed translation. The deciphering of the genetic code was necessary for understanding this process. mRNA molecules have no affinity for amino acids. An intermediate adapter molecule is required for the translation of the information in the mRNA nucleotide sequence into the amino acid sequence of a protein. This adapter molecule must recognize a specific nucleotide sequence and a specific amino acid. With such an adapter molecule, the cell can direct a specific amino acid into the proper sequential position of a protein during its synthesis as dictated by the nucleotide sequence of the specific mRNA. The functional groups of the amino acids do not actually come into contact with the mRNA template.

# THE GENETIC CODE IS DEGENERATE, UNAMBIGUOUS, NONOVERLAPPING, WITHOUT PUNCTUATION, & UNIVERSAL

Three of the 64 possible codons do not code for specific amino acids. Three of the 64 possible codons are termed nonsense codons. Nonsense codons are utilized in the cell as translation termination signals. The remaining 61 codons code for the 20 naturally occurring amino acids. There is degeneracy in the genetic code, meaning multiple codons can decode the same amino acid. Some amino acids are encoded by several codons. For example, six different codons specify serine. Other amino acids, such as methionine and tryptophan, have a single codon. In general, the third nucleotide in a codon is less important than the first two in determining the specific amino acid to be incorporated. The third nucleotide in a codon accounts for most of the degeneracy of the code. However, for any specific codon, only a single amino acid is specified. The genetic code is unambiguous, meaning given a specific codon, only a single amino acid is indicated. The distinction between ambiguity and degeneracy is an important concept.

The recognition of specific codons in the mRNA by the tRNA adapter molecules is dependent on the tRNA anticodon region. Specific base-pairing rules dictate tRNA–mRNA codon binding. Each tRNA molecule contains a specific sequence, complementary to a codon, termed its anticodon. For a given codon in the mRNA, only a single species of tRNA molecule possesses the proper anticodon. Each tRNA molecule can be charged with only one specific amino acid. Each codon therefore specifies only one amino acid. Some tRNA molecules can recognize more than one codon. With few exceptions, given a specific codon, only a specific amino acid will be incorporated. Given a specific amino acid, more than one codon may be used.

The reading of the genetic code during the process of protein synthesis does not involve any overlap of codons. The genetic code is nonoverlapping. Once the reading is commenced at a specific start codon, there is no punctuation between codons. The message is read in a continuing sequence of nucleotide triplets until a translation stop codon is reached.

Until recently, the genetic code was thought to be universal. The set of tRNA molecules in mitochondria reads four codons differently from the tRNA molecules in the cytoplasm of even the same cells. In mammalian mitochondria, the codon AUA is read as Met. In mammalian mitochondria, UGA codes for Trp. In mitochondria, the codons AGA and AGG are read as stop- or chain-terminator codons rather than as Arg. Mitochondria require only 22 tRNA molecules to read their genetic code. The cytoplasmic translation system possesses a full complement of 31 tRNA species. These exceptions noted, the genetic code is universal.

The frequency of use of each amino acid codon varies considerably between species. The frequency of use of each amino acid codon varies even among different tissues within a species. The specific tRNA levels generally mirror these codon usage biases. A particular abundantly used codon is decoded by a similarly abundant-specific tRNA which recognizes that particular codon. Tables of codon usage are quite accurate now that many genomes have been sequenced and analyzed. Tables of codon usage are vital for large-scale production of proteins for therapeutic purposes, such as insulin and erythropoietin. Currently, many therapeutic proteins are often produced using in vitro synthetic systems. Currently, many therapeutic proteins are often produced through the utilization of cultured nonhuman cells using recombinant DNA technology.

Although the initial change may not occur in the template strand of the double-stranded DNA molecule for that gene, after replication, daughter DNA molecules with mutations in the template strand will segregate and appear in the population of organisms.

## Frameshift Mutations Result From the Deletion or Insertion of Nucleotides in DNA That Generates Altered mRNAs

The deletion of a single nucleotide from the coding strand of a gene results in an altered reading frame in the mRNA. The machinery translating the mRNA does not recognize that a base was missing. The reading of codons has no punctuation. A major alteration in the sequence of polymerized amino acids results from altering the reading frame. The alteration results in a garbled translation of the mRNA distal to the single nucleotide deletion. The sequence of amino acids distal to this deletion is garbled. The reading of the message can also result in the appearance of a nonsense codon. The production of a polypeptide is both garbled and prematurely terminated by the appearance of a nonsense codon.

If three nucleotides or a multiple of three nucleotides are deleted from a coding region, translation of the corresponding mRNA will generate a protein that is missing the corresponding number of amino acids. The reading frame is a triplet, so the reading phase will not be disturbed for those codons distal to the deletion. If deletion of one or two nucleotides occurs just prior to or within the normal termination codon, the reading of the normal termination signal is disturbed. Deletion of one or two nucleotides might result in reading through the now “mutated” termination signal until another nonsense codon is encountered.

# LIKE TRANSCRIPTION, PROTEIN SYNTHESIS CAN BE DESCRIBED IN THREE PHASES: INITIATION, ELONGATION, & TERMINATION

Ribosomes are the machinery on which the mRNA nucleotide sequence is translated into the sequence of amino acids of the specified protein. The translation of the mRNA commences near its 5′ end with the formation of the corresponding amino terminus of the protein molecule. The message is decoded from 5′ to 3′, concluding with the formation of the carboxyl terminus of the protein. The concept of polarity is apparent in this process. The transcription of a gene into the corresponding mRNA or its precursor first forms the 5′ end of the RNA molecule. In prokaryotes, mRNA translation can begin before the transcription of the gene is completed. In eukaryotic organisms, transcription occurs in the nucleus, while mRNA translation occurs in the cytoplasm. In eukaryotic organisms, transcription and translation cannot occur simultaneously, enabling the processing necessary to generate mature mRNA from the primary transcript.

## Initiation Involves Several Protein-RNA Complexes

Initiation of eukaryotic protein synthesis requires an mRNA molecule to be selected for translation by a ribosome. The ribosome must locate the initiation codon on the mRNA to set the correct reading frame, and translation begins. This process involves tRNA, rRNA, mRNA, and at least 10 eukaryotic initiation factors (eIFs), some of which have multiple subunits. GTP, ATP, and amino acids are also involved in the initiation process. Initiation can be divided into three steps, all of which are preceded by the dissociation of the 80S ribosome into its 40S and 60S subunits. The first step is the binding of a ternary complex consisting of the initiator methionyl-tRNA (met-tRNAi), GTP, and eIF-2 to the 40S ribosome subunit to form the 43S preinitiation complex. The second step involves the binding of mRNA to the 40S preinitiation complex to form the 48S initiation complex. The third step is the combination of the 48S initiation complex with the 60S ribosomal subunit to form the 80S initiation complex.

Eukaryotic mRNAs contain a 5′ 7meG-cap (Cap) and a 3′ poly(A) tail. Translation preinitiation complex formation proceeds in several steps. The first step is the dissociation of the 80S complex into component 40S and 60S subunits, facilitated by the binding of factors eIF1, eIF1A, and eIF3 to the ribosomal 40S subunit. The second step is the formation of the 43S preinitiation complex, a ternary complex consisting of met-tRNA and GTP-bound to the initiation factor eIF-2. The first step of translation initiation involves the binding of GTP by eIF-2. The GTP-eIF-2 complex then binds to methionyl tRNAi. Methionyl tRNAi is a tRNA specifically involved in binding to the initiation codon AUG. There are two tRNAs for methionine: one specifies methionine for the initiator codon (met tRNAi), and the other for internal methionines (met tRNA). The GTP-eIF-2-tRNAi ternary complex binds to the 40S ribosomal subunit to form the 43S preinitiation complex. The ternary complex–40S subunit complex is stabilized by eIF-3 and eIF-1A. The subsequent binding of eIF5 stabilizes the ternary complex–40S subunit complex.

The eIF-4F complex consists of eIF-4E and the eIF-4G-eIF-4A complex. eIF-4B binds to the complex and reduces the secondary structure of the 5' end of the mRNA through its ATP-dependent helicase activity. ## Termination Occurs When a Stop Codon Is Recognized

RF1 is bound by a complex consisting of releasing factor 3 (RF3) with bound GTP. Therefore, the releasing factors are proteins that hydrolyze the peptidyl-tRNA bond when a stop codon occupies the A site. The mRNA is then released from the ribosome, which dissociates into its component 40S and 60S subunits, and another cycle can be repeated. ## Polysomes Are Assemblies of Ribosomes

Multiple ribosomes on the same mRNA molecule form a polyribosome, or 'polysome'. # POSTTRANSLATIONAL PROCESSING AFFECTS THE ACTIVITY OF MANY PROTEINS

Some animal viruses, such as HIV, poliovirus, and hepatitis virus, synthesize long polycistronic proteins from one long mRNA molecule. The viral protein molecules translated from these long mRNAs are cleaved at defined sites. The cleavage of viral protein molecules provides several specific viral proteins required for viral function. In animal cells, many cellular proteins are synthesized from the mRNA template as a precursor molecule. The precursor molecule must be modified to achieve the active protein. Insulin is a prototype of such proteins. Insulin is a small protein with two polypeptide chains connected by interchain and intrachain disulfide bridges. Insulin is synthesized as a single chain precursor, or prohormone. The prohormone folds to allow the formation of specific disulfide bridges. A specific protease removes the segment connecting the two chains to form the functional insulin molecule.

Many other peptides are synthesized as precursor proproteins that require modifications before attaining biologic activity. Posttranslational modifications often involve the removal of amino terminal amino acid residues by specific aminopeptidases. Collagen is an abundant protein in the extracellular spaces of higher eukaryotes. Collagen is synthesized as procollagen. Three procollagen polypeptide molecules, which are not always identical in sequence, align in a particular way dependent on specific amino terminal peptides. Specific enzymes hydroxylate and oxidize specific amino acid residues within the procollagen molecules to provide cross-links for greater stability. Amino terminal peptides are cleaved off the molecule to form the final product, a strong, insoluble collagen molecule.

Covalent modification by acetylation is common. Covalent modification by phosphorylation is common. Covalent modification by methylation is common. Covalent modification by ubiquitylation is common. Covalent modification by glycosylation is common. Acetylation, phosphorylation, methylation, ubiquitylation, and glycosylation are part of the broader category of posttranslational processing. Posttranslational processing affects the activity of many proteins.
