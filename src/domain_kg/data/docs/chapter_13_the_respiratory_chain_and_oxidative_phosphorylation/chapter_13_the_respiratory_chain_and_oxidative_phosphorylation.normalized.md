# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Aerobic organisms capture a greater proportion of the available free energy of respiratory substrates than anaerobic organisms. Most of this energy capture takes place inside mitochondria. Mitochondria are termed the 'powerhouses' of the cell. Respiration is coupled to the generation of the high-energy intermediate, ATP, by oxidative phosphorylation.

A number of drugs, such as amobarbital, inhibit oxidative phosphorylation. Poisons, such as cyanide and carbon monoxide, also inhibit oxidative phosphorylation. Inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation usually has fatal consequences.

Several inherited defects of mitochondria involve components of the respiratory chain and oxidative phosphorylation. Patients with these defects present with myopathy and encephalopathy. Patients with these defects often have lactic acidosis.

## Components of the Respiratory Chain Are Contained in Four Large Protein Complexes Embedded in the Inner Mitochondrial Membrane

Electrons pass through three large protein complexes in the respiratory chain: NADH-Q oxidoreductase (Complex I), Q-cytochrome c oxidoreductase (Complex III), and cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV). Complex I transfers electrons from NADH to coenzyme Q (Q), also known as ubiquinone. Complex III passes electrons to cytochrome c.

The four complexes are embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Coenzyme Q (Q) and cytochrome c are mobile components. Q diffuses rapidly within the membrane, while cytochrome c is a soluble protein. (Figure 13–3)

## Molecular Oxygen Is Reduced to Water via Complex IV

Reduced cytochrome c is oxidized by Complex IV, also known as cytochrome c oxidase. The products are 4 oxidized cytochrome c molecules, 2 water molecules, and 4 protons pumped into the intermembrane space. Eight protons are removed from the matrix during this reaction. Four protons are used to form water, and four are pumped into the intermembrane space. This binding minimizes the release of potentially damaging intermediates such as superoxide anions or peroxide.

# THE RESPIRATORY CHAIN CREATES A PROTON GRADIENT THAT DRIVES THE SYNTHESIS OF ATP

The flow of electrons through the respiratory chain generates ATP by the process of oxidative phosphorylation. The chemiosmotic theory, proposed by Peter Mitchell in 1961, explains how these processes are coupled. The inner mitochondrial membrane is impermeable to ions, particularly protons. As electrons pass through complexes I, III, and IV, a proton gradient builds across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The proton gradient creates an electrochemical potential difference, known as the proton motive force, which is negative on the matrix side. The proton motive force drives the mechanism of ATP synthesis. Complexes I, III, and IV act as proton pumps to create this proton gradient across the membrane. The proton motive force generated drives the synthesis of ATP as protons flow back into the matrix through the ATP synthase enzyme (Figure 13-6).

## An ATP Synthase Functions as a Rotary Motor to Form ATP

ATP synthase is embedded in the inner membrane, together with the respiratory chain complexes.

The process of ATP formation by the ATP synthase is thought to occur by a binding change mechanism in which the conformation of the \beta subunits in F_1 is changed as the axis rotates from one that binds ATP tightly to one that releases ATP and binds ADP and P_i so that the next ATP can be formed. The F_0 subcomplex of the enzyme is a disk of "C" protein subunits attached to a \gamma subunit in the form of a "bent axle," which fits inside the F_1 subcomplex of three \alpha and three \beta subunits. Protons passing through the disk cause it and the \gamma subunit, but not the F_1 component, to rotate. ADP and P_i are taken up sequentially by the \beta subunits to form ATP, which is expelled as the rotating \gamma subunit squeezes each \beta subunit in turn and changes its conformation. Thus, three ATP molecules are generated per revolution (Figure 13-7).

# THE RESPIRATORY CHAIN PROVIDES MOST OF THE ENERGY CAPTURED DURING CATABOLISM

ADP captures a significant proportion of the free energy released by catabolic processes in the form of high-energy phosphate. There is a net direct capture of two high-energy phosphate groups in the glycolytic reactions. Two more high-energy phosphates per mole of glucose are captured in the citric acid cycle during the conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate. All of these phosphorylations occur at the substrate level.

When 1 mol of substrate, such as succinate or 3-phosphoglycerate, is oxidized via Complexes II, III, and IV, only 1.5 mol of ATP are formed, meaning the P:O ratio is 1.5. Oxidative phosphorylation at the respiratory chain level is known as oxidative phosphorylation at the respiratory chain level.

Nearly 90% of the high-energy phosphates produced from the complete oxidation of 1 mol glucose is obtained via oxidative phosphorylation coupled to the respiratory chain.

## Respiratory Control Ensures a Constant Supply of ATP

The rate of respiration of mitochondria can be controlled by the availability of ADP. Oxidation and phosphorylation are tightly coupled. Oxidation cannot proceed via the respiratory chain without concomitant phosphorylation of ADP. Five conditions control the rate of respiration in mitochondria. The availability of ADP and substrate is known as state 1. The availability of substrate only is known as state 2. The capacity of the respiratory chain when all substrates and components are present in saturating amounts is known as state 3. The availability of ADP only is known as state 4. The availability of oxygen only is known as state 5. Most cells in the resting state are in state 4. Respiration in most cells is controlled by the availability of ADP. When work is performed, ATP is converted to ADP. More respiration occurs when ATP is converted to ADP. More respiration allows the replenishment of the store of ATP. As respiration increases, the cell approaches states 3 or 5. The capacity of the respiratory chain becomes saturated as respiration increases. The ADP/ATP transporter facilitates entry of cytosolic ADP into and ATP out of the mitochondrion. The ADP/ATP transporter can become rate limiting.

Biologic oxidative processes allow free energy to become available and captured in a stepwise, efficient, and controlled manner. Nonbiologic processes are explosive, inefficient, and uncontrolled. The remaining free energy that is not captured as high-energy phosphate is liberated as heat. Liberated heat need not be considered wasted. Liberated heat ensures that the respiratory system as a whole is sufficiently exergonic to be removed from equilibrium. Liberated heat allows continuous unidirectional flow and constant provision of ATP. Liberated heat contributes to the maintenance of body temperature.

# THE CHEMIOSMOTIC THEORY EXPLAINS RESPIRATORY CONTROL AND THE ACTION OF UNCOUPLERS

The electrochemical potential difference across the membrane inhibits further transport of reducing equivalents through the respiratory chain unless discharged by back-translocation of protons across the membrane through the ATP synthase. Uncouplers such as dinitrophenol are amphipathic and increase the permeability of the lipoid inner mitochondrial membrane to protons. Physiologic uncouplers such as UCP1 are membrane-spanning proteins that have a similar effect. Uncouplers reduce the electrochemical potential and short-circuit the ATP synthase. In these circumstances, oxidation can proceed without phosphorylation.

# EXCHANGE TRANSPORTERS ARE REQUIRED IN THE INNER MITOCHONDRIAL MEMBRANE

Exchange diffusion systems involving transporter proteins that span the membrane are necessary for the uptake and output of ionized metabolites while preserving electrical and osmotic equilibrium. Long-chain fatty acids are transported into mitochondria via the carnitine system (see Figure 22–1). Dicarboxylate and tricarboxylate anions, such as malate and citrate, require specific transporter or carrier systems to facilitate their passage across the membrane. Amino acids require specific transporter or carrier systems to facilitate their passage across the membrane.

The transport of di- and tricarboxylate anions is closely linked to that of inorganic phosphate. The net uptake of malate by the dicarboxylate transporter requires inorganic phosphate for exchange in the opposite direction. The net uptake of citrate, isocitrate, or cis-aconitate by the tricarboxylate transporter requires malate in exchange. The adenine nucleotide transporter allows the exchange of ATP and ADP, but not AMP. The adenine nucleotide transporter is vital for ATP to exit from mitochondria to the sites of extramitochondrial utilization. The adenine nucleotide transporter is vital for the return of ADP for ATP production within the mitochondrion (Figure 13–10). In this translocation, four negative charges are removed from the matrix for every three taken in. The electrochemical gradient across the membrane (the proton motive force) favors the export of ATP.

## Oxidation of Extramitochondrial NADH Is Mediated by Substrate Shuttles

NADH is produced continuously in the cytosol during glycolysis by glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase. NADH must enter the mitochondria for utilization via the respiratory chain. NADH cannot cross the mitochondrial membrane freely. There are no NADH transporter systems. The reducing equivalents are transferred by an indirect shuttle mechanism involving substrate pairs. The shuttle mechanism uses suitable dehydrogenases on each side of the membrane. The glycerophosphate shuttle is one mechanism of transfer. The glycerophosphate shuttle is present in some tissues, such as the brain and white muscle. The glycerophosphate shuttle is deficient in other tissues, such as heart muscle. The malate shuttle system is believed to be of more universal utility. The mitochondrial membrane is impermeable to oxaloacetate. The impermeability of the mitochondrial membrane to oxaloacetate is overcome by a transamination reaction with glutamate. Oxaloacetate is reformed in the cytosol. NAD⁺ is generated from NADH outside the mitochondrion via the formation of malate from oxaloacetate. Malate is converted back to oxaloacetate, releasing NADH inside the matrix. Oxaloacetate is reformed by a second transaminase in the cytosol. Reformed oxaloacetate may be used to generate another NAD⁺ from NADH.

# CLINICAL ASPECTS

Fatal infantile mitochondrial myopathy and renal dysfunction involves severe diminution or absence of most oxidoreductases of the respiratory chain.

MELAS, or mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke, is an inherited condition due to NADH-Q oxidoreductase (Complex I) or cytochrome oxidase (Complex IV) deficiency.

MELAS is caused by a mutation in mitochondrial DNA.

MELAS may be involved in Alzheimer disease and diabetes mellitus.

A number of drugs and poisons act by inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation.

These are funneled into the respiratory chain, where they are passed down a redox gradient of carriers to their final reaction with oxygen to form water.
The redox carriers are grouped into four respiratory chain complexes in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Three of the four complexes are able to use the energy released in the redox gradient to pump protons to the outside of the membrane, creating an electrochemical potential between the matrix and the inner membrane space.
In this way, oxidation is closely coupled to phosphorylation to meet the energy needs of the cell.
Many well-known poisons such as cyanide arrest respiration by inhibition of the respiratory chain.
