# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

The pentose phosphate pathway is an alternative route for the metabolism of glucose. The pentose phosphate pathway does not lead to the formation of ATP. The pentose phosphate pathway has two major functions. The pentose phosphate pathway forms nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) for synthesis of fatty acids. The pentose phosphate pathway forms nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) for synthesis of steroids. The pentose phosphate pathway maintains reduced glutathione for antioxidant activity. The pentose phosphate pathway synthesizes ribose for nucleotide and nucleic acid formation. Glucose, fructose, and galactose are the main hexoses absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. Glucose is derived from dietary starch. Fructose is derived from sucrose. Galactose is derived from lactose. Fructose can be converted to glucose, mainly in the liver. Galactose can be converted to glucose, mainly in the liver.

Genetic deficiency of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase causes acute hemolysis of red blood cells. Acute hemolysis of red blood cells results in hemolytic anemia. Glucuronic acid is synthesized from glucose via the uronic acid pathway. The uronic acid pathway is of minor quantitative importance. The uronic acid pathway is of major significance for the conjugation and excretion of metabolites and foreign chemicals as glucuronides. Deficiency in the pathway leads to the condition of essential pentosuria. Lack of one enzyme of the pathway, gulonolactone oxidase, in primates and some other animals explains why ascorbic acid is a dietary requirement for human beings. Ascorbic acid is not a dietary requirement for most other mammals. Deficiencies in the enzymes of fructose and galactose metabolism lead to metabolic diseases. Essential fructosuria is a metabolic disease. Hereditary fructose intolerance is a metabolic disease. Galactosemia is a metabolic disease.

# THE PENTOSE PHOSPHATE PATHWAY FORMS NADPH & RIBOSE PHOSPHATE

The pentose phosphate pathway, also known as the hexose monophosphate shunt, is more complex than glycolysis. Three molecules of glucose-6-phosphate produce three molecules of CO and three five-carbon sugars. Three molecules of glucose-6-phosphate are rearranged to regenerate two molecules of glucose-6-phosphate and one molecule of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. Two molecules of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate can regenerate glucose-6-phosphate. The pentose phosphate pathway can account for the complete oxidation of glucose. The pentose phosphate pathway consists of three interconnected cycles where glucose-6-phosphate is both the substrate and end product (Figure 20-1). Reactions above the broken line in the pathway are nonreversible, while those below are freely reversible, except for the reaction catalyzed by fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase. The pentose phosphate pathway is related to glycolysis (Figure 20-1).

# REACTIONS OF THE PENTOSE PHOSPHATE PATHWAY OCCUR IN THE CYTOSOL

The enzymes of the pentose phosphate pathway are located in the cytosol. The pentose phosphate pathway uses NADP^+ as the hydrogen acceptor for oxidation. The pentose phosphate pathway is divided into two phases: an irreversible oxidative phase and a reversible nonoxidative phase. In the oxidative phase, glucose-6-phosphate undergoes dehydrogenation and decarboxylation. The oxidative phase yields ribulose-5-phosphate, a pentose. In the nonoxidative phase, ribulose-5-phosphate is converted back to glucose-6-phosphate. The nonoxidative phase involves reactions catalyzed by transketolase and transaldolase.

## The Oxidative Phase Generates NADPH

Dehydrogenation of glucose-6-phosphate to 6-phosphogluconate occurs via the formation of 6-phosphogluconolactone, catalyzed by glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, an NADP-dependent enzyme. The hydrolysis of 6-phosphogluconolactone is accomplished by the enzyme gluconolactone hydrolase. Decarboxylation forms the ketopentose ribulose-5-phosphate.

In the endoplasmic reticulum, an isoenzyme of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, hexose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, provides NADPH for hydroxylation reactions. Hexose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase also provides NADPH for 11-β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-1. 11-β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-1 catalyzes the reduction of (inactive) cortisone to (active) cortisol in the liver, the nervous system, and adipose tissue. 11-β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-1 is the major source of intracellular cortisol in these tissues and may be important in obesity and the metabolic syndrome.

## The Nonoxidative Phase Generates Ribose Precursors

Ribulose-5-phosphate 3-epimerase alters the configuration about carbon 3 of ribulose-5-phosphate, forming the epimer xylulose 5-phosphate. Ribulose-5-phosphate ketoisomerase converts ribulose-5-phosphate to ribose-5-phosphate, an aldopentose used for nucleotide and nucleic acid synthesis. Transketolase transfers the two-carbon unit comprising carbons 1 and 2 of a ketose onto the aldehyde carbon of an aldose sugar. Transketolase affects the conversion of a ketose sugar into an aldose with two carbons less. Transketolase affects the conversion of an aldose sugar into a ketose with two carbons more. The two-carbon moiety is transferred as glycolaldehyde bound to thiamin diphosphate. Transketolase catalyzes the transfer of the two-carbon unit from xylulose 5-phosphate to ribose-5-phosphate, producing the seven-carbon ketose sedoheptulose-7-phosphate and the aldose glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. Transaldolase catalyzes the transfer of a three-carbon dihydroxyacetone moiety (carbons 1–3) from the ketose sedoheptulose-7-phosphate onto the aldose glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. Transaldolase forms the ketose fructose-6-phosphate and the four-carbon aldose erythrose-4-phosphate. Transaldolase has no cofactor. The reaction catalyzed by transaldolase proceeds via the intermediate formation of a Schiff base of dihydroxyacetone to the ε-amino group of a lysine residue in the enzyme. In a further reaction catalyzed by transketolase, xylulose 5-phosphate serves as a donor of glycolaldehyde. In this case, erythrose-4-phosphate is the acceptor, and the products of the reaction are fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. In tissues that lack this enzyme, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate follows the normal pathway of glycolysis to pyruvate.

## The Two Major Pathways for the Catabolism of Glucose Have Little in Common

Glucose-6-phosphate is common to both the pentose phosphate pathway and glycolysis. The pentose phosphate pathway utilizes NADP+ rather than NAD+. The pentose phosphate pathway produces CO2, which is not produced in glycolysis. No ATP is generated in the pentose phosphate pathway, whereas it is a product of glycolysis.

Xylulose 5-phosphate activates the protein phosphatase that dephosphorylates phosphofructokinase-2. Dephosphorylation of phosphofructokinase-2 activates the kinase and inactivates the phosphatase. Increased formation of fructose 2,6-bisphosphate results from the activation of phosphofructokinase-2. Increased activity of phosphofructokinase-1 leads to increased glycolytic flux. Xylulose 5-phosphate activates the protein phosphatase that initiates the nuclear translocation and DNA binding of the carbohydrate response element-binding protein. Activation of the carbohydrate response element-binding protein leads to increased synthesis of fatty acids in response to a high-carbohydrate diet. Activation of the carbohydrate response element-binding protein couples the demand for NADPH for lipogenesis and activation of the lipogenic enzymatic machinery.

## Reducing Equivalents Are Generated in Those Tissues Specializing in Reductive Syntheses

The pentose phosphate pathway is active in the liver, adipose tissue, adrenal cortex, thyroid, erythrocytes, testis, and lactating mammary gland. The pentose phosphate pathway has low activity in the nonlactating mammary gland and skeletal muscle. NADPH is used in reductive syntheses in tissues where the pentose phosphate pathway is active. NADPH is used for the synthesis of fatty acids in these tissues. NADPH is used for the synthesis of steroids in these tissues. NADPH is used for the synthesis of amino acids via glutamate dehydrogenase in these tissues. NADPH is used for the synthesis of reduced glutathione in these tissues. Insulin induces the synthesis of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase in the fed state. Insulin induces the synthesis of 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase in the fed state. Lipogenesis increases in the fed state. NADPH oxidase in phagocytes and neutrophils uses NADPH to destroy engulfed cells and bacteria using superoxide.

## Ribose Can Be Synthesized in Virtually All Tissues

Ribose does not circulate in the bloodstream.

Tissues must synthesize ribose for nucleotide and nucleic acid synthesis using the pentose phosphate pathway.

A completely functioning pentose phosphate pathway is not necessary for a tissue to synthesize ribose-5-phosphate.

Muscle has low activity of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase and 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase.

Muscle can synthesize ribose-5-phosphate by reversing the nonoxidative phase of the pentose phosphate pathway.

Fructose-6-phosphate is used in the synthesis of ribose-5-phosphate in muscle.

Most tissues are capable of synthesizing ribose-5-phosphate by reversing the nonoxidative phase of the pentose phosphate pathway.

# THE PENTOSE PHOSPHATE PATHWAY & GLUTATHIONE PEROXIDASE PROTECT ERYTHROCYTES AGAINST HEMOLYSIS

In red blood cells, the pentose phosphate pathway is the sole source of NADPH for the reduction of oxidized glutathione catalyzed by glutathione reductase. Glutathione reductase is a flavoprotein containing flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). Glutathione peroxidase is an enzyme that contains the selenium analog of cysteine (selenocysteine) at the active site (Figure 20--3).

In other tissues, NADPH can also be generated by the reaction catalyzed by the malic enzyme.

# GLUCURONATE, A PRECURSOR OF PROTEOGLYCANS & CONJUGATED GLUCURONIDES, IS A PRODUCT OF THE URONIC ACID PATHWAY

In the liver, the uronic acid pathway catalyzes the conversion of glucose to glucuronic acid. In the liver, the uronic acid pathway catalyzes the conversion of glucose to ascorbic acid. In the liver, the uronic acid pathway catalyzes the conversion of glucose to pentoses (Figure 20–4).

Glucose-6-phosphate is isomerized to glucose-1-phosphate. Glucose-1-phosphate reacts with uridine triphosphate (UTP) to form uridine diphosphate glucose (UDPGlc). UDPGlc pyrophosphorylase catalyzes the reaction that forms uridine diphosphate glucose (UDPGlc).

UDPGlc is oxidized at carbon 6 by NAD-dependent UDPGlc dehydrogenase. The oxidation of UDPGlc occurs in a two-step reaction. The reaction yields UDPglucuronate.

UDP-glucuronate is the source of glucuronate for reactions involving its incorporation into proteoglycans.

UDP-glucuronate is the source of glucuronate for reaction with substrates such as steroid hormones, bilirubin, and a number of drugs. UDP-glucuronate reactions result in the excretion of glucuronide conjugates in urine or bile.

Glucuronate is reduced to L-gulonate in an NADPH dependent reaction. L-gulonate is the direct precursor of ascorbate in those animals capable of synthesizing this vitamin.

In human beings and other primates, as well as guinea pigs, bats, and some birds and fishes, ascorbic acid cannot be synthesized because of the absence of L-gulonolactone oxidase.

L-Gulonate is oxidized to 3-keto-L-gulonate. 3-keto-L-gulonate is then decarboxylated to L-xylulose.

L-Xylulose is converted to xylitol by an NADPH-dependent reduction. Xylitol is oxidized in an NAD-dependent reaction to D-xylulose.

D-xylulose 5-phosphate is metabolized via the pentose phosphate pathway.

# INGESTION OF LARGE QUANTITIES OF FRUCTOSE HAS PROFOUND METABOLICCONSEQUENCES

Diets high in sucrose or in high-fructose syrups (HFCS 42 and HFCS55) used in manufactured foods and beverages lead to large amounts of fructose and glucose entering the hepatic portal vein. High fructose corn syrup does not have much more fructose than sucrose, with 50% fructose. Other dietary sources of sugar, such as apples, have more fructose, at 73%. The total quantity of simple sugars ingested is the important issue. In 1900, Americans ingested about 15 g/day of fructose, primarily from fruits and vegetables. By 2020, this increased to 77 g/day for adults and about 81 g/day for children, totaling approximately 300 kcals/day, a sixfold increase. The American Heart Association recommends keeping fructose intake below 25 g/day for women and children and 37 g/day for men. High simple sugar intake increases the risk of obesity, hyperuricemia, high blood pressure, and diabetes.

Nearly 90% of dietary fructose is metabolized by the liver. Fructose undergoes more rapid glycolysis in the liver than glucose because it bypasses the regulatory step catalyzed by phosphofructokinase. Fructose allows fructose to flood liver pathways, leading to increased fatty acid synthesis. Fructose allows fructose to flood liver pathways, leading to increased esterification of fatty acids. Fructose allows fructose to flood liver pathways, leading to increased secretion of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL). Increased secretion of VLDL may raise serum triacylglycerols and ultimately increase LDL cholesterol concentrations. Fructokinase in the liver, kidney, and intestine catalyzes the phosphorylation of fructose to fructose-1-phosphate. Fructokinase does not act on glucose. Its activity is not affected by fasting or insulin, which explains why fructose is cleared from the blood of diabetic patients at a normal rate. Fructose-1-phosphate is cleaved to D-glyceraldehyde and dihydroxyacetone phosphate by aldolase B, an enzyme found in the liver. D-Glyceraldehyde enters glycolysis via phosphorylation to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate catalyzed by triokinase. The two triose phosphates, dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, may either be degraded by glycolysis or serve as substrates for aldolase and hence gluconeogenesis. Fructose-1-phosphate activates glucokinase, amplifying dietary glucose entry into the liver. The rapid entry and phosphorylation of fructose consume ATP quickly, causing a rise in ADP and AMP. AMP can be converted to hypoxanthine in the liver and to uric acid, which can cause gout.

# GALACTOSE IS NEEDED FOR THE SYNTHESIS OF LACTOSE, GLYCOLIPIDS, PROTEOGLYCANS,& GLYCOPROTEINS

Galactose is derived from the intestinal hydrolysis of lactose, the sugar found in milk. Galactose is transported by GLUT5. Galactose is converted in the liver to glucose. The majority of dietary galactose is metabolized by the liver, similar to fructose. Galactokinase catalyzes the phosphorylation of galactose. ATP is used as the phosphate donor in the phosphorylation of galactose. Galactose-1-phosphate reacts with UDPGlc to form uridine diphosphate galactose (UDPGal) and glucose-1-phosphate. Galactose-1-phosphate uridyl transferase catalyzes the reaction between galactose-1-phosphate and UDPGlc. The conversion of UDPGal to UDPGlc is catalyzed by UDPGal 4-epimerase. The reaction involving UDPGal and UDPGlc includes oxidation and reduction at carbon 4. NAD+ acts as a coenzyme in the reaction involving UDPGal and UDPGlc. UDPGlc is incorporated into glycogen.

The epimerase reaction is freely reversible, allowing glucose to be converted to galactose. Galactose is not a dietary essential. Galactose is required for the formation of lactose during lactation. Galactose is a constituent of glycolipids (cerebrosides). Galactose is a constituent of proteoglycans. Galactose is a constituent of glycoproteins. In the synthesis of lactose in the mammary gland, UDPGal condenses with glucose to yield lactose, catalyzed by lactose synthase (Figure 20–6).

## Glucose Is the Precursor of Amino Sugars (Hexosamines)

Amino sugars are important components of glycoproteins. Amino sugars are components of certain glycosphingolipids, such as gangliosides. Amino sugars are components of glycosaminoglycans. The major amino sugars are the hexosamines glucosamine, galactosamine, and mannosamine. The major amino sugars include the nine-carbon compound sialic acid. The principal sialic acid found in human tissues is N-acetylneuraminic acid (NeuAc).

## Impairment of the Pentose Phosphate Pathway Leads to Erythrocyte Hemolysis

Genetic defects of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase impair the generation of NADPH. Genetic defects of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase are common in populations of Mediterranean and Afro-Caribbean origin. The gene for glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase is on the X chromosome, so it mainly affects males. Approximately 400 million people carry a mutated gene for glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, making it the most common genetic defect. Most carriers are asymptomatic. In some populations, defects in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase are common enough to be considered a genetic polymorphism. The distribution of mutant genes parallels that of malaria, suggesting heterozygosity confers resistance against malaria. The defect manifests as red cell hemolysis (hemolytic anemia) when susceptible individuals are subjected to oxidative stress from infection, drugs such as the antimalarial primaquine and sulfonamides, or after eating fava beans (Vicia faba). The disease associated with this defect is called favism.

Many different mutations are known in the gene for glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase. These mutations lead to two main variants of favism. In the Afro-Caribbean variant, the enzyme is unstable. In the Afro-Caribbean variant, the enzyme results in low average red-cell activities. In the Afro-Caribbean variant, only older erythrocytes are affected by oxidative stress. In the Afro-Caribbean variant, the hemolytic crises tend to be self-limiting. In the Mediterranean variant, the enzyme is stable. In the Mediterranean variant, the enzyme has low activity in all erythrocytes. In the Mediterranean variant, hemolytic crises are more severe and can be fatal.

Glutathione peroxidase depends on a supply of NADPH. Erythrocytes can only form NADPH via the pentose phosphate pathway.

## Disruption of the Uronic Acid Pathway Is Caused by Enzyme Defects & Some Drugs

In essential pentosuria, a lack of xylulose reductase causes xylulose to appear in the urine. Xylulose reductase is the enzyme necessary to reduce xylulose to xylitol. Essential pentosuria is a rare benign hereditary condition. Essential pentosuria has no clinical consequences. Xylulose is a reducing sugar. Xylulose can cause false-positive results when urinary glucose is measured using alkaline copper reagents.

Barbital increases the conversion of glucose to glucuronate, L-gulonate, and ascorbate in rats. Chlorobutanol increases the conversion of glucose to glucuronate, L-gulonate, and ascorbate in rats. Aminopyrine increases the excretion of xylulose in pentosuric subjects. Antipyrine increases the excretion of xylulose in pentosuric subjects.

Consumption of large amounts of fruits rich in pentoses, such as pears, can cause pentosuria. Pentosuria is known as alimentary pentosuria.

## Loading of the Liver With Fructose May Potentiate Hypertriacylglycerolemia, Hypercholesterolemia, & Hyperuricemia

In the liver, fructose increases fatty acid and triacylglycerol synthesis. Fructose increases VLDL secretion. Fructose leads to hypertriacylglycerolemia. Hypertriacylglycerolemia is potentially atherogenic. Fructose increases LDL cholesterol. Increased LDL cholesterol is potentially atherogenic. Fructose enters glycolysis via fructokinase. Fructose-1-phosphate bypasses the regulatory step catalyzed by phosphofructokinase. Acute loading of the liver with fructose causes sequestration of inorganic phosphate in fructose-1-phosphate. Acute fructose loading results in diminished ATP synthesis. Diminished ATP synthesis leads to less inhibition of de novo purine synthesis by ATP. Less inhibition of de novo purine synthesis increases uric acid formation. Increased uric acid formation causes hyperuricemia. Hyperuricemia is the cause of gout. Fructose is absorbed from the small intestine by carrier-mediated diffusion. High oral doses of fructose may lead to osmotic diarrhea.

## Defects in Fructose Metabolism Cause Disease

A lack of hepatic fructokinase causes essential fructosuria. Essential fructosuria is a benign and asymptomatic condition. Hereditary fructose intolerance is characterized by profound hypoglycemia and vomiting after consumption of fructose. Hereditary fructose intolerance is also characterized by profound hypoglycemia and vomiting after consumption of sucrose. Sucrose yields fructose on digestion. Diets low in fructose, sorbitol, and sucrose are beneficial for essential fructosuria. Diets low in fructose, sorbitol, and sucrose are beneficial for hereditary fructose intolerance. Fructose induced hypoglycemia occurs in hereditary fructose intolerance. Fructose induced hypoglycemia occurs despite high glycogen reserves in hereditary fructose intolerance. Fructose-1-phosphate allosterically inhibits liver glycogen phosphorylase. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate allosterically inhibits liver glycogen phosphorylase. Fructose induced hypoglycemia also occurs in fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase deficiency. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase deficiency is related to hereditary fructose intolerance. The sequestration of inorganic phosphate leads to depletion of ATP. The sequestration of inorganic phosphate leads to hyperuricemia.

## Fructose & Sorbitol in the Lens Are Associated With Diabetic Cataract

Fructose and sorbitol are found in increased concentrations in the lens of the eye in diabetes mellitus.

Increased concentrations of fructose and sorbitol in the lens may be involved in the pathogenesis of diabetic cataract.

The sorbitol (polyol) pathway is responsible for fructose formation from glucose.

The sorbitol pathway increases in activity as the glucose concentration rises in tissues that are not insulin sensitive, such as the lens, peripheral nerves, and renal glomeruli.

Glucose is reduced to sorbitol by aldose reductase.

Sorbitol is oxidized to fructose in the presence of NAD+ and sorbitol dehydrogenase (polyol dehydrogenase).

Sorbitol does not diffuse through cell membranes, causing it to accumulate and potentially cause osmotic damage.

Myoinositol levels fall simultaneously with sorbitol accumulation.

In experimental animals, sorbitol accumulation and myoinositol depletion can be prevented by aldose reductase inhibitors.

Aldose reductase inhibitors can prevent diabetic cataract in experimental animals.

A number of aldose reductase inhibitors are undergoing clinical trials for the prevention of adverse effects of diabetes.

## Enzyme Deficiencies in the Galactose Pathway Cause Galactosemia

Inability to metabolize galactose occurs in the galactosemias. Galactosemias may be caused by inherited defects of galactokinase, uridyl transferase, or 4-epimerase. Deficiency of uridyl transferase is the best known cause. Galactose is a substrate for aldose reductase. Aldose reductase forms galactitol from galactose. Galactitol accumulates in the lens of the eye. Accumulation of galactitol causes cataract. Defects in uridyl transferase result in more severe conditions. Galactose-1-phosphate accumulates due to uridyl transferase deficiency. Accumulation of galactose-1-phosphate depletes the liver of inorganic phosphate. Depletion of inorganic phosphate in the liver ultimately leads to liver failure. Mental deterioration results from the deficiency of uridyl transferase. In uridyl transferase deficiency, the epimerase is present in adequate amounts. Adequate amounts of epimerase allow the formation of UDPGal from glucose. Affected children can experience normal growth and development despite galactose-free diets. Galactose-free diets are used to control the symptoms of the disease.

The pathway has an oxidative phase, which is irreversible and generates NADPH, and a nonoxidative phase, which is reversible and provides ribose precursors for nucleotide synthesis. The complete pathway is present mainly in those tissues having a requirement for NADPH for reductive syntheses, for example, lipogenesis or steroidogenesis, whereas the nonoxidative phase is present in all cells requiring ribose.
In erythrocytes, the pathway has a major function in preventing hemolysis by providing NADPH to maintain glutathione in the reduced state as the substrate for glutathione peroxidase.
The uronic acid pathway is the source of glucuronic acid for conjugation of many endogenous and exogenous substances before excretion as glucuronides in urine and bile.
Fructose bypasses the main regulatory step in glycolysis, catalyzed by phosphofructokinase, and stimulates liver glucose uptake, fatty acid synthesis, and hepatic triacylglycerol secretion.
Galactose is synthesized from glucose in the lactating mammary gland and in other tissues where it is required for the synthesis of glycolipids, proteoglycans, and glycoproteins.
