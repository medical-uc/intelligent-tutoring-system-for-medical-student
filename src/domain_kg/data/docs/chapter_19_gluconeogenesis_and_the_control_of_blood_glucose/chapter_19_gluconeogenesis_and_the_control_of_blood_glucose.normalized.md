# Gluconeogenesis & the Control of Blood Glucose

Explain the importance of gluconeogenesis in glucose homeostasis.
Describe the pathway of gluconeogenesis, how irreversible enzymes of glycolysis are bypassed, and how glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are regulated reciprocally.
Explain how plasma glucose concentration is maintained within narrow limits in the fed and fasting states.

# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Gluconeogenesis is the process of synthesizing glucose from noncarbohydrate precursors. The major substrates for gluconeogenesis are glucogenic amino acids, lactate, glycerol, and propionate. The liver and kidney are the major gluconeogenic tissues. The liver is the primary gluconeogenic organ. The renal cortex of the kidney may contribute about 10% of whole body gluconeogenesis after a short-term fast of 18 to 24 hours. The kidney is not a net source of glucose because the renal medulla is a consumer of glucose. With long-term fasting of about 7 days, the kidney can supply net glucose carbon to contribute to glucose homeostasis. The key gluconeogenic enzymes are expressed in the small intestine. Propionate from intestinal bacterial fermentation of carbohydrates is a substrate for gluconeogenesis in enterocytes. The intestine is not a net consumer of lactate and alanine or glycerol, the major substrates for gluconeogenesis. The intestine is a consumer of glucose in the fasting state. Any glucose synthesis that occurs locally in the intestine is metabolized locally.

The rate of hepatic gluconeogenesis is determined by the availability of gluconeogenic substrates. The rate of hepatic gluconeogenesis is determined by the capacity of the liver to take up gluconeogenic substrates. The rate of hepatic gluconeogenesis is determined by the quantity of the gluconeogenic enzymes. The rate of hepatic gluconeogenesis is determined by the activity of the gluconeogenic enzymes. The rate of hepatic gluconeogenesis is determined by the oxidative capacity of the liver to supply the energy required for gluconeogenesis. The rate of hepatic gluconeogenesis is determined by the oxidative capacity of the liver to metabolize the nitrogen from glucogenic amino acids. Ureagenesis is related to the metabolism of nitrogen from glucogenic amino acids.

Throughout the 24-hour feeding fasting cycle, gluconeogenic precursors are available. In the fasting state, adipose tissue lipolysis releases glycerol. In the fasting state, skeletal muscle releases lactate. In the fasting state, skeletal muscle releases gluconeogenic amino acids. As the fast is extended, glycerol provides an increasing role in supplying carbon for gluconeogenesis. As the fast is extended, amino acids provide an increasing role in supplying carbon for gluconeogenesis. Glucose demands of peripheral tissues, including the brain, decrease as a fast progresses. The decrease in glucose demand helps preserve vital protein stores. In the fed state, gluconeogenic supply does not decrease; it is elevated. In the fed state, glycerol supply decreases due to decreased lipolysis. In the fed state, lactate supply does not decrease primarily because of high rates of glycolysis in skeletal muscle. In the fed state, amino acid supply increases as amino acids derived from dietary protein are delivered directly into the portal vein. During exercise, lactate supply from working muscle supports the increased gluconeogenic demand of exercise.

The transport and uptake of amino acids, glycerol, and lactate are regulated differently. The liver is extremely efficient at taking up glycerol. More than 60% of glycerol delivered to the liver is removed on first pass. More than 60% of glycerol delivered to the liver is removed on first pass remains constant in response to changes in insulin, glucagon, and epinephrine. Amino acid removal is very sensitive to glucagon and to a lesser extent insulin. Glucagon is a potent stimulator of the transport of glucogenic amino acids by the liver. About 20% of these amino acids are removed on first pass. First pass extraction can increase to more than 60% in the presence of an increase in glucagon. Insulin can stimulate amino acid transport, but the response is much smaller than that of glucagon. Lactate uptake by the liver is complex because the liver can produce lactate during high rates of glycogen breakdown. In the fasting state, the primary driver of lactate uptake is the availability of lactate. When lactate is increased along with increases in glucagon, the liver can become an efficient consumer of lactate, supporting the gluconeogenic response, for example, as seen in exercise.

In disease states associated with hyperglycemia, such as infection and diabetes, gluconeogenesis is inappropriately increased. In critically ill patients, in response to injury and infection, gluconeogenic supply is very high. Elevated lactate, increased lipolysis, and increased protein catabolism contribute to the high gluconeogenic supply in critically ill patients. Underlying insulin resistance and high glucagon levels drive gluconeogenesis in critically ill patients. Hyperglycemia is associated with poor outcomes in critically ill patients. In insulin deficiency, as seen in diabetic ketoacidosis in type 1 diabetes, unopposed lipolysis and protein catabolism amplify hyperglycemia. Hyperglycemia leads to changes in osmolality of body fluids. Hyperglycemia leads to impaired blood flow. Hyperglycemia leads to intracellular acidosis. Hyperglycemia leads to increased superoxide radical production. Changes in osmolality, blood flow, intracellular acidosis, and superoxide radical production result in deranged endothelial and immune system function. Changes in osmolality, blood flow, intracellular acidosis, and superoxide radical production lead to impaired blood coagulation.

With liver failure, gluconeogenesis is impaired. With liver failure, hypoglycemia develops. Liver failure occurs despite high substrate supply, severe insulin resistance, and very high glucagon levels. The inability to support energy production in the liver starves the gluconeogenic pathway of the energy required to support the synthesis of glucose. The inability to support energy production in the liver is due to impaired citric acid cycle flux and ureagenesis.

## Pyruvate & Phosphoenolpyruvate

Reversal of the reaction catalyzed by pyruvate kinase in glycolysis involves two endothermic reactions.

Mitochondrial pyruvate carboxylase catalyzes the carboxylation of pyruvate to oxaloacetate.

The carboxylation of pyruvate to oxaloacetate requires ATP and uses biotin as a coenzyme.

The resultant oxaloacetate is reduced to malate.

Malate is exported from the mitochondrion into the cytosol.

Malate is then oxidized back to oxaloacetate in the cytosol.

Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase catalyzes the decarboxylation and phosphorylation of oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate.

The reaction catalyzed by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase uses GTP as the phosphate donor.

In the liver and kidney, the reaction of succinate thiokinase in the citric acid cycle produces GTP rather than ATP as in other tissues.

GTP is used for the reaction of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase.

## Fructose 1,6-Bisphosphate & Fructose-6-Phosphate

The conversion of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate to fructose-6-phosphate is catalyzed by fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase.

Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase enables the reversal of glycolysis.

Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase determines whether a tissue can synthesize glucose or glycogen from pyruvate and triose phosphates such as glycerol.

Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase is present in the liver, kidney, and skeletal muscle.

Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase is probably absent from heart and smooth muscle.

## Glucose-6-Phosphate & Glucose

The conversion of glucose-6-phosphate to glucose is catalyzed by glucose-6-phosphatase.

Glucose-6-phosphatase is present in the liver and kidney (renal cortex).

Glucose-6-phosphatase is absent from muscle.

Muscle cannot export glucose derived from glycogen into the bloodstream.

# GLYCOLYSIS & GLUCONEOGENESIS SHARE THE SAME PATHWAY BUT IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS, & ARE RECIPROCALLY REGULATED

Changes in the availability of substrates are responsible for most changes in metabolism either directly or indirectly acting via changes in hormone secretion.

Three mechanisms are responsible for regulating the activity of enzymes concerned in carbohydrate metabolism: changes in the rate of enzyme synthesis, covalent modification by reversible phosphorylation, and allosteric effects.

Glucagon and epinephrine are hormones that respond to a decrease in blood glucose.

Glucagon and epinephrine stimulate gluconeogenesis in the liver.

Glucagon is a potent stimulator of amino acid transport.

## Allosteric Modification Is Instantaneous

In gluconeogenesis, pyruvate carboxylase catalyzes the synthesis of oxaloacetate from pyruvate. Pyruvate carboxylase requires acetyl-CoA as an allosteric activator. The addition of acetyl-CoA changes the tertiary structure of the protein. Acetyl-CoA is formed from pyruvate, ensuring the provision of oxaloacetate by activating pyruvate carboxylase. Acetyl-CoA derived from the oxidation of fatty acids inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase. The inhibition spares the oxidation of pyruvate and glucose, stimulating gluconeogenesis. The reciprocal relationship between pyruvate carboxylase and pyruvate dehydrogenase alters the metabolic fate of pyruvate. The alteration occurs as the tissue changes from carbohydrate oxidation to gluconeogenesis during the transition from the fed to fasting state. Fatty acid oxidation promotes gluconeogenesis by supplying the ATP required for glucose synthesis.

Phosphofructokinase-1 regulates glycolysis and is subject to feedback control. Phosphofructokinase-1 is inhibited by citrate and normal intracellular concentrations of ATP. Phosphofructokinase-1 is activated by 5′AMP. At normal intracellular [ATP], the enzyme is about 90% inhibited. The inhibition of phosphofructokinase-1 by ATP is reversed by 5′AMP. 5′AMP acts as an indicator of the energy status of the cell. When ATP is used in energy-requiring processes, resulting in the formation of ADP, [AMP] increases. A relatively small decrease in [ATP] causes a several fold increase in [AMP]. [AMP] acts as a metabolic amplifier of a small change in [ATP], serving as a sensitive signal of the energy state of the cell. The activity of phosphofructokinase-1 is regulated in response to the energy status of the cell to control the quantity of carbohydrate undergoing glycolysis prior to its entry into the citric acid cycle. AMP activates glycogen phosphorylase, increasing glycogenolysis. The inhibition of phosphofructokinase-1 by ATP leads to an accumulation of glucose-6-phosphate. The accumulation of glucose-6-phosphate inhibits further uptake of glucose in extrahepatic tissues by inhibiting hexokinase. Glucokinase, present in the liver, is not inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate. This allows for high rates of glucose entry and diversion to glycogen deposition. Gluconeogenesis-derived carbon can be diverted to glycogen as well.

Fructose 2,6-bisphosphate increases the affinity of phosphofructokinase-1 for fructose-6-phosphate. The concentration of fructose 2,6-bisphosphate is under substrate (allosteric) control. ## Substrate (Futile) Cycles Allow Fine Tuning & Rapid Response

Glycolysis and glycogen metabolism involve a cycle of phosphorylation and dephosphorylation. Glucokinase and glucose-6-phosphatase are part of this cycle. Phosphofructokinase-1 and fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase are part of this cycle. Pyruvate kinase, pyruvate carboxylase, and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase are part of this cycle. Glycogen synthase and phosphorylase are part of this cycle. Opposing enzymes in glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are regulated to prevent cycling between phosphorylated and nonphosphorylated intermediates. Cycling would lead to net hydrolysis of ATP. In the liver, futile cycling of carbon occurs at a low rate of 1-2%. Futile cycling of carbon allows the liver to rapidly transition between fed, fasted, or exercising states.

In muscle, phosphofructokinase and fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase have some activity at all times. The activity of phosphofructokinase and fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase results in wasteful substrate cycling. At rest, phosphofructokinase activity is about 10 times higher than fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase activity. In anticipation of muscle contraction, the activity of both enzymes increases. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase activity increases 10 times more than phosphofructokinase activity. The increase in fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase activity maintains the same net rate of glycolysis. At the start of muscle contraction, phosphofructokinase activity increases further. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase activity decreases. The decrease in fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase activity increases the net rate of glycolysis and ATP formation by up to 1000 times.

# THE BLOOD CONCENTRATION OF GLUCOSE IS REGULATED WITHIN NARROW LIMITS

In the postabsorptive state, the concentration of blood glucose is maintained between 4.5 and 5.5 mmol/L. After the ingestion of a carbohydrate meal, the concentration of blood glucose may rise to 6.5 to 7.2 mmol/L. In starvation, the concentration of blood glucose may fall to 3.3 to 3.9 mmol/L.

A sudden decrease in blood glucose causes convulsions because of the brain's dependence on glucose. Much lower concentrations of blood glucose can be tolerated if hypoglycemia develops slowly enough for adaptation to occur.

The blood glucose level in birds is considerably higher, at 14 mmol/L. In ruminants, the blood glucose level is considerably lower, at approximately 2.2 mmol/L in sheep and 3.3 mmol/L in cattle.

Ruminants ferment virtually all dietary carbohydrate to short-chain fatty acids. These short-chain fatty acids largely replace glucose as the main metabolic fuel of the tissues, including the brain, in the fed state.

# BLOOD GLUCOSE IS DERIVED FROM THE DIET, GLUCONEOGENESIS, & GLYCOGENOLYSIS

The digestible dietary carbohydrates yield glucose, galactose, and fructose. Glucose, galactose, and fructose are transported to the liver via the hepatic portal vein. Galactose is readily converted to glucose in the liver. Fructose is readily converted to glucose in the liver.

Glucose is formed from two groups of compounds that undergo gluconeogenesis: those that involve a direct net conversion to glucose, including most amino acids and propionate. Glucose is formed from two groups of compounds that undergo gluconeogenesis: those that are the products of the metabolism of glucose in tissues. Lactate is formed by glycolysis in skeletal muscle and erythrocytes. Lactate is transported to the liver and kidney where it reforms glucose. The process of lactate being transported to the liver and kidney where it reforms glucose is known as the Cori cycle, or the lactic acid cycle (Figure 19-4).

In the fasting state, there is a considerable output of alanine from skeletal muscle. Alanine is formed by transamination of pyruvate produced by glycolysis of muscle glycogen. Alanine is exported to the liver. In the liver, alanine is transaminated back to pyruvate. Pyruvate is a substrate for gluconeogenesis in the liver. ## Metabolic & Hormonal Mechanisms Regulate the Concentration of Blood Glucose

The maintenance of a stable blood glucose concentration is one of the most finely regulated of all homeostatic mechanisms. The regulation involves the liver, extrahepatic tissues, and several hormones. Liver cells are freely permeable to glucose in either direction via the GLUT 2 transporter. Cells of extrahepatic tissues, apart from pancreatic β-islets, are relatively impermeable to glucose. Unidirectional glucose transporters in extrahepatic tissues are regulated by insulin in insulin-sensitive tissues such as muscle, heart, and adipose tissue. Transport from the bloodstream is an important determinant of glucose utilization in extrahepatic tissues, but it is not the sole determinant. ## Glucokinase Is Important in Regulating Blood Glucose After a Meal

Hexokinase acts at a constant rate under all normal conditions in the liver. Hexokinase ensures an adequate rate of glycolysis to meet the liver’s needs. Glucokinase has a lower affinity for glucose compared to hexokinase. The activity of glucokinase increases with an increase in the concentration of glucose in the hepatic portal vein. In the fasting state, glucokinase is located in the nucleus. In response to an increased intracellular concentration of glucose, glucokinase migrates into the cytosol. Glucokinase permits hepatic uptake of large amounts of glucose after a carbohydrate meal. Glucokinase facilitates glycogen and fatty acid synthesis in the liver. The concentration of glucose in the hepatic portal vein may reach 20 mmol/L after a meal. The glucose concentration leaving the liver into the peripheral circulation does not normally exceed 8 to 9 mmol/L. Glucokinase is absent from the liver of ruminants. Ruminants have little glucose entering the portal circulation from the intestines. At normal peripheral blood glucose concentrations of 4.5-5.5 mmol/L, the liver is a net producer of glucose. As the glucose level rises, the output of glucose from the liver ceases. A rise in glucose level leads to net uptake by the liver.

## Insulin & Glucagon Play a Central Role in Regulating Blood Glucose

Insulin is produced by the β cells of the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas in response to hyperglycemia. The β-islet cells are freely permeable to glucose via the GLUT 2 transporter. Glucose is phosphorylated by glucokinase. Increasing blood glucose increases metabolic flux through glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and the generation of ATP. The increase in [ATP] inhibits ATP-sensitive K+ channels. Inhibition of ATP-sensitive K+ channels causes depolarization of the cell membrane. Depolarization increases Ca2+ influx via voltage-sensitive Ca2+ channels. Increased Ca2+ influx stimulates exocytosis of insulin. The concentration of insulin in the blood parallels that of the blood glucose. Amino acids cause release of insulin from the pancreas. Nonesterified fatty acids cause release of insulin from the pancreas. Ketone bodies cause release of insulin from the pancreas. Glucagon causes release of insulin from the pancreas. Secretin causes release of insulin from the pancreas. The sulfonylurea drugs tolbutamide and glyburide cause release of insulin from the pancreas. Sulfonylurea drugs are used to stimulate insulin secretion in type 2 diabetes mellitus via the ATP-sensitive K+ channels. GLP-1 receptor agonists increase cyclic-AMP, which potentiates glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. Epinephrine blocks the release of insulin. Norepinephrine blocks the release of insulin. Insulin lowers blood glucose immediately by enhancing glucose transport into adipose tissue and muscle. Insulin recruits glucose transporters (GLUT 4) from the interior of the cell to the plasma membrane. Insulin does not affect glucose transport activity in the liver. Insulin directly augments liver glucose uptake and glycogen deposition. Insulin affects glucokinase and glycogen synthase and phosphorylase activity. Insulin and other hormones modulate long-term uptake by affecting transcriptional signals mediated in part by the carbohydrate response element binding protein (ChREBP).

Glucagon is produced by the α cells of the pancreatic islets in response to hypoglycemia. In the liver, glucagon stimulates glycogenolysis by activating glycogen phosphorylase. Glucagon does not affect muscle phosphorylase. Glucagon enhances gluconeogenesis from amino acids and lactate. Glucagon acts via generation of cAMP. Hepatic glycogenolysis contributes to the hyperglycemic effect of glucagon. Gluconeogenesis contributes to the hyperglycemic effect of glucagon. Glucagon's actions oppose those of insulin. Most of the endogenous glucagon is cleared from the circulation by the liver. Most of the insulin is cleared from the circulation by the liver (Table 19–3).

## Other Hormones Affect Blood Glucose

The anterior pituitary gland secretes hormones that tend to elevate blood glucose and therefore antagonize the action of insulin. Growth hormone is one of these hormones. Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) is another hormone secreted by the anterior pituitary gland. Growth hormone secretion is stimulated by hypoglycemia. Growth hormone decreases glucose uptake in muscle. Growth hormone stimulates the mobilization of nonesterified fatty acids from adipose tissue. Nonesterified fatty acids inhibit glucose utilization. The glucocorticoids, or 11-oxysteroids, are secreted by the adrenal cortex. Glucocorticoids are also synthesized at low rates in an unregulated manner in tissues such as immune cells and adipose tissue. Glucocorticoids increase gluconeogenesis as a result of enhanced hepatic catabolism of amino acids. Glucocorticoids induce aminotransferases and other enzymes such as tryptophan dioxygenase. Glucocorticoids induce key enzymes of gluconeogenesis. Glucocorticoids inhibit the utilization of glucose in extrahepatic tissues. Glucocorticoids act in a manner antagonistic to insulin. Cytokines secreted by macrophages infiltrating adipose tissue have insulin antagonistic actions. Glucocorticoids secreted by adipose tissue contribute to insulin resistance in obese people. Epinephrine is secreted by the adrenal medulla. Epinephrine is secreted in response to stressful stimuli such as fear, excitement, hemorrhage, hypoxia, and hypoglycemia. Epinephrine leads to glycogenolysis in the liver and muscle. Epinephrine stimulates phosphorylase via the generation of cAMP. In muscle, glycogenolysis results in increased glycolysis and lactate release. In the liver, glycogenolysis results in the release of glucose into the bloodstream. Epinephrine is a potent stimulator of gluconeogenesis. Epinephrine increases substrate supply robustly.

## Glucosuria Occurs When the Renal Threshold for Glucose Is Exceeded

When the blood glucose concentration rises above about 10 mmol/L, the kidney exerts a passive regulatory effect. Glucose is continuously filtered by the glomeruli. Glucose is normally completely reabsorbed in the renal tubules by active transport. The capacity of the tubular system to reabsorb glucose is limited to a rate of about 2 mmol/min. In hyperglycemia, the glomerular filtrate may contain more glucose than can be reabsorbed. Hyperglycemia occurs in poorly controlled diabetes mellitus. When the renal threshold for glucose is exceeded, glucosuria results. A common clinical presentation of diabetes is unexplained weight loss and frequent urination. The weight loss is due to volume loss and subsequent dehydration. Dehydration results from osmotic diuresis in the kidney. The loss of glucose calories in the urine contributes to the weight loss.

## Hypoglycemia May Occur During Pregnancy & in the Neonate

During pregnancy, fetal glucose consumption increases.

Maternal hypoglycemia may occur during pregnancy, particularly if there are long intervals between meals or at night.

Fetal hypoglycemia may occur during pregnancy, particularly if there are long intervals between meals or at night.

Premature and low-birth-weight babies are more susceptible to hypoglycemia.

Premature and low-birth-weight babies have little adipose tissue to provide nonesterified fatty acids.

Gluconeogenesis enzymes may not be fully developed in premature and low-birth-weight babies.

Gluconeogenesis is dependent on an adequate supply and subsequent oxidation of nonesterified fatty acids for ATP formation.

Little glycerol is available for gluconeogenesis in premature and low-birth-weight babies.

Glycerol is normally released from adipose tissue for gluconeogenesis.

## The Ability to Utilize Glucose May Be Ascertained by Measuring Glucose Tolerance

Glucose tolerance is the ability to regulate blood glucose concentration after the administration of a test dose of glucose, typically 1 g/kg body weight (Figure 19-5). Normal glucose tolerance requires appropriate timing and quantity of insulin secretion. Normal glucose tolerance requires tissue response to insulin. Weight gain leads to insulin resistance. Most people who gain weight do not become glucose intolerant because their beta cells compensate by producing additional insulin. The risk of developing diabetes increases with weight gain. The glucose tolerance test is primarily used to detect gestational diabetes. If the hemoglobin A1c level is more than 6.5%, it is diagnostic of diabetes.

Diabetes mellitus, including type 1 (insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus [IDDM]), is characterized by impaired glucose tolerance. Impaired glucose tolerance in type 1 diabetes mellitus is due to decreased insulin secretion from the destruction of pancreatic β-islet cells. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (noninsulin-dependent diabetes [NIDDM]) impairs glucose tolerance. Impaired glucose tolerance in type 2 diabetes mellitus is due to reduced tissue sensitivity to insulin and impaired insulin secretion. Insulin resistance associated with obesity, particularly abdominal obesity, increases the risk of atherosclerosis, stroke, coronary heart disease, and overt diabetes. Individuals with three or more conditions, such as hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, elevated fasting glucose, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and central obesity, are diagnosed with metabolic syndrome. Impaired glucose tolerance can occur with liver damage. Impaired glucose tolerance can occur with certain infections. Impaired glucose tolerance can occur with some drugs. Impaired glucose tolerance can occur with conditions causing hyperactivity of the pituitary gland or adrenal cortex. Hormones from the pituitary gland or adrenal cortex antagonize insulin action.

Insulin administration lowers blood glucose concentration. Insulin administration increases glucose utilization and storage as glycogen in the liver and muscle. Excess insulin can cause hypoglycemia. Hypoglycemia can lead to convulsions and death if glucose is not administered promptly. Hypoglycemia upon fasting can occur in pituitary or adrenocortical insufficiency. Pituitary or adrenocortical insufficiency leads to decreased antagonism to insulin. Pituitary or adrenocortical insufficiency leads to reduced gluconeogenic capacity of the liver. In a glucose tolerance test, a normal person's blood glucose returns to baseline within 2 hours. A diabetic person's fasting glucose level is typically above 7 mM, indicating diabetes without further glucose tolerance measurement (Figure 19-5).

## Think Otherwise: Very Low Carbohydrate Diets Promote Weight Loss

Very low-carbohydrate diets provide only 20 g/day of carbohydrate or less. Very low-carbohydrate diets permit unlimited consumption of fat and protein. Very low-carbohydrate diets are promoted as an effective regime for weight loss. Very low-carbohydrate diets are counter to all advice on a prudent diet composition for health. There is a continual demand for glucose. There will be a considerable amount of gluconeogenesis from amino acids. The associated high ATP cost must be met by oxidation of fatty acids. Controlled energy balance studies demonstrate that energy expenditure is not increased. A calorie is a calorie. Weight loss occurs because they eat fewer calories. Low carbohydrate diets decrease glycogen stores. Glycogen stores have 3 g of water per gram of glycogen. The rapid weight loss commonly seen with these diets is due to decreased glycogen stores. Staying on these or other extreme diets is nearly impossible.

Gluconeogenesis is the process of synthesizing glucose or glycogen from noncarbohydrate precursors. It is of particular importance when carbohydrate is not available from the diet. The main substrates are amino acids, lactate, glycerol, and propionate.
The pathway of gluconeogenesis in the liver and kidney utilizes those reactions in glycolysis that are reversible plus four additional reactions that circumvent the irreversible nonequilibrium reactions.
Since glycolysis and gluconeogenesis share the same pathway but operate in opposite directions, their activities are regulated reciprocally.
Insulin is secreted as a direct response to hyperglycemia; it stimulates the liver to store glucose as glycogen and increases uptake of glucose into extrahepatic tissues.
Glucagon is secreted as a response to hypoglycemia and activates both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver, causing release of glucose into the blood.
