## Restriction Enzymes Cleave DNA Chains at Specific Locations

Endonucleases are enzymes that cut DNA at specific sequences within the molecule. Exonucleases digest DNA from the ends in a primarily sequence-independent manner. Restriction enzymes are a type of endonuclease. Restriction enzymes cut DNA into unique pieces in a sequence-specific manner. Restriction enzymes are termed restriction enzymes or REs. Restriction enzymes restrict the growth of certain bacteriophages in bacteria. Restriction enzymes protect host bacterial DNA from foreign organisms by digesting invading phage DNA. The viral RNA inducible interferon system provides a defense against RNA viruses in mammalian cells. The defense provided by the viral RNA inducible interferon system is distinct from restriction enzymes.

Restriction endonucleases are present in cells that also have a companion enzyme that methylates the host DNA. The companion enzyme renders the host DNA noncleavable by the restriction enzyme. Sequence-specific DNA methylases exist in pairs with restriction endonucleases in a given bacterium. Restriction endonucleases target the same sites as their companion DNA methylases.

Restriction enzymes are named after the bacterium from which they are isolated. BamHI is from Bacillus amyloliquefaciens. HpaI is from Haemophilus parainfluenzae. Each restriction enzyme recognizes and cleaves a specific double-stranded DNA sequence that is typically 4 to 8 bp long. The cuts can result in blunt ends or cohesive ends, depending on the enzyme. Cohesive ends are particularly useful in constructing hybrid DNA molecules.

The recognition sequences of restriction enzymes are usually palindromes. The frequency at which a restriction enzyme cuts DNA can be calculated based on the length of the recognition sequence. A 4-bp recognition sequence cuts DNA, on average, once every 256 bp. A 6-bp recognition sequence cuts DNA, on average, once every 4096 bp. Restriction maps can be constructed based on the linear array of cleavage sites in a DNA molecule. DNA fragments produced by restriction enzymes can be isolated by electrophoresis on agarose or polyacrylamide gels.

## Cloning Amplifies DNA

A clone is a large population of identical molecules, cells, or organisms that arise from a common ancestor. Molecular cloning allows for the production of a large number of identical DNA molecules. Molecular cloning allows for the production of a large number of identical DNA molecules can then be characterized or used for other purposes. Cloning vectors, such as bacterial plasmids, phages, or cosmids, are used to construct chimeric or hybrid DNA molecules. Cloning vectors replicate clonally in a single host cell under their own control systems. In this way, the chimeric DNA is amplified.

Bacterial plasmids are small, circular, duplex DNA molecules. The natural function of bacterial plasmids is to confer antibiotic resistance to the host cell. Plasmids exist as single or multiple identical copies within the bacterium. Plasmids replicate independently from the bacterial DNA as episomes. Plasmids use primarily the host replication machinery. The complete DNA sequence of thousands of plasmids is known. The precise location of restriction enzyme cleavage sites for inserting foreign DNA is available. Plasmids are smaller than the host chromosome and are easily biochemically separated from it in pure form. The desired plasmid-inserted DNA can be readily removed by cutting the plasmid with the enzyme specific for the restriction site into which the original piece of DNA was inserted.

Phages, or bacterial viruses, often have linear DNA genomes. Foreign DNA can be inserted into these genomes at unique restriction enzyme sites. The resulting chimeric DNA is collected after the phage proceeds through its lytic cycle. Phage vectors can accept DNA fragments up to ~20-kb long. Phage vectors can accept DNA fragments up to ~20-kb long is a major advantage over plasmids, which accept DNA pieces up to about 10-kb long. The ultimate insert size is imposed by the amount of DNA that can be packed into the phage head during virus propagation.

Cosmids are DNA cloning vectors that combine the best features of plasmids and phages. Cosmids are plasmids that contain the DNA sequences required for packaging lambda DNA into the λ-phage particle. These vectors grow in plasmid form in bacteria. Since much of the unnecessary lambda DNA has been removed, more chimeric DNA can be packaged into the particle head. Cosmids can carry inserts of chimeric DNA that are 35- to 50-kb long. Even larger pieces of DNA can be incorporated into bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC; 50-250-kb), yeast artificial chromosome (YAC; 500-3000kb), or E. coli bacteriophage P1-derived artificial chromosome (PAC; 50-250kb) vectors. Because BAC, PAC, and YAC vectors can accept and propagate DNA inserts of several hundred kilobases or more, they have largely replaced plasmid, phage, and cosmid vectors for many cloning applications.

Insertion of DNA into a functional region of the vector can interfere with the action of this region. Care must be taken not to interrupt an essential function of the vector. Insertion of DNA into a functional region of the vector can be exploited to provide a powerful double positive/negative selection technique. For example, the plasmid vector pBR322 has genes conferring resistance to both tetracycline (Tet) and ampicillin (Amp). A single PstI restriction enzyme site within the Amp resistance gene is commonly used as the insertion site for a piece of foreign DNA. Insertion at this site disrupts the ORF of the β-lactamase-encoding bla gene. β-Lactamase degrades and inactivates ampicillin. A bacterium carrying a plasmid with an inserted DNA fragment in the bla gene will be Amp-sensitive. Thus, cells carrying the parental plasmid, which provides resistance to both antibiotics, can be distinguished via replica plating from cells carrying the chimeric plasmid, which is resistant only to tetracycline (Figure 39-3).

## A Library Is a Collection of Recombinant Clones

A library is a collection of recombinant clones. A genomic library is prepared from the total DNA of a cell line or tissue fragmented using either restriction endonucleases or shearing and adaptor ligation. A cDNA library comprises complementary DNA copies of the population of mRNAs in a particular cell or tissue. Genomic DNA libraries are often prepared by performing partial digestion of total DNA with a restriction enzyme that cuts DNA frequently, such as a four-base cutter like Taql. The goal is to generate large fragments so that most genes remain intact. BAC, YAC, and P1 vectors are preferred because they can accept very large fragments of DNA. Large fragments increase the chance of isolating an intact eukaryotic mRNA-encoding gene on a single DNA fragment.

An expression vector is a vector in which the protein coded by the gene introduced by recombinant DNA technology is synthesized. Expression vectors are used to detect specific cDNA molecules in libraries. Expression vectors are used to produce proteins by genetic engineering techniques. Expression vectors are constructed to contain very active inducible promoters. Expression vectors are constructed to contain proper in-phase, optimized translation initiation codons. Expression vectors are constructed to contain transcription and translation termination signals. Expression vectors are constructed to contain appropriate protein processing signals if needed. Some expression vectors contain genes that code for protease inhibitors to enhance the final yield of the product. The cost of synthetic DNA synthesis has dropped. The cost drop allows investigators to synthesize an entire cDNA (gene) of interest in about 200 nt segments. The synthesis incorporates the codon preferences of the host used for expression to maximize protein production. New efficiencies in synthetic DNA synthesis allow for the de novo synthesis of complete genes and even small genomes, such as the Baker's Yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae genome of 12.07 Mbp. New efficiencies in synthetic DNA synthesis reduce the need to generate and probe complex genomic libraries to identify specific gene sequences. New efficiencies in synthetic DNA synthesis introduce new possibilities in synthetic biology. New efficiencies in synthetic DNA synthesis raise potential ethical conundrums.

## Blotting & Probing Techniques Allow Visualization and Quantification of Specific Target Molecules

Visualization of a specific DNA or RNA fragment among many nontarget molecules in a complex sample requires the use of blot transfer techniques. The Southern blot technique is used for DNA, the Northern blot for RNA, and the Western blot for proteins. Edward Southern devised the Southern blot technique, while the names Northern and Western originated as laboratory jargon but are now accepted terms. Blot transfer techniques are useful for determining the number of gene copies in a tissue and detecting alterations in a gene, such as deletions, insertions, or rearrangements. The initial step in these procedures involves gel electrophoresis, which separates molecules based on size. Occasionally, these procedures can detect point mutations if a specific base change alters a restriction site. The Northern and Western blot techniques are used to size and quantify specific RNA and protein molecules, respectively. The Southwestern blot examines protein-nucleic acid interactions, while the overlay assay examines protein-protein interactions. In these techniques, proteins are separated by electrophoresis, blotted to a membrane, renatured, and analyzed for interactions with specific probes. In the Southwestern blot, a labeled nucleic acid probe is used, while in the overlay assay, a labeled protein or antibody is used.

All nucleic acid-based hybridization procedures depend on the specific base-pairing properties of complementary nucleic acid strands. Perfect matches hybridize readily and can withstand high temperatures and low ionic strength conditions. Less than perfect matches do not tolerate such stringent conditions, and hybridization may not occur or may be disrupted during the washing step. Hybridization conditions have been devised to detect single base-pair mismatches between probe and target. Quantification and characterization of specific DNA sequences and RNA species are now generally performed using the Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) method.

In the Southern blot procedure, DNA isolated from a cell line or tissue is digested with restriction enzymes and subjected to gel electrophoresis. The DNA is then denatured and transferred to nitrocellulose or nylon paper via capillary action or electrotransfer. The DNA is bound to the paper by heat or UV exposure and hybridized with a labeled DNA probe. After washing, the paper is exposed to x-ray film or an imaging screen to reveal specific bands corresponding to the DNA fragments recognized by the probe (Figure 39-4). The Northern blot procedure is conceptually similar to the Southern blot, but it involves RNA instead of DNA. RNA is subjected to electrophoresis before blot transfer, with steps taken to ensure RNA integrity. The Western blot involves electrophoresing proteins and transferring them to special paper that binds proteins, followed by probing with a specific antibody or probe molecule. In Southwestern blotting, a protein blot is exposed to labeled nucleic acid, and protein-nucleic acid complexes are detected by autoradiography or imaging (Figure 39-4).

Sanger sequencing uses radiolabeled dideoxynucleotides. The radiolabeled dideoxynucleotides terminate DNA strand synthesis at specific nucleotides. DNA strand synthesis is catalyzed by DNA polymerase on single-stranded template DNA.

## The Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) Method Amplifies DNA Sequences

PCR is a method of amplifying a target sequence of DNA. The development of PCR has revolutionized the ways in which both DNA and RNA can be studied. PCR provides a sensitive, selective, and extremely rapid means of amplifying any desired sequence of DNA. Specificity in PCR is based on the use of two oligonucleotide primers that hybridize to complementary sequences on opposite strands of DNA and flank the target sequence (Figure 39-6).

[FIGURE:fig-39-6]

The polymerase chain reaction technique is used to amplify specific gene seguences. Double-stranded DNA is heated to separate it into individual strands. These bind two distinct primers that are directed at specific sequences on opposite strands and that define the segment to be amplified. DNA polymerase extends the primers in each direction and synthesizes two strands complementary to the original two. This cycle is repeated 30 or more times, giving an amplified product of defined length and sequence. Note that the four dXTPs and the two primers are present in excess to minimize the possibility that these components are limiting for polymerization/amplification. It is important to note though that as cycle number increases, incorporation rates can drop, and mutation/error rates can increase

The DNA sample is first heat denatured at a temperature greater than 90°C to separate the two strands of the template DNA containing the target sequence. Primers, added in excess, are allowed to anneal to the DNA typically at 50-75°C to generate the required template-primer complex. Each strand is copied by a special thermostable DNA polymerase, starting at the primer sites in the presence of all four dXTPs. The two DNA strands each serve as a template for the synthesis of new DNA from the two primers. Repeated cycles of heat denaturation, annealing of the primers to their complementary sequences, and extension of the annealed primers with DNA polymerase result in the exponential amplification of DNA segments of defined length. Product DNA doubles with each PCR cycle.

DNA synthesis is catalyzed by a heat-stable DNA polymerase purified from one of a number of different thermophilic bacteria, organisms that grow at 70° to 80°C. Thermostable DNA polymerases can withstand multiple, short incubations at more than 90°C, temperatures required to completely denature DNA. Thermostable DNA polymerases have made automation of PCR possible.

DNA sequences as short as 50 to 100 bp and as long as 10 kb can readily be amplified by PCR. Twenty cycles provide an amplification of 10⁶ (ie, 2²⁰) and 30 cycles provide an amplification of 10⁹ (2³⁰). Each cycle takes less than or equal to 5 to 10 minutes so that even large DNA molecules can be amplified fairly rapidly. Because of subtle differences in DNA sequence with each new PCR target, the exact conditions for amplification must sometimes be empirically optimized. Double-stranded DNA is heated to separate it into individual strands (Figure 39-6). The individual strands bind two distinct primers that are directed at specific sequences on opposite strands and that define the segment to be amplified. DNA polymerase extends the primers in each direction and synthesizes two strands complementary to the original two. ## RNA & Protein Profiling, & Protein-DNA Interaction Mapping

The -omic revolution has led to the complete nucleotide sequence determination of many genomes, including those of budding and fission yeasts, numerous bacteria, the fruit fly, the worm Caenorhabditis elegans, many plants, the mouse, rat, chicken, monkey, and humans. Additional genomes are being sequenced at an accelerating pace. The availability of DNA sequence information, combined with engineering advances, has resulted in the development of revolutionary methodologies, most of which are based on second- and third-generation sequencing platforms. In automated DNA sequencing, mRNAs are converted to cDNAs using retroviral RNA-dependent DNA polymerases to reverse transcribe mRNAs to DNAs. The resulting cDNAs are amplified and directly sequenced; this method is termed RNA-Seq. RNA-Seq allows for the quantitative description of the entire transcriptome. Recent reports have used RNA-Seq to describe the transcriptome of single cells. High-sensitivity ribosome profiling and mass spectrometry-based proteomics allow investigators to define gene expression profiles at the mRNA and protein levels.

Recent methodologic advances, such as PRO-Seq, Precision Run-On sequencing, and NET-Seq, allow for sequencing of RNA within elongating RNA polymerase-DNA-RNA ternary complexes, providing nucleotide-level descriptions of active transcription in living cells. Transcriptome information enables quantitative prediction of the collection of proteins that might be expressed in a particular cell, tissue, or organ in normal and disease states. Ribosome profiling allows for the quantitative measurement of the actual cellular proteome, particularly when coupled with newer high-sensitivity mass spectrometry to analyze protein content and PTM status of cellular proteomes.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) is a method used to map the location or occupancy of specific proteins bound to discrete DNA sequences within living cells. In ChIP, proteins are cross-linked in situ in cells or tissues, the cellular chromatin is isolated, sheared, and specific cross-linked protein-DNA complexes are purified using antibodies that recognize a particular protein or protein isoform. The DNA bound to this protein is recovered and amplified using PCR and analyzed using gel electrophoresis imaging, microarray hybridization (ChIP-chip), or direct sequencing. Immunopurified DNA is directly subjected to NGS/high-throughput DNA sequencing (ChIP-Seq), or treated with exonucleases to remove crosslinked DNA sequences not in intimate contact with the protein of interest (ChIP-Exo). ChIP-chip and ChIP-Seq methods allow investigators to identify the locations of a single protein genome-wide throughout all the chromosomes. ChIP-Exo allows investigators to map in vivo protein occupancy at single nucleotide-level resolution.

High-sensitivity, high-throughput mass spectrometry methods have been developed for metabolomics, lipidomics, glycomics, and proteomics. Newer mass spectrometry methods allow scientists to identify thousands of proteins in complex samples extracted from very small numbers of cells (<1 g). High-sensitivity, high-throughput mass spectrometry methods can quantify the relative amounts of proteins in two samples, as well as the level of certain PTMs, such as phosphorylation and acetylation. Specific antibodies can define specific protein-protein interactions. High-sensitivity, high-throughput mass spectrometry methods help determine which mRNAs detected in transcriptome mapping studies are being translated into protein, which is crucial for understanding cellular/tissue/organismal phenotypes.

New genetic means for identifying protein-protein interactions and protein function have been devised. Systematic genome-wide gene expression knockdown using siRNAs, synthetic lethal genetic interaction screens, or CRISPR-Cas9 knockdown have been used to assess the contribution of individual genes to various processes in model systems and mammalian cells. Specific network mappings of protein-protein interactions have been identified using high-throughput variants of the two-hybrid interaction test. This method can be performed in bacteria, yeast, or metazoan cells, and allows for the detection of specific protein-protein interactions in living cells. Protein-protein interactions with binding affinities of Kd ~10-6 mol/L or tighter can be detected with this method. The yeast cell functions as a molecular 'test tube' to probe for specific protein-protein interactions of biological significance. Readout of specific protein-protein interactions can be monitored via growth or other methods such as microscopy. Together, these technologies provide powerful tools for dissecting the intricacies of human biology.

Measurement of allelic SNP differences is useful for various types of gene mapping studies snRNA: Small nuclear RNA. This family of RNAs is best known for its role in mRNA processing.
Southern blot is a method for transferring DNA from an agarose gel to nitrocellulose filter, on which the DNA can be detected by a suitable probe (eg, complementary DNA or RNA).
Southwestern blot is a method for detecting protein-DNA interactions by applying a labeled DNA probe to a transfer membrane that contains a renatured protein.
Spliceosome is the macromolecular complex responsible for precursor mRNA splicing. The spliceosome consists of at least five small nuclear RNAs (snRNA; U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6) and many proteins.
Splicing is the removal of introns from RNA accompanied by the joining of its exons.
Sticky-ended DNA is Complementary single strands of DNA that protrude from opposite ends of a DNA duplex or from the ends of different duplex molecules (see also Blunt-ended DNA, earlier).
Tandem is Used to describe multiple copies of the same sequence (eg, DNA) that lie adjacent to one another.
Terminal transferase is an enzyme that adds nucleotides of one type (eg, deoxyadenonucleotidyl residues) to the 3' end of DNA strands.
Transcription is Template DNA-directed synthesis of nucleic acids, typically DNA-directed synthesis of RNA.
Transcriptome is the entire collection of expressed RNAs in a cell, tissue, organ, or organism; includes both mRNAs and ncRNAs.
Transgenic is Describing the introduction of new DNA into germ cells by its injection into the nucleus of the ovum.
Translation is Synthesis of protein using mRNA as template.
Vector is a plasmid or bacteriophage into which foreign DNA can be introduced for the purposes of cloning.
Western blot is a method for transferring protein to special filters, on which a protein of interest can be detected by a suitable probe (eg antibody, nucleic acid, ligand).
