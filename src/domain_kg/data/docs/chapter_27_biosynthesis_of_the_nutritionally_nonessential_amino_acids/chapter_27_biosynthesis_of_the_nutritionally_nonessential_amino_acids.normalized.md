# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Amino acid deficiency states can result if nutritionally essential amino acids are absent or present in inadequate amounts in the diet. Kwashiorkor sometimes results when a child is weaned onto a starchy diet poor in protein in certain regions of West Africa. Marasmus may occur if both caloric intake and specific amino acids are deficient. Patients with short bowel syndrome may suffer from significant nutritional and metabolic abnormalities if unable to absorb sufficient quantities of calories and nutrients.

The nutritional disorder scurvy is a dietary deficiency of vitamin C. Scurvy manifests as bleeding gums, swelling joints, poor wound healing, and ultimately in death. Specific genetic disorders are associated with an impaired ability to form peptidyl 4-hydroxyproline and peptidyl 5-hydroxylysine in connective tissue proteins.

Menkes syndrome is characterized by kinky hair and growth retardation. Menkes syndrome results from a dietary deficiency of copper. Copper is an essential cofactor for the enzyme lysyl oxidase. Lysyl oxidase functions in the formation of the covalent cross-links that strengthen collagen fibers.

Genetic disorders of collagen biosynthesis include several forms of osteogenesis imperfecta. Osteogenesis imperfecta is characterized by fragile bones. Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is a group of connective tissue disorders. Ehlers-Danlos syndrome results in mobile joints and skin abnormalities. Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is due to defects in the genes that encode enzymes such as procollagen-lysine 5-hydroxylase.

# NUTRITIONALLY ESSENTIAL & NUTRITIONALLY NONESSENTIAL AMINO ACIDS

The terms 'essential' and 'nonessential' when applied to dietary amino acids can be misleading. All 20 common amino acids are essential for human health. Of these 20 amino acids, 8 must be present in the human diet and are termed 'nutritionally essential.' The other 12 amino acids are termed 'nutritionally nonessential.' Nutritionally nonessential amino acids are metabolically essential and do not need to be present in the diet. The distinction between these two classes was established in the 1930s through experiments where human subjects were fed a diet with purified amino acids replacing protein.

Amino acid deficiency disorders are common in certain regions of West Africa. Diets in certain regions of West Africa are heavily reliant on grains that are poor in tryptophan and lysine. Amino acid deficiency disorders include kwashiorkor. Kwashiorkor occurs when a child is weaned onto a starchy diet lacking in protein. Amino acid deficiency disorders include marasmus. Marasmus occurs where both caloric intake and specific amino acids are deficient.

The existence of nutritional requirements indicates that relying on an external source of a nutrient can be more advantageous for survival than the ability to biosynthesize it. If the diet provides ample quantities of a nutrient, retaining the ability to biosynthesize it becomes unnecessary. The chapter focuses on the reactions and intermediates involved in the biosynthesis of the 12 nutritionally nonessential amino acids in human tissues. The chapter highlights selected medically significant disorders related to their metabolism. The chapter does not cover the reactions by which organisms like plants and bacteria, but not humans, form certain amino acids.

## Cysteine

Cysteine is not nutritionally essential. Cysteine is formed from methionine, which is nutritionally essential. Methionine converts to homocysteine. Homocysteine and serine form cystathionine. Cystathionine hydrolysis forms cysteine and homoserine (Figure 27–10).

## Tyrosine

Phenylalanine hydroxylase converts phenylalanine to tyrosine (Figure 27-11). Phenylalanine is a nutritionally essential amino acid. If the diet contains adequate quantities of phenylalanine, tyrosine is nutritionally nonessential. The phenylalanine hydroxylase reaction is irreversible. Dietary tyrosine cannot replace phenylalanine. Tetrahydrobiopterin provides the reducing power for this reaction. The reducing power ultimately derives from NADPH. Activity II catalyzes the reduction of dihydrobiopterin by NADPH. The reaction is associated with several defects of phenylalanine metabolism discussed in Chapter 29.

Activity II catalyzes reduction of dihydrobiopterin by NADPH, and activity I the reduction of O2 to H2O and of phenylalanine to tyrosine. ## Hydroxyproline & Hydroxylysine

Hydroxyproline and hydroxylysine occur principally in collagen. There is no transfer RNA (tRNA) for either hydroxylated amino acid. Dietary hydroxyproline is not incorporated during protein synthesis. Dietary hydroxylysine is not incorporated during protein synthesis. Peptidyl hydroxyproline arises from proline after it has been incorporated into peptides. Peptidyl hydroxylysine arises from lysine after it has been incorporated into peptides. Hydroxylation of peptidyl prolyl residues is catalyzed by prolyl hydroxylase. Hydroxylation of peptidyl lysyl residues is catalyzed by lysyl hydroxylase. Prolyl hydroxylase and lysyl hydroxylase are found in skin, skeletal muscle, and granulating wounds. Hydroxylases are mixed-function oxidases. A deficiency of vitamin C results in scurvy. Scurvy results in bleeding gums. Scurvy results in swelling joints. Scurvy results in impaired wound healing. Impaired stability of collagen results from impaired hydroxylation. Procollagen-proline 4-hydroxylase (EC 1.14.11.2) is a mixed-function oxidase. Procollagen-lysine 5-hydroxylase (EC 1.14.11.4) catalyzes an analogous reaction. (Figure 27-12)

## Selenocysteine, the 21st Amino Acid

Selenocysteine is present in at least 25 human selenoproteins. Selenocysteine is located at the active site of several human enzymes that catalyze redox reactions. Thioredoxin reductase is an example of an enzyme that contains selenocysteine. Glutathione peroxidase is another example of an enzyme that contains selenocysteine. The deiodinase enzyme, which converts thyroxine to triiodothyronine, also contains selenocysteine. Selenocysteine participates in the catalytic mechanism of these enzymes. Replacing selenocysteine with cysteine can reduce catalytic activity. Impairments in human selenoproteins have been implicated in tumorigenesis. Impairments in human selenoproteins have been associated with atherosclerosis. Selenium deficiency cardiomyopathy, known as Keshan disease, is linked to impairments in human selenoproteins.

[FIGURE:fig-27-13]

Selenocysteine (top) and the reaction catalyzed by selenophosphate synthetase (EC 2.7.9.3) (bottom).

Serine provides the carbon skeleton of selenocysteine. Selenophosphate serves as the selenium donor. Selenophosphate is formed from ATP and selenate. Unlike 4-hydroxyproline or 5-hydroxylysine, selenocysteine arises cotranslationally during its incorporation into peptides. The protein synthetic apparatus identifies a selenocysteine-specific UGA codon using the selenocysteine insertion element. The selenocysteine insertion element is a stem-loop structure in the untranslated region of the messenger RNA (mRNA). The serine oxygen is replaced by selenium involving selenophosphate formed by selenophosphate synthetase.

All vertebrates can form certain amino acids from amphibolic intermediates or from other dietary amino acids.
Cysteine, tyrosine, and hydroxylysine are formed from nutritionally essential amino acids. Serine provides the carbon skeleton and homocysteine the sulfur for cysteine biosynthesis.
In scurvy, a nutritional disease that results from a deficiency of vitamin C, impaired hydroxylation of peptidyl proline and peptidyl lysine results in a failure to form stabilizing cross-links in maturing collagen.
Phenylalanine hydroxylase converts phenylalanine to tyrosine. Since the reaction catalyzed by this mixed function oxidase is thermodynamically disfavored, tyrosine cannot give rise to phenylalanine in vivo.
Peptidyl hydroxyproline and hydroxylysine are formed posttranslationally by hydroxylation of peptidyl proline or lysine in reactions catalyzed by mixed-function oxidases that require vitamin C as cofactor.
Selenocysteine, an essential active site residue in several mammalian enzymes, arises by cotranslational insertion from a previously modified tRNA.
