# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

The survival of multicellular organisms depends on their ability to adapt to a constantly changing environment. Intercellular communication mechanisms are necessary for this adaptation. The nervous system and the endocrine system provide intercellular, organism-wide communication. The nervous system was originally viewed as providing a fixed communication system. The endocrine system supplied hormones, which are mobile messages. There is a remarkable convergence of these regulatory systems. Neural regulation of the endocrine system is important in the production and secretion of some hormones. Many neurotransmitters resemble hormones in their synthesis, transport, and mechanism of action. Many hormones are synthesized in the nervous system. The word â€«hormoneâ€» is derived from a Greek term â€«hormoâ€» that means to excite or arouse to activity. As classically defined, a hormone is a substance that is synthesized in one organ and transported by the circulatory system to act on another tissue. Hormones can act on adjacent cells through paracrine action. Hormones can act on the cell in which they were synthesized through autocrine action. Hormones do not need to enter the systemic circulation for their action. A diverse array of hormones has evolved to provide homeostatic responses. Each hormone has distinctive mechanisms of action. Each hormone has properties of biosynthesis, storage, secretion, transport, and metabolism. Biochemical diversity is the topic of this chapter.

# THE TARGET CELL CONCEPT

There are over 200 types of differentiated cells in humans. Only a few of these cells produce hormones. Virtually all of the 75 trillion cells in a human are targets of one or more of the 50+ known hormones. The concept of the target cell is a useful way of looking at hormone action. It was once thought that hormones affected a single cell type or only a few kinds of cells. It was also thought that a hormone elicited a unique biochemical or physiologic action. We now know that a given hormone can affect several different cell types. More than one hormone can affect a given cell type. Hormones can exert many different effects in one cell or in different cells. The discovery of specific cell surface and intracellular hormone receptors has expanded the definition of a target. A target cell is now defined as any cell in which the hormone (ligand) binds to its receptor, whether or not a biochemical or physiologic response has yet been determined.

Several factors determine the response of a target cell to a hormone. These factors can be categorized into two general ways: (1) factors that affect the concentration of the hormone at the target cell (Table 41–1) and (2) factors that affect the actual response of the target cell to the hormone (Table 41–2).

Determinants of the Concentration of a Hormone at the Target Cell

The rate of synthesis and secretion of the hormones determines the concentration of a hormone at the target cell.
The proximity of the target cell to the hormone source, which affects dilution, determines the concentration of a hormone at the target cell.
The conversion of inactive or suboptimally active forms of the hormone into the fully active form determines the concentration of a hormone at the target cell.
The rate of clearance of hormone from plasma, by other tissues, or by degradation, metabolism, or excretion determines the concentration of a hormone at the target cell.

The number of specific receptors on the plasma membrane or in the cytoplasm or nucleus determines the target cell response. The relative activity of these receptors influences the target cell response. The state of occupancy of the specific receptors affects the target cell response. The metabolism of the hormone in the target cell, whether activation or inactivation, influences the target cell response. The presence of other factors within the cell that are necessary for the hormone response affects the target cell response. The up- or downregulation of the receptor consequent to the interaction with its ligand influences the target cell response. Postreceptor desensitization of the cell, including downregulation of the receptor, affects the target cell response.

## Receptors Discriminate Precisely

The high degree of discrimination is provided by cell-associated recognition molecules called receptors.

Hormones initiate their biological effects by binding to hormone-specific receptors.

Hormone-induced actions generally, but not always, terminate when the effector dissociates from the receptor (see Figure 38-1; type A response).

A target cell is defined by its ability to selectively bind a given hormone to its cognate receptor.

For hormone-receptor interactions to be physiologically relevant, binding should be specific, that is, displaceable by agonist or antagonist.

For hormone-receptor interactions to be physiologically relevant, binding should be saturable.

For hormone-receptor interactions to be physiologically relevant, binding should occur within the concentration range of the expected biological response.

## Both Recognition & Coupling Domains Occur on Receptors

All receptors have at least two functional domains.

A recognition domain binds the hormone ligand.

A second region generates a signal that couples hormone recognition to some intracellular function.

Polypeptide and protein hormones and the catecholamines bind to receptors located in the plasma membrane.

Polypeptide and protein hormones and the catecholamines generate a signal that regulates various intracellular functions, often by changing the activity of an enzyme.

Lipophilic steroid, retinoid, and thyroid hormones interact with intracellular receptors.

The ligand-receptor complex within the nucleus directly provides the signal, generally to specific genes whose rate of transcription is thereby affected.

The domains responsible for hormone recognition and signal generation have been identified in the protein polypeptide and catecholamine hormone receptors.

Steroid, thyroid, and retinoid hormone receptors have multiple functional domains.

One domain binds the hormone.

Another domain binds to specific DNA regions.

A third domain is involved in the interaction with various coregulator proteins that result in the activation or repression of gene transcription.

A fourth domain may specify binding to one or more other proteins that influence the intracellular trafficking of the receptor.

The dual functions of binding and coupling ultimately define a receptor.

The coupling of hormone binding to signal transduction provides the first step in amplification of the hormonal response.

The dual functions of binding and coupling distinguish the target cell receptor from the plasma carrier proteins that bind hormone but do not generate a signal.

## Receptors Are Proteins

The insulin receptor subunits are linked by multiple disulfide bonds. The receptors for insulin-like growth factor I (IGF-I) are generally similar in structure to the insulin receptor. The receptors for epidermal growth factor (EGF) are generally similar in structure to the insulin receptor. The growth hormone (GH) receptors span the plasma membrane of target cells. The prolactin (PRL) receptors span the plasma membrane of target cells. The growth hormone (GH) receptors do not contain intrinsic protein kinase activity. The prolactin (PRL) receptors do not contain intrinsic protein kinase activity. Ligand binding to the growth hormone (GH) receptors results in the association and activation of the Jak-Stat pathway. Ligand binding to the prolactin (PRL) receptors results in the association and activation of the Jak-Stat pathway. Polypeptide hormones transduce signals by altering the rate of production of cAMP through G-proteins. Catecholamine receptors transduce signals by altering the rate of production of cAMP through G-proteins. G-proteins are characterized by the presence of seven membrane-spanning domains. Protein kinase activation is a downstream action of this class of receptor. The generation of cyclic AMP (cAMP, 3'5'-adenylic acid) is a downstream action of this class of receptor. A comparison of several different steroid receptors with thyroid hormone receptors revealed a remarkable conservation of the amino acid sequence in certain regions, particularly in the DNA-binding domains. Receptors of the steroid and thyroid type are members of a large superfamily of nuclear receptors. Many related members of this family currently have no known ligand and are called orphan receptors. The nuclear receptor superfamily plays a critical role in the regulation of gene transcription by hormones.

The hormones in group I are lipophilic. After secretion, these hormones associate with plasma transport or carrier proteins. The association with plasma transport or carrier proteins circumvents the problem of solubility while prolonging the plasma half-life of the hormone. The relative percentages of bound and free hormone are determined by the amount, binding affinity, and binding capacity of the transport protein. The free hormone is the biologically active form. The free hormone readily traverses the lipophilic plasma membrane of all cells. The free hormone encounters receptors in either the cytosol or nucleus of target cells. The ligand-receptor complex is the intracellular messenger in this group.

The second major group consists of water-soluble hormones. Water-soluble hormones bind to specific receptors spanning the plasma membrane of the target cell. Hormones that bind to these surface receptors communicate with intracellular metabolic processes through intermediary molecules called second messengers. The hormone itself is the first messenger. The second messenger concept arose from an observation that epinephrine binds to the plasma membrane of certain cells and increases intracellular cAMP. cAMP mediates the effects of many hormones. ## Hormones Are Synthesized in a Variety of Cellular Arrangements

Hormones are synthesized in discrete organs designed solely for this specific purpose. The adrenal gland produces glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids. The pituitary gland produces thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH], follicle-stimulating hormone [FSH], luteinizing hormone [LH], growth hormone [GH], PRL, and adrenocorticotropic hormone [ACTH].

Some organs perform two distinct but closely related functions. The ovaries produce mature oocytes and the reproductive hormones estradiol and progesterone. The testes produce mature spermatozoa and testosterone.

Hormones are also produced in specialized cells within other organs. The small intestine produces glucagon-like peptide. The thyroid produces calcitonin. The kidney produces angiotensin II.

The synthesis of some hormones requires the parenchymal cells of more than one organ.

The diversity in the approach to hormone synthesis has evolved to fulfill a specific purpose.

Testosterone is an obligatory intermediate in the formation of dihydrotestosterone (DHT).

## Hormones Are Synthesized & Modified for Full Activity in a Variety of Ways

Some hormones are synthesized in final form and secreted immediately. Hormones derived from cholesterol are included in this class. Some hormones, such as catecholamines, are synthesized in final form and stored in the producing cells. Insulin is synthesized as a precursor molecule in the producing cell. Insulin is then processed and secreted upon a physiologic cue, such as plasma glucose concentrations. All of these examples are discussed in more detail in the following discussion.

## Adrenal Steroidogenesis

The adrenal steroid hormones are synthesized from cholesterol, which is mostly derived from the plasma. A small portion of cholesterol is synthesized in situ from acetyl-CoA via mevalonate and squalene. Much of the cholesterol in the adrenal is esterified and stored in cytoplasmic lipid droplets. Upon stimulation of the adrenal by ACTH, an esterase is activated. The free cholesterol formed is transported into the mitochondrion. A cytochrome P450 side chain cleavage enzyme (P450scc) converts cholesterol to pregnenolone in the mitochondrion. Cleavage of the side chain involves removal of the six-carbon fragment isocaproaldehyde to give the 21-carbon steroid (Figure 41–3, top). An ACTH-dependent steroidogenic acute regulatory (StAR) protein is essential for the transport of cholesterol to P450scc in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

## Mineralocorticoid Synthesis

Synthesis of aldosterone follows the mineralocorticoid pathway and occurs in the zona glomerulosa. Corticosterone has glucocorticoid activity and is a weak mineralocorticoid. Corticosterone has less than 5% of the potency of aldosterone. In some species, such as rodents, corticosterone is the most potent glucocorticoid. The zona glomerulosa does not have the smooth endoplasmic reticulum enzyme 17α-hydroxylase. The zona glomerulosa has a mitochondrial 18-hydroxylase. The 18-hydroxylase (aldosterone synthase) acts on corticosterone to form 18-hydroxycorticosterone. 18-hydroxycorticosterone is changed to aldosterone by conversion of the 18-alcohol to an aldehyde.

## Glucocorticoid Synthesis

Cortisol synthesis requires three hydroxylases located in the fasciculata and reticularis zones of the adrenal cortex. The first two reactions are rapid. When 17α-hydroxylase is impeded, the mineralocorticoid pathway is followed. Cortisol is the most potent natural glucocorticoid hormone in humans. 21-Hydroxylase is a smooth endoplasmic reticulum enzyme. 11β-Hydroxylase is a mitochondrial enzyme. Steroidogenesis involves the repeated shuttling of substrates into and out of the mitochondria.

## Androgen Synthesis

The major androgen or androgen precursor produced by the adrenal cortex is dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA). Most 17-hydroxypregnenolone follows the glucocorticoid pathway. A small fraction of 17-hydroxypregnenolone is subjected to oxidative fission and removal of the two-carbon side chain through the action of 17,20-lyase. The lyase activity is part of the same enzyme (P450c17) that catalyzes 17α-hydroxylation. P450c17 is a dual-function protein. The lyase activity is important in both the adrenals and the gonads. The lyase activity acts exclusively on 17α-hydroxy-containing molecules. Adrenal androgen production increases markedly if glucocorticoid biosynthesis is impeded by the lack of one of the hydroxylases, as in adrenogenital syndrome. DHEA is a prohormone. Small amounts of androstenedione are also formed in the adrenal by the action of the lyase on 17α-hydroxyprogesterone. Testosterone is the most potent adrenal androgen. Small amounts of testosterone are produced in the adrenal by this mechanism. Most of this conversion occurs in the testes.

## Testicular Steroidogenesis

Testicular androgens are synthesized in the interstitial tissue by the Leydig cells. The immediate precursor of the gonadal steroids is cholesterol. The rate-limiting step in steroidogenesis is the delivery of cholesterol to the inner membrane of the mitochondria. The transport protein StAR facilitates the delivery of cholesterol to the inner membrane of the mitochondria. Once cholesterol reaches the proper location, it is acted upon by the side chain cleavage enzyme P450scc. The conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone is identical in the adrenal, ovary, and testis. In the testis and ovary, the reaction is promoted by LH rather than ACTH.

The conversion of pregnenolone to testosterone requires the action of five enzyme activities contained in three proteins: 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3β-OHSD) and Δ^{5,4} -isomerase; 17α-hydroxylase and 17,20-lyase; and 17β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (17β-OHSD). The conversion of pregnenolone to testosterone is referred to as the progesterone (or Δ^{4} ) pathway. Pregnenolone can also be converted to testosterone by the dehydroepiandrosterone (or Δ^{5} ) pathway. The Δ^{5} route appears to be most used in human testes. ## DHT Is Formed From Testosterone in Peripheral Tissues

Testosterone is metabolized by two pathways. One pathway involves oxidation at the 17-position. The other pathway involves reduction of the A ring double bond and the 3-ketone. The first pathway occurs in many tissues, including the liver. The first pathway produces 17-ketosteroids that are generally inactive or less active than testosterone. The second pathway is less efficient. The second pathway occurs primarily in target tissues. The second pathway produces the potent metabolite DHT.

The most significant metabolic product of testosterone is DHT. In many tissues, including the prostate, external genitalia, and some areas of the skin, DHT is the active form of the hormone. The plasma content of DHT in the adult male is about one-tenth that of testosterone. Approximately 400 micrograms of DHT are produced daily, compared with about 5 milligrams of testosterone. About 50 to 100 micrograms of DHT are secreted by the testes. The rest of the DHT is produced peripherally from testosterone. The peripheral production of DHT is catalyzed by the NADPH-dependent 5 alpha-reductase (Figure 41-6).

Fish liver oil and egg yolk contain this precursor. Ultraviolet light mediates the conversion of 7-dehydrocholesterol. The conversion of 7-dehydrocholesterol is a nonenzymatic photolysis reaction. ## Catecholamines Are Synthesized in Final Form & Stored in Secretion Granules

Dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine are synthesized from tyrosine in the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla. Epinephrine is the major product of the adrenal medulla. Epinephrine constitutes about 80% of the catecholamines in the medulla. Epinephrine is not made in extramedullary tissue. Most of the norepinephrine present in organs innervated by sympathetic nerves is made in situ. About 80% of the total norepinephrine is made in situ. The rest of the norepinephrine is made in other nerve endings and reaches the target sites via the circulation. Epinephrine and norepinephrine may be produced and stored in different cells in the adrenal medulla. Epinephrine and norepinephrine may be produced and stored in different cells in other chromaffin tissues.

The conversion of tyrosine to epinephrine requires four sequential steps: ring hydroxylation, decarboxylation, side chain hydroxylation to form norepinephrine, and N-methylation to form epinephrine. Tyrosine hydroxylase is the rate-limiting enzyme in catecholamine biosynthesis. Tyrosine hydroxylase converts L-tyrosine to L-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-dopa). Tyrosine hydroxylase is regulated by feedback inhibition by the catecholamines. Catecholamines compete with the enzyme for the pteridine cofactor. Catecholamines cannot cross the blood–brain barrier. In the brain, catecholamines must be synthesized locally. L-Dopa, the precursor of dopamine, readily crosses the blood–brain barrier. ## Phenylethanolamine-N-Methyltransferase (PNMT) Catalyzes the Production of Epinephrine

Phenylethanolamine-N-Methyltransferase (PNMT) catalyzes the N-methylation of norepinephrine to form epinephrine. PNMT is soluble, indicating that the norepinephrine-to-epinephrine conversion occurs in the cytoplasm. The synthesis of PNMT is induced by glucocorticoid hormones. Glucocorticoid hormones reach the medulla via the intra-adrenal portal system. The intra-adrenal portal system provides a 100-fold steroid concentration gradient over systemic arterial blood. The high intra-adrenal concentration is necessary for the induction of PNMT.

## T3 & T4 Illustrate the Diversity in Hormone Synthesis

Triiodothyronine and tetraiodothyronine require iodine for bioactivity. Triiodothyronine and tetraiodothyronine are synthesized as part of a very large precursor molecule called thyroglobulin. Triiodothyronine and tetraiodothyronine are stored in an intracellular reservoir known as colloid.

Iodine is a scarce component of soil in most parts of the world. For that reason, there is little iodine in food. A complex mechanism has evolved to acquire and retain this crucial element and to convert it into a form suitable for incorporation into organic compounds. The thyroid must synthesize thyronine from tyrosine. The synthesis of thyronine from tyrosine takes place in thyroglobulin.

Thyroglobulin is a large iodinated, glycosylated protein with a molecular mass of 660 kDa. Carbohydrate accounts for 8 to 10% of the weight of thyroglobulin. Iodide accounts for about 0.2 to 1% of the weight of thyroglobulin, depending on the iodine content in the diet. Thyroglobulin is composed of two large subunits. Thyroglobulin contains 115 tyrosine residues, each of which is a potential site of iodination. About 70% of the iodide in thyroglobulin exists in the inactive precursors, monoiodotyrosine (MIT) and diiodotyrosine (DIT). In iodine deficiency, the DIT:MIT ratio decreases. Thyroglobulin, a large molecule of about 5000 amino acids, provides the conformation required for tyrosyl coupling and iodide organization necessary in the formation of the diaminoacid thyroid hormones. Thyroglobulin is synthesized in the basal portion of the cell. Several weeks' supply of these hormones exist in the normal thyroid.

Within minutes after stimulation of the thyroid by TSH, colloid reenters the cell and there is a marked increase of phagolysosome activity. Thyroglobulin is thus a very large prohormone. (Figure 41-11)

## Iodide Metabolism Involves Several Discrete Steps

The thyroid can concentrate I- against a strong electrochemical gradient. The process is energy-dependent and linked to the Na+-K+-ATPase-dependent thyroidal I- transporter. The T:S ratio reflects the activity of the thyroidal I- transporter. The T:S ratio is primarily controlled by TSH. In humans on a normal iodine diet, the T:S ratio is about 25:1. In animals chronically stimulated with TSH, the T:S ratio can reach 500:1. In hypophysectomized animals, the T:S ratio is 5:1 or less.

The thyroid is the only tissue that can oxidize I- to a higher valence state. Oxidizing I- is an essential step in I- organization and thyroid hormone biosynthesis. Oxidizing I- involves a heme-containing peroxidase. Oxidizing I- occurs at the luminal surface of the follicular cell. Thyroperoxidase is a tetrameric protein with a molecular mass of 60 kDa. Thyroperoxidase requires hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as an oxidizing agent. H2O2 is produced by an NADPH-dependent enzyme resembling cytochrome c reductase. Thiourea drugs inhibit I- oxidation. Thiourea drugs are used as antithyroid drugs because of this inhibition. Once iodination occurs, the iodine does not readily leave the thyroid. Free tyrosine can be iodinated. Iodinated tyrosine is not incorporated into proteins since no tRNA recognizes it.

The coupling of two DIT molecules to form T4 or of an MIT and DIT to form T3 occurs within the thyroglobulin molecule. No separate coupling enzyme has been found. The coupling of two DIT molecules to form T4 or of an MIT and DIT to form T3 is an oxidative process. The same thyroperoxidase catalyzes this reaction by stimulating free radical formation of iodotyrosine. The same drugs that inhibit I- oxidation also inhibit coupling. The formed thyroid hormones remain as integral parts of thyroglobulin until the thyroglobulin is degraded.

A deiodinase removes I- from the inactive mono and diiodothyronine molecules in the thyroid. A deiodinase provides a substantial amount of the I- used in T3 and T4 biosynthesis. A peripheral deiodinase in target tissues such as the pituitary, kidney, and liver selectively removes I- from the 5' position of T4 to make T3. T4 can be thought of as a prohormone. T4 does have some intrinsic activity.

## Several Hormones Are Made From Larger Peptide Precursors

Formation of the critical disulfide bridges in insulin requires that this hormone be first synthesized as part of a larger precursor molecule, proinsulin.

Proinsulin is a precursor molecule for insulin.

The thyroid hormones are formed in the context of a much larger molecule.

Several other hormones are synthesized as parts of large precursor molecules.

Several other hormones are synthesized not because of some special structural requirement but rather as a mechanism for controlling the available amount of the active hormone.

Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is an example of a hormone synthesized as part of a larger precursor molecule for regulation.

Angiotensin II is another example of a hormone synthesized as part of a larger precursor molecule for regulation.

Pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) is a protein that can be processed into many different hormones in a tissue-specific manner.

The processing of POMC into different hormones is an example of tissue-specific regulation.

## Insulin Is Synthesized as a Preprohormone & Modified Within the β Cell

Insulin is synthesized as a preprohormone with a molecular weight of approximately 11,500, which is the prototype for peptides processed from larger precursor molecules.

The preprohormone has a hydrophobic 23-amino-acid pre-, or leader, sequence that directs the molecule into the cisternae of the endoplasmic reticulum and is then removed.

The removal of the leader sequence results in the 9000-MW proinsulin molecule, which provides the conformation necessary for the proper and efficient formation of the disulfide bridges.

The sequence of proinsulin, starting from the amino terminus, is B chain—connecting (C) peptide—A chain.

Proinsulin undergoes a series of site-specific peptide cleavages that result in the formation of equimolar amounts of mature insulin and C-peptide.

An initial cleavage by a trypsin-like enzyme followed by several cleavages by a carboxypeptidase-like enzyme results in the production of the heterodimeric (AB) insulin molecule, which is held together by two intrapeptide cysteine disulfide bonds (Figure 41-12).

## Angiotensin II Is Also Synthesized From a Large Precursor

The renin-angiotensin system regulates blood pressure and electrolyte metabolism. Angiotensin II is an octapeptide involved in these processes. Renin is an enzyme produced in the juxtaglomerular cells of the renal afferent arteriole. Juxtaglomerular cells are sensitive to blood pressure changes. Physiologic regulators of renin release act through renal baroreceptors. Factors that decrease fluid volume, such as dehydration, decreased blood pressure, or fluid or blood loss, stimulate renin release. Renal sympathetic nerves mediate the central nervous system and postural effects on renin release independently of the baroreceptor and salt effects. Renin acts on angiotensinogen to produce the decapeptide angiotensin I.

Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) is a glycoprotein found in the lung, endothelial cells, and plasma. ACE removes two carboxyl terminal amino acids from angiotensin I to form angiotensin II. The step is not thought to be rate-limiting. Various nonapeptide analogs of angiotensin I and other compounds act as competitive inhibitors of converting enzyme. These inhibitors are used to treat renin-dependent hypertension and are referred to as ACE inhibitors. Angiotensin II increases blood pressure by causing vasoconstriction of the arteriole. Angiotensin II is a very potent vasoactive substance. Angiotensin II inhibits renin release from the juxtaglomerular cells. Angiotensin II is a potent stimulator of aldosterone production.

In some species, angiotensin II is converted to the heptapeptide angiotensin III. Angiotensin III is an equally potent stimulator of aldosterone production. In humans, the plasma level of angiotensin II is four times greater than that of angiotensin III. Most effects are exerted by the octapeptide angiotensin II. Angiotensins II and III are rapidly inactivated by angiotensinases.

Angiotensin II binds to specific receptors on adrenal cortex glomerulosa cells. The hormone-receptor interaction does not activate adenylyl cyclase. cAMP does not mediate the action of angiotensin II. Angiotensin II stimulates the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone and of corticosterone to 18-hydroxycorticosterone and aldosterone. Angiotensin II may involve changes in the concentration of intracellular calcium and phospholipid metabolites. The mechanisms are similar to those described in Chapter 42.

# THERE IS SIGNIFICANT VARIATION IN THE STORAGE & SECRETION OF HORMONES

Steroid hormones are synthesized in their final active form. Steroid hormones are secreted as they are made. There is no intracellular reservoir of steroid hormones. Catecholamines are synthesized in active form. Catecholamines are stored in granules in the chromaffin cells in the adrenal medulla. Catecholamines are released into the circulation through exocytosis in response to neural stimulation. A several-hour reserve supply of catecholamines exists in the chromaffin cells.

PTH exists in storage vesicles. Up to 80 to 90% of proPTH synthesized is degraded before entering the final storage compartment. A several-hour supply of PTH is stored in the parathyroid cells.

The human pancreas secretes about 40 to 50 units of insulin daily. Insulin and C-peptide are secreted in equimolar amounts. Glucose provokes insulin secretion. Glucose triggers the processing of proinsulin to insulin as part of the secretory response.

# SOME HORMONES HAVE PLASMA TRANSPORT PROTEINS

Class I hormones are hydrophobic in chemical nature. Class I hormones are not very soluble in plasma. Class I hormones include steroids and thyroid hormones. Specialized plasma transport proteins are required for these hormones to overcome solubility issues. Specialized plasma transport proteins deliver the hormone to the target cell. The transport proteins provide a circulating reservoir of the hormone. This reservoir can be substantial, as seen with thyroid hormones. When hormones are bound to transport proteins, they cannot be metabolized. The binding affinity of a hormone to its transporter determines the ratio of bound to free hormone. Only the free form of a hormone is biologically active. It is important to distinguish between plasma transport proteins and hormone receptors. Both plasma transport proteins and hormone receptors bind hormones, but they have very different characteristics (Table 41-6).

## Gonadal Steroids Are Transported by Sex Hormone-Binding Globulin

Many conditions that affect SHBG production also affect the production of CBG and TBG. SHBG and albumin bind 97 to 99% of circulating testosterone. Only a small fraction of circulating testosterone is in the free (biologically active) form. The primary function of SHBG is to restrict the free concentration of testosterone in the serum. Testosterone binds to SHBG with higher affinity than estradiol does. A change in the level of SHBG causes a greater change in the free testosterone level than in the free estradiol level. Estrogens are bound to SHBG. Progestins are bound to CBG. SHBG binds estradiol about five times less avidly than it binds testosterone or DHT. Progesterone has little affinity for SHBG. CBG has even less affinity for testosterone, DHT, or estrone. These binding proteins provide a circulating reservoir of hormone. The large binding capacity of these proteins probably buffers against sudden changes in the plasma level. The metabolic clearance rates of these steroids are inversely related to the affinity of their binding to SHBG. Estrone is cleared more rapidly than estradiol. Estradiol is cleared more rapidly than testosterone or DHT.
