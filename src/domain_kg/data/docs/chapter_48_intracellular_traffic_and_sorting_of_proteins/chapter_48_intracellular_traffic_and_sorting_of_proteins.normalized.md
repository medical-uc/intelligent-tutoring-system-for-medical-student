# SIGNAL SEQUENCES TARGET MANY PROTEINS TO THEIR DESTINATIONS

The protein biosynthetic pathways in cells can be considered to be one large sorting system. Many proteins carry signals, usually specific sequences of amino acids known as targeting sequences, that direct them to their specific subcellular destinations. Targeting sequences are a fundamental component of the sorting system. Signal sequences are recognized by and interact with complementary areas of other proteins that serve as receptors.

Proteins with altered sequences are unable to enter the lumen of the ER, while nonmembrane proteins with added signal peptides are able to insert into the lumen. The involvement of signal peptides in protein translocation across membranes is supported by this.

A major sorting decision is made early in protein biosynthesis, when specific proteins are synthesized either on cytosolic (free) polyribosomes or polyribosomes bound to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (membrane-bound polyribosomes). ER regions containing attached polyribosomes are called the rough ER (RER). The distinction between the two types of ribosomes results in two branches of the protein-sorting pathway, called the cytosolic branch and the RER branch.

Proteins synthesized by cytosolic polyribosomes are directed to mitochondria, nuclei, and peroxisomes by specific signals, or remain in the cytosol if they lack a signal. Any protein that contains a targeting sequence that is subsequently removed is designated as a preprotein. In some cases, a second peptide is also removed, and in that event the original, newly synthesized protein is known as a preproprotein, such as preproalbumin.

Proteins synthesized and sorted in the RER branch include many destined for various membranes, such as the ER, Golgi apparatus (GA), plasma membrane (PM), and also lysosomal enzymes. In addition, proteins for export from the cell via exocytosis (secretion) are synthesized via this route. Proteins synthesized and sorted in the RER branch may reside in the membranes or lumen of the ER, or follow the major transport route of intracellular proteins to the GA. In the secretory or exocytotic pathway, proteins are transported from the ER to the GA to the PM and then released into the external environment. Secretion may be constitutive, meaning that transport occurs continuously, or regulated, where transport is switched on and off as required.

Proteins destined for the GA, the PM, certain other sites, or for constitutive secretion are carried in transport vesicles (Figure 48–1). Other proteins that are subject to regulated secretion are carried in secretory vesicles (Figure 48–1). Secretory vesicles are particularly prominent in the pancreas and certain other glands. Mannose 6-phosphate acts as a signal for transporting enzymes to lysosomes (see Chapter 46).

## The Golgi Apparatus Is Involved in Glycosylation & Sorting of Proteins

The Golgi apparatus (GA) plays two major roles in protein synthesis. The GA is involved in the processing of the oligosaccharide chains of membrane and other N-linked glycoproteins. The GA contains enzymes involved in O-glycosylation. The GA is involved in the sorting of various proteins prior to their delivery to their appropriate intracellular destinations. The GA consists of cis-cisternae, medial cisternae, trans-cisternae, and the trans-Golgi network (TGN). The cis-cisternae face the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). All parts of the GA participate in oligosaccharide chain processing. The TGN is particularly involved in protein sorting. The TGN is very rich in vesicles.

## Chaperones Are Proteins That Stabilize Unfolded or Partially Folded Proteins

Molecular chaperones are proteins that stabilize unfolded or partially folded intermediates. Molecular chaperones allow these intermediates time to fold covalently. Molecular chaperones prevent inappropriate interactions that could lead to the formation of nonfunctional structures. More than 20 families of these proteins are known. The major families of molecular chaperones include the heat shock proteins Hsp90, Hsp70, and Hsp60. Most chaperones exhibit ATPase activity. Most chaperones bind ADP and ATP. ATPase activity is important for their effect on protein folding. The ADP-chaperone complex often has a high affinity for the unfolded protein. The binding of the unfolded protein to the ADP-chaperone complex stimulates the replacement of ADP with ATP. The ATP-chaperone complex releases segments of the protein that have folded properly. The cycle involving ADP and ATP binding is repeated until the protein is released. Chaperones are required for the correct targeting of proteins to their subcellular locations. A number of important properties of these proteins are listed in Table 48–2.

## Some Properties of Chaperone Proteins

Chaperone proteins are present in a wide range of species from bacteria to humans. Many chaperone proteins are so-called heat shock proteins (Hsp), which are induced by stressors including heat, oxidants, toxins, free radicals, and viruses. Some chaperone proteins are inducible by conditions that cause unfolding of newly synthesized proteins, such as elevated temperature and various chemicals. Chaperone proteins bind to predominantly hydrophobic regions of unfolded proteins and prevent their aggregation. Chaperone proteins act in part as a quality control or editing mechanism for detecting misfolded or otherwise defective proteins. Most chaperones show associated ATPase activity, with ATP or ADP being involved in the protein-chaperone interaction. Chaperone proteins are found in various cellular compartments such as the cytosol, mitochondria, and the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum.

Chaperonins are a type of chaperone that enable the correct folding of denatured proteins. Chaperonins differ from chaperones in that they are much larger, being oligomers with a molecular weight of 800 kDa as compared to monomers of 70 to 100 kDa. The structure of the bacterial chaperonin GroEL/GroES has been studied in detail. GroEL/GroES has two ring-like structures, each composed of seven identical subunits, which form a barrel-like shape, and again ATP is involved in its action. The structure of GroEL/GroES allows it to sequester an unfolded protein away from other proteins, giving it time and suitable conditions to fold properly. Hsp60 is the equivalent of GroEL in eukaryotes.

# THE CYTOSOLIC SORTING BRANCH DIRECTS PROTEINS TO SUBCELLULAR ORGANELLES

Proteins synthesized via the cytosolic sorting branch either have no targeting signal if they are destined for the cytosol, or they contain a signal that enables them to be taken up into the correct subcellular organelle. Specific uptake signals direct proteins to the mitochondria, nucleus, and peroxisomes. Protein synthesis is complete before transport occurs, so these processes are termed posttranslational translocation.

There are usually three stages in the import of proteins into mitochondria, the nucleus, peroxisomes, and the ER: recognition, translocation, and maturation. Energy is required for translocation. Chaperone proteins play a part in keeping the protein unfolded and in pulling the protein through the membrane. Folding and processing of the newly translocated protein is carried out by other proteins within the organelle.

## Most Mitochondrial Proteins Are Imported

Mitochondria contain many proteins. Thirteen polypeptides are encoded by the mitochondrial genome. Thirteen polypeptides are mostly membrane components of the electron transport chain. The vast majority of mitochondrial proteins are encoded by nuclear genes. The vast majority of mitochondrial proteins are synthesized outside the mitochondria on cytosolic polyribosomes. The vast majority of mitochondrial proteins must be imported into the mitochondria.

Mitochondrial matrix proteins must pass through the outer mitochondrial membrane to reach their destination. Mitochondrial matrix proteins must pass through the inner mitochondrial membrane to reach their destination. The passage of mitochondrial matrix proteins through the outer and inner mitochondrial membranes is called translocation. Matrix proteins have an amino terminal leader sequence. The leader sequence is about 20 to 50 amino acids in length. The leader sequence is amphipathic. The leader sequence contains many hydrophobic amino acids. The leader sequence contains many positively charged amino acids, such as Lys or Arg. The leader sequence targets proteins to the matrix.

Translocation occurs posttranslationally, after the matrix preproteins are released from the cytosolic polyribosomes. Interactions with cytosolic proteins occur prior to translocation. Cytosolic proteins act as chaperones and targeting factors. Two distinct translocation complexes are situated in the outer and inner mitochondrial membranes. The translocase of the outer membrane is known as TOM. The translocase of the inner membrane is known as TIM.

The TOM complex includes proteins like Tom20/22, which act as receptors for incoming proteins. The TOM complex includes Tom40, which is a component of the transmembrane pores. The TIM complex includes proteins such as Tim23 and Tim17, which are involved in transport across the inner membrane. Proteins must be in an unfolded state to pass through these complexes. ATP-dependent binding to chaperone proteins like Hsp70 aids proteins in passing through these complexes.

Chaperones in mitochondria are involved in translocation. Chaperones in mitochondria are involved in sorting. Chaperones in mitochondria are involved in folding. Chaperones in mitochondria are involved in assembly. Chaperones in mitochondria are involved in degradation of imported proteins. A proton-motive force across the inner membrane is required for import. The positively charged leader sequence may be helped through the membrane by the negative charge in the matrix. Close apposition at contact sites between the outer and inner membranes is necessary for translocation to occur.

The presequence is cleaved by a matrix protease once the protein reaches the matrix. Contact with chaperones in the matrix, such as mt-Hsp70, ensures proper import into the matrix. Contact with chaperones in the matrix, such as mt-Hsp70, prevents misfolding or aggregation. Interaction with the chaperonin mt-Hsp60/Hsp10 system ensures proper folding. These interactions require hydrolysis of ATP to drive them.

Some proteins insert into the outer mitochondrial membrane with the help of the TOM complex. Some proteins remain in the intermembrane space. Some proteins insert into the inner membrane. Some proteins proceed into the matrix and then return to the inner membrane or intermembrane space. Some proteins contain two signaling sequences: one for entering the mitochondrial matrix and another for subsequent relocation. Certain mitochondrial proteins, like cytochrome c, do not contain presequences. Certain mitochondrial proteins locate in the intermembrane space. Other proteins contain internal presequences. Proteins employ a variety of mechanisms and routes to reach their final destinations in mitochondria.

## Transport of Macromolecules in & out of the Nucleus Involves Localization Signals

More than a million macromolecules per minute are transported between the nucleus and the cytoplasm in an active eukaryotic cell. These macromolecules include histones, ribosomal proteins and ribosomal subunits, transcription factors, and mRNA molecules. The transport is bidirectional and occurs through the nuclear pore complexes (NPCs). NPCs are complex structures with a mass approximately 15 times that of a ribosome. NPCs are composed of aggregates of about 30 different proteins. The minimal diameter of an NPC is approximately 9 nm. Molecules smaller than about 40 kDa can pass through the channel of the NPC by diffusion. Special translocation mechanisms exist for larger molecules.

Proteins to be imported into the nucleus carry a nuclear localization signal (NLS). NLSs are markedly rich in basic residues. A cargo molecule containing an NLS binds to importin. The importin-cargo complex enters the nucleus via the NPC. Ran proteins play a critical regulatory role in the import and export of proteins to and from the nucleus. Ran proteins are small monomeric nuclear GTPases. Ran proteins exist in either GTP-bound or GDP-bound states. Inside the nucleus, Ran-GTP binds to importin causing a conformational change that releases the cargo molecule. The importin-Ran-GTP complex then returns to the cytoplasm via the NPC. In the cytoplasm, the action of GTPase-accelerating protein (GAP) converts Ran-GTP to Ran-GDP. This process frees importin and enables it to bind to another cargo protein. Thus, when cargo molecules are released inside the nucleus, the importins recirculate to the cytoplasm to be used again.

Proteins similar to importins, referred to as exportins, are involved in the export of many macromolecules from the nucleus. These macromolecules include various proteins, tRNA molecules, ribosomal subunits, and certain mRNA molecules. Cargo molecules for export carry nuclear export signals (NESs). Ran proteins are involved in the export process as well. The processes of import and export share a number of common features. The family of importins and exportins are referred to as karyopherins.

Another system is involved in the translocation of the majority of mRNA molecules. mRNA molecules are exported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm as messenger ribonucleoprotein (mRNP) complexes. mRNP complexes are attached to a protein termed mRNP exporter. The mRNP exporter carries mRNP molecules through the NPC. Ran is not involved in this process.

Other small monomeric GTPases, such as ARF, Rab, Ras, and Rho, are important in various cellular processes. ARF and Rab are involved in vesicle formation and transport. Ras is involved in certain growth and differentiation processes. Rho is involved in the formation of the actin cytoskeleton. A process involving GTP and GDP is crucial in the transport of proteins across the membrane of the ER.

## Proteins Imported Into Peroxisomes Carry Unique Targeting Sequences

The peroxisome is an important organelle involved in the metabolism of many molecules, including fatty acids, plasmalogens, cholesterol, bile acids, purines, amino acids, and hydrogen peroxide. The peroxisome is bounded by a single membrane and contains more than 50 enzymes. Catalase and urate oxidase are marker enzymes for the peroxisome. Peroxisomal proteins are synthesized on cytosolic polyribosomes and fold prior to import. The pathways of import for some peroxisomal proteins and enzymes have been studied, including matrix components and membrane components. At least two peroxisomal-matrix targeting sequences (PTSs) have been discovered. One, PTS1, is a tripeptide located at the carboxyl terminal of a number of matrix proteins, including catalase. PTS1 is typically Ser-Lys-Leu (SKL), but variations of this sequence have been detected. Proteins containing PTS1 sequences form complexes with a cytosolic receptor protein called Pex5. The Pex5 complex then docks with the peroxisomal membrane protein Pex14, and the cargo protein is translocated into the lumen. The Pex5 receptor remains in the membrane where it is first ubiquitinylated by a complex of Pex2, 10, and 12 and then released from the membrane with the removal of ubiquitin by Pex 1 and 6 in an ATP-dependent process. A second peroxisome matrix targeting sequence, PTS2, is a nine amino acid sequence at the N-terminus and has been found in at least four matrix proteins, such as thiolase. Proteins containing PTS2 sequences complex with the receptor protein Pex7 rather than Pex5. The exact translocation mechanism for PTS2 is less well understood than for PTS1. The PTS1 and PTS2 signals are not cleaved after entry into the matrix. Most peroxisomal membrane proteins do not contain PTS1 or PTS2 but seem to contain other targeting sequences. The import system can handle intact oligomers, such as tetrameric catalase. Import of matrix proteins requires ATP, whereas import of membrane proteins does not. (Figure 48-4)

# THE ROUGH ER BRANCH DIRECTS PROTEINS TO THE ER PRIOR TO FURTHER SORTING

In the RER branch, proteins are synthesized on membrane-bound polyribosomes. Proteins are usually translocated into the lumen of the ER prior to further sorting (see Figure 48--1). Certain membrane proteins are transferred directly into the membrane of the ER without reaching its lumen. Some characteristics of signal peptides that direct proteins to the ER are summarized in Table 48-4.

## Some Properties of Signal Peptides Directing Proteins to the ER

Signal peptides are usually, but not always, located at the amino terminal.

Signal peptides contain approximately 12-35 amino acids.

Methionine is usually the amino-terminal amino acid in signal peptides.

Signal peptides contain a central cluster of about 6-12 hydrophobic amino acids.

The region near the N-terminus of signal peptides usually carries a net positive charge.

The amino acid residue at the cleavage site in signal peptides is variable, but residues -1 and -3 relative to the cleavage site must be small and neutral.

## Translocation of Proteins to the Endoplasmic Reticulum Lumen May Be Cotranslational or Posttranslational

Most nascent proteins destined for secretion or other organelles are transferred across the ER membrane into the lumen by the cotranslational pathway. The cotranslational pathway occurs during ongoing protein synthesis. The process of elongation of the remaining portion of the protein being synthesized facilitates passage of the nascent protein across the lipid bilayer. Proteins be kept in an unfolded state prior to entering the conducting channel is important. The pathway involves a number of specialized proteins, including the signal recognition particle (SRP), the SRP receptor (SRP-R), and the translocon (Sec 61 complex). The translocon consists of three membrane proteins that form a protein-conducting channel in the ER membrane. The channel opens only when a signal peptide is present. Closure of the channel when proteins are not being translocated prevents ions such as calcium and other molecules leaking through it, causing cell dysfunction. The process proceeds in five steps.

In Step 1, the signal sequence emerges from the ribosome and binds to the SRP. The SRP contains six proteins associated with an RNA molecule, and each of these plays a role in its function. This step temporarily stops further elongation of the polypeptide chain after some 70 amino acids have been polymerized. In Step 2, the SRP-ribosome-nascent protein complex travels to the ER membrane, where it binds to the SRP-R. The SRP-R is an ER membrane protein composed of two subunits. The SRP guides the complex to the SRP-R, preventing premature expulsion of the growing polypeptide into the cytosol. In Step 3, the hydrolysis of GTP drives the release of the SRP, translation resumes, and the signal peptide inserts into the channel in the translocon (Sec 61 complex). In Step 4, the signal peptide induces opening of the channel in the translocon by causing the plug to move. The growing polypeptide is then fully translocated across the membrane, driven by its ongoing synthesis. In Step 5, cleavage of the signal peptide by signal peptidase occurs, and the fully translocated polypeptide/protein is released into the lumen of the ER. The signal peptide is degraded by proteases. Ribosomes are released from the ER membrane and dissociate into their two types of subunits.

Many secretory proteins are N-glycosylated. N-Glycan chains, if present, are added by the enzyme oligosaccharide:protein transferase as these proteins traverse the inner part of the ER membrane—a process called cotranslational glycosylation. Subsequently, these glycoproteins are found in the lumen of the Golgi apparatus, where further changes in glycan chains occur prior to intracellular distribution or secretion.

In contrast, proteins destined to be embedded in membranes of the ER or in other membranes along the secretory pathway only partially translocate across the ER membrane. Proteins destined to be embedded in membranes of the ER or in other membranes along the secretory pathway are able to insert into the ER membrane by lateral transfer through the wall of the translocon.

Posttranslational translocation of proteins to the ER lumen does occur in eukaryotes, although it is less common than the cotranslational route. The process involves the Sec61 translocon complex, the Sec62/Sec63 complex that is also membrane bound, and chaperone proteins of the Hsp70 family. Some of these prevent the protein folding in the cytosol, but one of them, binding immunoglobulin protein (BiP) (also known as GRP78 or Hsp70), is inside the ER lumen. The protein to be translocated initially binds to the translocon, and cytosolic chaperones are released. The leading end of the peptide then binds to BiP in the lumen. ATP bound to BiP interacts with Sec62/63, ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP providing energy to move the protein forwards, while the bound BiP-ADP prevents its moving backward into the cytosol. The protein can then be pulled through by sequential binding of BiP molecules and ATP hydrolysis. When the entire protein has entered the lumen, ADP is exchanged for ATP, allowing BiP to be released. In addition to its function in protein sorting to the ER lumen, BiP promotes proper folding by preventing aggregation and will temporarily bind abnormally folded immunoglobulin heavy chains and many other proteins, preventing them from leaving the ER.

## Cotranslational Insertion Requires Stop Transfer Sequences or Internal Insertion Sequences

The amino termini of certain proteins, such as the low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor, are located on the extracytoplasmic face of the membrane. The carboxyl termini of other proteins, like the asialoglycoprotein receptor, are on the extracytoplasmic face. The dispositions of the amino and carboxyl termini are determined by the initial biosynthetic events at the ER membrane. The LDL receptor enters the ER membrane in a manner similar to a secretory protein. The signal peptide of the LDL receptor is cleaved, allowing its amino terminal to protrude into the ER lumen. The LDL receptor contains a highly hydrophobic segment that acts as a stop-transfer signal, causing its retention in the membrane. The stop-transfer sequence has its N-terminal end in the ER lumen and the C-terminal in the cytosol. The stop-transfer signal forms the single transmembrane segment of the protein and serves as its membrane-anchoring domain. The protein exits the translocon into the membrane through a lateral gate that opens and closes continuously, allowing hydrophobic sequences to enter the lipid bilayer. The small patch of ER membrane containing the newly synthesized LDL receptor buds off as a component of a transport vesicle, which eventually fuses with the plasma membrane so that the C-terminal faces the cytosol and the N-terminal faces the outside of the cell. The asialoglycoprotein receptor lacks a cleavable N-terminal signal peptide but possesses an internal insertion sequence that inserts into the membrane without being cleaved. This internal insertion sequence acts as an anchor, with its C-terminus extruded into the ER lumen. Cytochrome P450 is anchored in a similar way, but its N-terminal is extruded into the lumen. Each pair of helical segments is inserted as a hairpin. Sequences that determine the structure of a protein in a membrane are called topogenic sequences. The LDL receptor is an example of a type I transmembrane protein, the asialoglycoprotein receptor is a type II transmembrane protein, and the glucose transporter is a type IV transmembrane protein. Cytochrome P450 is an example of a type III protein that remains in the ER membrane. Type I transmembrane proteins, such as the LDL receptor and influenza hemagglutinin, cross the membrane once and have their N-termini in the ER lumen or cell exterior. Type II transmembrane proteins, such as the asialoglycoprotein and transferrin receptors, also cross the membrane once but have their C-termini in the ER lumen or cell exterior. Type III transmembrane proteins, such as cytochrome P450, have a disposition similar to type I proteins but do not contain a cleavable signal peptide. Type IV transmembrane proteins, such as G-protein–coupled receptors and glucose transporters, cross the membrane multiple times. G-protein–coupled receptors cross the membrane 7 times, and glucose transporters cross 12 times. ## Other Routes Include Retention in the GA With Retrieval to the ER & Also Retrograde Transport From the GA

Certain proteins contain the amino acid sequence KDEL (Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu) at their carboxyl terminal. KDEL-containing proteins travel to the Golgi apparatus (GA) in vesicles coated with coat protein II (COPII). The process of KDEL-containing proteins traveling to the Golgi apparatus (GA) in vesicles coated with coat protein II (COPII) is known as anterograde vesicular transport. In the Golgi apparatus (GA), these proteins interact with a specific KDEL receptor protein. The interaction with the KDEL receptor protein retains them transiently in the Golgi apparatus (GA). After transient retention, these proteins return to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in vesicles coated with coat protein I (COPI). The return process of these proteins to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) in vesicles coated with coat protein I (COPI) is known as retrograde vesicular transport. Once in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), the proteins dissociate from the receptor. HDEL sequences (H = histidine) serve a similar purpose to KDEL sequences. The processes of anterograde and retrograde vesicular transport result in the net localization of certain soluble proteins to the ER lumen.

Certain other non-KDEL-containing proteins pass to the Golgi and then return by retrograde vesicular transport to the ER. Certain other non-KDEL-containing proteins include vesicle components that must be recycled. Certain ER membrane proteins also follow this retrograde transport. Certain ER membrane proteins often possess a C-terminal signal located in the cytosol rich in basic residues. Proteins reach the ER membrane by a variety of routes. Similar pathways are likely to be used for other membranes, such as mitochondrial membranes and the plasma membrane (PM). Precise targeting sequences have been identified in some instances, such as KDEL sequences.

# MISFOLDED PROTEINS UNDERGO ENDOPLASMIC RETICULUM-ASSOCIATED DEGRADATION

Maintenance of homeostasis in the ER is important for normal cell function. Perturbation of the unique environment within the lumen of the ER can lead to reduced protein folding capacity. Perturbation of the unique environment within the lumen of the ER can lead to the accumulation of misfolded proteins. The accumulation of misfolded proteins in the ER is referred to as ER stress. The unfolded protein response (UPR) is a mechanism within cells that senses the levels of misfolded proteins. The unfolded protein response (UPR) activates intracellular signaling mechanisms to restore ER homeostasis. The UPR is initiated by ER stress sensors. ER stress sensors are transmembrane proteins embedded in the ER membrane. The activation of these sensors causes the transient inhibition of translation so that the synthesis of new proteins is reduced. The activation of these sensors induces transcription to increase the expression of ER chaperones. The activation of these sensors increases the synthesis of proteins involved in the degradation of misfolded ER proteins. The UPR increases the ER folding capacity. The UPR prevents a buildup of unproductive and potentially toxic protein products. The UPR promotes other responses to restore cellular homeostasis. If impairment of folding persists, cell death pathways (apoptosis) are activated. A more complete understanding of the UPR is likely to provide new approaches to treating diseases in which ER stress and defective protein folding occur (Table 48–6, available online).

## Ubiquitin Is a Key Molecule in Protein Degradation

There are two major pathways of protein degradation in eukaryotes. One pathway involves lysosomal proteases and does not require ATP. The major pathway involves ubiquitin and is ATP-dependent. The ubiquitin pathway is associated with the disposal of misfolded proteins. The ubiquitin pathway is associated with the degradation of regulatory enzymes that have short half-lives. Ubiquitin is involved in diverse physiologic processes including cell cycle regulation. Ubiquitin is involved in DNA repair. Ubiquitin is involved in inflammation and the immune response. Ubiquitin is involved in muscle wasting. Ubiquitin is involved in viral infections. Ubiquitin is a small protein consisting of 76 amino acids. Ubiquitin is a highly conserved protein. Ubiquitin tags various proteins for degradation in proteasomes. The mechanism of attachment of ubiquitin to a target protein is described in detail in Chapter 28. An example of a target protein is a misfolded form of cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR). The protein involved in the causation of cystic fibrosis is CFTR. Details about CFTR are discussed in Chapter 40.

## Ubiquitinated Proteins Are Degraded in Proteasomes

Polyubiquitinated target proteins enter proteasomes located in the cytosol. Proteasomes are protein complexes with a relatively large cylindrical structure. Proteasomes are composed of four rings with a hollow core containing the protease active sites. Proteasomes have one or two caps or regulatory particles that recognize the polyubiquinated substrates and initiate degradation. ATPases present in the proteasome caps unfold target proteins. Unfolded target proteins pass into the core of the proteasome to be degraded to small peptides. Small peptides exit the proteasome to be further degraded by cytosolic peptidases. Both normally and abnormally folded proteins are substrates for the proteasome. The proteasome can hydrolyze a wide variety of peptide bonds. Liberated ubiquitin molecules are recycled. The proteasome plays an important role in presenting small peptides to MHC class I molecules. Small peptides produced by degradation of various viruses and other molecules are presented to MHC class I molecules. Small peptides produced by degradation of various viruses and other molecules are a key step in antigen presentation to T lymphocytes.

Different lipids have different half-lives. The half-lives of the proteins in the ER membranes vary widely. Some proteins in the ER membranes have short half-lives measured in hours. Other proteins in the ER membranes have long half-lives measured in days. Individual lipids and proteins of the ER membranes are inserted into it relatively independently. Independent insertion of individual lipids and proteins of the ER membranes is believed to be the case for many other membranes.

The biogenesis of membranes is a complex process. Much remains to be learned about the biogenesis of membranes. Membrane proteins may undergo several posttranslational modifications before reaching their mature state. Posttranslational modifications may include disulfide formation. Posttranslational modifications may include proteolysis. Posttranslational modifications may include assembly into multimers. Posttranslational modifications may include glycosylation. Posttranslational modifications may include the addition of a glycophosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor. Posttranslational modifications may include sulfation on tyrosine or carbohydrate moieties. Posttranslational modifications may include phosphorylation. Posttranslational modifications may include acylation. Posttranslational modifications may include prenylation.

## Various Disorders Result From Mutations in Genes Encoding Proteins Involved in Intracellular Transport

Some disorders involve abnormal peroxisomal function. Some disorders involve abnormalities in protein synthesis in the ER. Some disorders involve abnormalities in lysosomal proteins.

Mutations affecting protein folding have been reported. Mutations affecting intracellular transport to organelles have been reported.

Mutations affecting intracellular transport to organelles include those linked to neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease, Huntington's disease, and Parkinson's disease.

Conformational disorders are referred to as conformational disorders. Conformational disorders have contributed to our understanding of molecular pathology.

Diseases of proteostasis deficiency are due to protein misfolding.

Normal proteostasis involves a balance of synthesis, folding, trafficking, aggregation, and degradation of proteins.

Disturbances in these processes can lead to various disorders. The disorders depend on the proteins involved.

Hsp90 is a chaperone protein that processes 300 to 400 proteins. Some proteins processed by Hsp90 are involved in cancer development.

Hsp90 was identified as a target for antitumor therapy in the 1990s.

Several Hsp90 inhibitors have undergone clinical trials. None of the Hsp90 inhibitors have been approved by the FDA due to toxicity issues.

The search for an Hsp90 inhibitor suitable for clinical use is ongoing.

Hsp70 is more strongly expressed in tumor cells than in normal cells. Hsp70 is another potential anticancer target currently under investigation.

Small drug molecules can act as chemical chaperones. Chemical chaperones can prevent protein misfolding. Chemical chaperones can restore protein function.

Many proteins are targeted to their destinations by signal sequences. A major sorting decision is made when proteins are partitioned between cytosolic (or free) and membrane-bound polyribosomes.
Proteins synthesized on cytosolic polyribosomes are targeted by specific signal sequences to mitochondria, nuclei, and peroxisomes. Proteins that lack a signal remain in the cytosol.
Proteins synthesized on membrane-bound polyribosomes initially enter the ER membrane or lumen, and many are ultimately destined for other membranes including the plasma membrane and that of the Golgi, for lysosomes and for secretion via exocytosis.
Many glycosylation reactions occur in compartments of the Golgi, and proteins are further sorted in the trans-Golgi network.
Molecular chaperones stabilize unfolded or partially folded proteins. Chaperones are required for the correct targeting of proteins to their subcellular locations.
In posttranslational translocation, proteins are transported to their target organelles after their synthesis is complete. Proteins destined for mitochondria, the nucleus, and peroxisomes follow this route, as well as a minority of proteins targeted to the ER.
Most proteins enter the ER lumen by the cotranslational pathway, where translocation occurs during ongoing protein synthesis.
Proteins embedded in the ER membrane may be inserted cotranslationally, posttranslationally, or after transport to the Golgi (anterograde transport), transient retention, and return to the ER (retrograde transport).
Harmful buildup of misfolded proteins triggers the unfolded protein response (UPR) and they are degraded via the ERAD pathway. Proteins are tagged for degradation by the addition of a number of ubiquitin molecules and then enter the cytosol where they are broken down in proteasomes.
Different types of transport vesicles are coated with different proteins. Clathrin-coated vesicles are destined for exocytosis and lysosomes, while coat proteins I and II are associated with COPI and COPII vesicles, which are responsible for retrograde and anterograde transport, respectively.
Transport vesicle processing is complex and requires many protein factors. Budding from the donor membrane is followed by movement through the cytosol, tethering, docking, and fusion with the target membrane.
Certain proteins (eg, precursors of albumin and insulin) are subjected to proteolysis while inside transport vesicles, producing the mature proteins.
Small GTPases (eg, Ran, Rab) and GEFs play key roles in many aspects of intracellular trafficking.
Vesicles formed from membranes of the ER and Golgi are asymmetrical in both lipid and protein content. The asymmetry is maintained during assembly and during fusion of transport vesicles with the PM, so that the inside of the vesicles after fusion becomes the outside of the PM, and the cytoplasmic side of the vesicles remains facing the cytosol.
Lipids and proteins are inserted into membranes independently and turn over at different rates.
Inhibitors of the molecular chaperones Hsp90 and Hsp70 are under intensive investigation as possible targets for anticancer therapy.
