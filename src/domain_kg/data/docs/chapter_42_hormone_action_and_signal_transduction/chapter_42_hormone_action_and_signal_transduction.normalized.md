# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

The homeostatic adaptations of an organism are largely achieved through changes in protein activity and quantity. Hormones are a primary mechanism for facilitating these changes. Hormone-receptor interactions generate amplified intracellular signals. Intracellular signals can regulate the activity of specific genes, altering protein amounts in target cells. Intracellular signals can also affect the activity of specific proteins, such as enzymes, transporters, or channel proteins. The signals influence protein location within the cell. The signals impact general processes like protein synthesis, cell growth, and replication by affecting gene expression. Cytokines, interleukins, growth factors, and metabolites use similar mechanisms and signal transduction pathways. Excessive, deficient, or inappropriate production and release of hormones and other regulatory signaling molecules are major causes of disease. Many pharmacotherapeutic agents target these pathways to correct or influence them.

# HORMONES TRANSDUCE SIGNALS TO AFFECT HOMEOSTATIC MECHANISMS

The stimulus can be a challenge or a threat to the organism, to an organ, or to the integrity of a single cell within that organism. Recognition of the stimulus is the first step in the adaptive response. At the organismic level, this generally involves the nervous system and the special senses such as sight, hearing, pain, smell, and touch. Appropriate recognition of such stimuli results in the release of one or more hormones that will govern generation of the necessary adaptive response.

For purposes of this discussion, the hormones are categorized as described in Tables 41–3 and 41–4. Group I hormones interact with an intracellular receptor. Group II hormones interact with receptor recognition sites located on the extracellular surface of the plasma membrane of target cells. The cytokines, interleukins, and growth factors are also considered in this latter category. Cytokines, interleukins, and growth factors are critical in homeostatic adaptation. Cytokines, interleukins, and growth factors are produced in specific cells and have the equivalent of autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine actions. Cytokines, interleukins, and growth factors bind to cell surface receptors and activate many of the same signal transduction pathways employed by the more traditional group II hormones.

## The Ligand-Receptor Complex Is the Signal for Group I Hormones

Group I hormones are lipophilic and diffuse through the plasma membrane of all cells. Group I hormones encounter their specific, high-affinity intracellular receptors only in target cells. The receptors for these hormones can be located in the cytoplasm or in the nucleus of target cells. The hormone-receptor complex undergoes an activation reaction. Receptor activation occurs by at least two mechanisms. Glucocorticoids diffuse across the plasma membrane and encounter their cognate receptor in the cytoplasm of target cells. Ligand-receptor binding causes a conformational change in the receptor. This change leads to the dissociation of the heat shock protein 90 (hsp90 chaperone). Dissociation of hsp90 is necessary for the nuclear translocation of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR). The GR contains a nuclear localization sequence (NLS) that facilitates its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus. The activated receptor moves into the nucleus and binds with high affinity to a specific DNA sequence called the hormone response element (HRE). In the case of the GR, this is a glucocorticoid response element (GRE). The liganded DNA-bound receptor serves as a high-affinity binding target for one or more transcriptional coactivator proteins. Accelerated gene transcription typically ensues when this occurs. Thyroid hormones and retinoids diffuse from the extracellular fluid across the plasma membrane and go directly into the nucleus. In this case, the cognate receptor is already bound to the HRE, such as the thyroid hormone response element (TRE). However, this DNA-bound receptor fails to activate transcription because it exists in complex with a corepressor. The receptor-corepressor complex serves as a tonic repressor of gene transcription. Association of ligand with these receptors results in the dissociation of the corepressor(s). The liganded receptor is capable of binding one or more coactivators with high affinity. This results in the recruitment of RNA polymerase II and the general transcription factors (GTFs) and activation of gene transcription. The relationship of hormone receptors to other nuclear receptors and to coregulators is discussed in more detail later. (Figure 42–2)

# GROUP II (PEPTIDE & CATECHOLAMINE) HORMONES HAVE MEMBRANE RECEPTORS & USE INTRACELLULAR MESSENGERS

Many hormones are water soluble. Water soluble hormones have no transport proteins. Water soluble hormones have a short plasma half-life. Water soluble hormones initiate a response by binding to a receptor located in the plasma membrane. The mechanism of action of this group of hormones can be discussed in terms of the intracellular signals they generate. These signals include cAMP (cyclic AMP; 3',5'-adenylic acid). cAMP is a nucleotide derived from ATP through the action of adenylyl cyclase. These signals also include cGMP. cGMP is a nucleotide formed from GTP by guanylyl cyclase. Phosphatidylinositides are also part of these signals. Such small molecules are termed second messengers. The synthesis of second messengers is triggered by the presence of the primary hormone binding its receptor. Many of these second messengers affect gene transcription. ## Protein Kinase

In prokaryotic cells, cAMP binds to a specific protein called cAMP activator protein (CAP). cAMP activator protein (CAP) binds directly to DNA and influences gene expression. In eukaryotic cells, cAMP binds to a protein kinase called protein kinase A (PKA). PKA is a heterotetrameric molecule consisting of two regulatory subunits (R) that inhibit the activity of the two catalytic subunits (C) when bound as a tetrameric complex. The binding of cAMP to the R subunit induces dissociation of the R-C complex, thereby activating the catalytic subunits (Figure 42–5).

## Phosphoproteins

The effects of cAMP in eukaryotic cells are mediated by protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, principally on serine and threonine residues. The control of cAMP effects, such as steroidogenesis, secretion, ion transport, carbohydrate and fat metabolism, enzyme induction, gene regulation, synaptic transmission, and cell growth and replication, can be conferred by specific protein kinases, specific phosphatases, or specific substrates for phosphorylation. The array of specific substrates contributes to defining a target tissue and the extent of a particular response within a given cell. The effects of cAMP on gene transcription are mediated by the cyclic AMP response element binding protein (CREB). CREB binds to a cAMP responsive DNA enhancer element (CRE) in its nonphosphorylated state, acting as a weak activator of transcription. When phosphorylated by PKA at key amino acids, CREB becomes a much more potent transcriptional activator. CREB binds the coactivator CREB-binding protein CBP/p300 when phosphorylated. CBP and the related p300 contain histone acetyltransferase activities (HATs) and serve as chromatin-active transcriptional coregulators. CBP/p300 can also acetylate certain transcription factors, thereby stimulating their ability to bind DNA and modulate transcription.

## Phosphodiesterases

Phosphodiesterases hydrolyze cAMP to 5'-AMP. The presence of phosphodiesterases ensures rapid turnover of the cAMP signal. Rapid turnover of cAMP leads to rapid termination of biological processes once the hormonal stimulus is removed. There are at least 11 known members of the phosphodiesterase family of enzymes. Phosphodiesterases are regulated by their substrates, cAMP and cGMP. Phosphodiesterases are regulated by hormones. Phosphodiesterases are regulated by intracellular messengers such as calcium. Calcium probably acts through calmodulin to regulate phosphodiesterases. Inhibitors of phosphodiesterase, such as methylated xanthine derivatives like caffeine, increase intracellular cAMP. Increased intracellular cAMP mimics or prolongs the actions of hormones through this signal.

## Phosphoprotein Phosphatases

Protein phosphorylation is an important control mechanism. The phosphoprotein phosphatases are regulated by phosphorylation-dephosphorylation reactions. The phosphoprotein phosphatases are regulated by protein-protein interactions. The substrate specificity of phosphoserine-phosphothreonine phosphatases is influenced by regulatory subunits. Regulatory subunits of phosphoserine-phosphothreonine phosphatases are hormonally regulated. Dephosphorylation of proteins plays a crucial role in glycogen metabolism in muscle. Two major types of phosphoserine-phosphothreonine phosphatases exist. Type I phosphatase regulates glycogen synthase, phosphorylase, and phosphorylase kinase. Type I phosphatase is regulated by the phosphorylation of its subunits. The phosphorylation of type I phosphatase subunits is reversed by type II phosphatases. Two heat-stable protein inhibitors regulate type I phosphatase activity. Inhibitor-1 is phosphorylated and activated by cAMP-dependent protein kinases. Inhibitor-2 is possibly a subunit of the inactive phosphatase. Inhibitor-2 is phosphorylated by glycogen synthase kinase-3. Phosphatases targeting phosphotyrosine are significant in signal transduction.

## cGMP Is Also an Intracellular Signal

Cyclic GMP is made from GTP by the enzyme guanylyl cyclase. Guanylyl cyclase exists in soluble and membrane-bound forms. Each form of guanylyl cyclase has unique physiologic properties. The atriopeptins are a family of peptides produced in cardiac atrial tissues. The atriopeptins cause natriuresis, diuresis, vasodilation, and inhibition of aldosterone secretion. The atrial natriuretic factor is an example of an atriopeptin. The atriopeptins bind to and activate the membrane-bound form of guanylyl cyclase. Activation of the membrane-bound guanylyl cyclase results in an increase of cGMP by as much as 50-fold in some cases. The increase in cGMP is thought to mediate the effects of atriopeptins. Other evidence links cGMP to vasodilation. Nitroprusside causes smooth muscle relaxation. Nitroglycerin causes smooth muscle relaxation. Nitric oxide causes smooth muscle relaxation. Sodium nitrite causes smooth muscle relaxation. Sodium azide causes smooth muscle relaxation. These compounds are potent vasodilators. These compounds increase cGMP by activating the soluble form of guanylyl cyclase. Inhibitors of cGMP phosphodiesterase enhance and prolong the responses of these agents. Sildenafil, also known as Viagra, is an example of an inhibitor of cGMP phosphodiesterase. Increased cGMP activates cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG). PKG phosphorylates a number of smooth muscle proteins. This phosphorylation is involved in the relaxation of smooth muscle and vasodilation.

## Some Hormones Act Through a Protein Kinase Cascade

The phosphorylation of serine and threonine residues in target proteins is a result of these kinases. The discovery that the epidermal growth factor (EGF) receptor contains an intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity was an important breakthrough. The tyrosine kinase activity of the epidermal growth factor (EGF) receptor is activated by the binding of the ligand EGF. The insulin and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) receptors also contain intrinsic ligand-activated tyrosine kinase activity. Receptors involved in binding ligands that control cellular growth, differentiation, and the inflammatory response either have intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity or are tightly associated with proteins that are tyrosine kinases. These kinases preferentially phosphorylate tyrosine residues. Tyrosine phosphorylation is infrequent, occurring in less than 0.03% of total amino acid phosphorylation in mammalian cells. The ligand-receptor interaction that results in a tyrosine phosphorylation event initiates a cascade involving several protein kinases, phosphatases, and other regulatory proteins.

## Insulin Transmits Signals Through Several Kinase Signaling Cascades

The insulin, EGF, and IGF-1 receptors have intrinsic protein tyrosine kinase activities located within their cytoplasmic domains. The intrinsic protein tyrosine kinase activities are stimulated when their ligands bind to the cognate receptor. The receptors are then autophosphorylated on specific tyrosine residues. This tyrosine residue phosphorylation initiates a complex series of events for the case of the insulin receptor. The tyrosine-phosphorylated sites on the insulin receptor recruit one of several forms of integral membrane proteins, the insulin receptor substrates, on tyrosine residues. ## The NF-κB Pathway Is Regulated by Glucocorticoids

The DNA-binding transcription factor NF-κB is a heterodimeric complex. The heterodimeric complex NF-κB is typically composed of two subunits termed p50 and p65. NF-κB is sequestered in the cytoplasm in a transcriptionally inactive form. The sequestration of NF-κB in the cytoplasm is by interactions with members of the IκB family of proteins. Extracellular stimuli such as proinflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species, and mitogens activate the IKK complex. The IKK complex is a heterohexameric structure. The heterohexameric structure of the IKK complex consists of α, β, and γ subunits. Activated IKK phosphorylates IκB on two serine residues. Phosphorylation of IκB on two serine residues targets IκB for polyubiquitylation and subsequent degradation by the proteasome. Following IκB degradation, free NF-κB translocates to the nucleus. In the nucleus, NF-κB binds to a number of gene enhancers and activates transcription. NF-κB particularly activates transcription of genes involved in the inflammatory response. Transcriptional regulation by NF-κB is mediated by a variety of coactivators such as CREB-binding protein (CBP).

The glucocorticoid receptor competes with NF-κB for binding to coactivators. The glucocorticoid receptor directly binds to the p65 subunit of NF-κB. ## There Is a Large Family of Nuclear Receptor Proteins

The nuclear receptor superfamily consists of a diverse set of transcription factors discovered due to sequence similarity in their DNA-binding domains (DBDs). The nuclear receptor superfamily includes more than 50 members. The family comprises nuclear hormone receptors. The family comprises receptors with subsequently discovered ligands. The family comprises orphan receptors whose physiologic ligands are yet to be identified.

All nuclear receptors have a centrally located DBD that binds with high affinity to its cognate hormone response element (HRE). The DBD contains two zinc finger binding motifs that facilitate binding as homodimers, heterodimers, or monomers. The target response element consists of one or two DNA half-site consensus sequences arranged as an inverted or direct repeat. The spacing between the DNA half-site consensus sequences determines binding specificity.

The ligand-binding domain (LBD) is located in the carboxyl terminal half of the receptor. The ligand-binding domain (LBD) binds hormones or metabolites selectively. The ligand-binding domain (LBD) specifies a particular biological response. The ligand-binding domain (LBD) contains domains for binding heat shock proteins. The ligand-binding domain (LBD) contains domains for dimerization. The ligand-binding domain (LBD) contains domains for nuclear localization. The ligand-binding domain (LBD) contains domains for transactivation.

The carboxyl-terminal transcription activation function, or activation domain (AD), known as the AF-2 domain, facilitates the interaction with coactivators. A highly variable hinge region separates the DBD from the LBD. The hinge region provides flexibility for different DNA-binding conformations.

A highly variable amino-terminal region contains another activation domain, AF-1. AF-1 provides distinct physiologic functions through the binding of different coregulator proteins. The amino-terminal region allows for receptor isoforms that share DBD and LBD identity. These receptor isoforms exert different physiologic responses due to the association of various coregulators with the variable amino terminal AF-1 AD.
