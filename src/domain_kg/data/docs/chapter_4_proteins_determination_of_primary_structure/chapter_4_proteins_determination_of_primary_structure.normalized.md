# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Proteins are physically and functionally complex macromolecules that perform multiple critically important roles. The cytoskeleton maintains a cell’s shape and physical integrity. Actin and myosin filaments form the contractile machinery of muscle. Hemoglobin transports oxygen. Circulating antibodies defend against foreign invaders. Enzymes catalyze reactions that generate energy, synthesize and degrade biomolecules, replicate and transcribe genes, and process messenger RNAs (mRNAs). Receptors enable cells to sense and respond to hormones and other extracellular cues. Proteins are subject to physical and functional changes that mirror the life cycle of the organisms in which they reside. A typical protein is 'born' at translation. Proteins mature through posttranslational processing events such as selective proteolysis. Proteins alternate between working and resting states through the intervention of regulatory factors. Proteins age through oxidation and deamidation. Proteins 'die' when degraded to their component amino acids. An important goal of molecular medicine is to identify proteins and/or modifications to proteins whose presence, absence, or deficiency serves as biomarkers for specific physiologic states or diseases (Figure 4–1).

The life cycle of a protein begins with the synthesis on a ribosome of a polypeptide chain, whose primary structure is dictated by an mRNA (Figure 4–1). As synthesis proceeds, the polypeptide begins to fold into its native conformation. Folding may be accompanied by processing events such as proteolytic cleavage of an N-terminal leader sequence or the formation of disulfide bonds. Subsequent covalent modifications may attach a fatty acid molecule for translocation of the modified protein to a membrane. Binding an allosteric effector may trigger the adoption of a catalytically active conformation. Over time, proteins get damaged by chemical attack, deamidation, or denaturation. Damaged proteins may be 'labeled' by the covalent attachment of several ubiquitin molecules. The ubiquitinated protein is subsequently degraded to its component amino acids, which become available for the synthesis of new proteins (Figure 4–1).

# PURIFICATION OF PROTEINS & PEPTIDES

The purification of a specific protein from a natural source is challenging because living organisms contain thousands of different proteins in varying amounts. Obtaining pure protein often requires the use of multiple separation techniques. Selective precipitation is a technique that exploits differences in protein solubility based on pH or salt concentration. Isoelectric precipitation involves separating proteins by their solubility at different pH levels. Salting out with ammonium sulfate separates proteins based on their solubility at varying salt concentrations. Chromatographic techniques are used to separate proteins based on their size and shape, net charge, hydrophobicity, or ability to bind a specific ligand. Size-exclusion chromatography separates proteins by their size and shape. Ion-exchange chromatography separates proteins based on their net charge. Hydrophobic interaction chromatography separates proteins based on their hydrophobicity. Affinity chromatography separates proteins based on their ability to bind a specific ligand.

## Column Chromatography

In column chromatography, the stationary phase matrix consists of small beads loaded into a cylindrical container of glass, plastic, or steel called a column.

Liquid-permeable frits confine the beads within the column while allowing the mobile-phase liquid to flow or percolate through.

The stationary phase matrix can be chemically derivatized to coat each bead's surface with acidic, basic, or ligand-like groups.

Acidic, basic, or ligand-like groups are required for ion exchange, hydrophobic interaction, or affinity chromatography.

As the mobile-phase liquid emerges from the column, it is automatically collected as a series of small portions called fractions.

## HPLC—High-Pressure Liquid Chromatography

First-generation column chromatography matrices were composed of long, intertwined oligosaccharide polymers shaped into spherical beads approximately a tenth of a millimeter in diameter. Oligosaccharide polymer beads were relatively large and disrupted the flow of the mobile phase. The resolution in chromatography could theoretically be improved by reducing the particle size. However, smaller particles required greater pressures, which crushed the soft and spongy polysaccharide beads. Later, polyacrylamide beads faced the same issue under high pressure. Eventually, small spherical silicon particles were introduced. Small spherical silicon particles were physically strong and porous, with a high surface area suitable for attaching various chemical groups. They were packed into stainless steel columns capable of withstanding the high pressures needed to push liquid through the tightly packed beads. The high surface area and uniformity of these silica beads significantly enhanced the resolving power of high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) systems. HPLC systems achieved a resolving power orders of magnitude higher than that of traditional glass columns with polysaccharide matrices.

## Size-Exclusion Chromatography

Size-exclusion chromatography separates proteins based on their Stokes radii, which is influenced by both molecular mass and shape. An elongated protein effectively occupies a larger volume than a globular protein of the same mass as it tumbles. Size-exclusion chromatography uses porous beads (Figure 4-2).

[FIGURE:fig-4-2]

Size-exclusion chromatography. A: A mixture of large molecules (brown) and small molecules (red) is applied to the top of a gel filtration column. B: Upon entering the column, the small molecules enter pores in the stationary phase matrix (gray). As the mobile phase (blue) flows down the column, they lag behind from the large molecules, which are excluded.

Proteins with Stokes radii too large to enter the pores remain in the flowing mobile phase and emerge first. The fraction of pores accessible to a protein increases with decreasing size, retarding their motion. Proteins emerge from the column in order of their Stokes radii.

In size-exclusion chromatography, large molecules are excluded from the pores and flow down the column faster, while small molecules enter the pores and lag behind (Figure 4-2).

## Ion-Exchange Chromatography

In ion-exchange chromatography, proteins interact with the stationary phase by charge-charge interactions. Proteins with a net positive charge at a given pH adhere to beads derivatized with negatively charged functional groups such as carboxylates or sulfates. Beads derivatized with negatively charged functional groups are cation exchangers. Proteins with a net negative charge adhere to beads with positively charged functional groups. These functional groups are typically tertiary or quaternary amines, and beads with positively charged functional groups are anion exchangers. Nonadherent proteins flow through the matrix and are washed away. Bound proteins can be selectively displaced from the matrix by gradually raising the ionic strength of the mobile phase. Raising the ionic strength weakens charge-charge interactions. Purification can be enhanced by manipulating the pH of the mobile phase. Manipulating the pH alters the magnitude and even the sign of the net charge of each component of a protein mixture.

## Affinity Chromatography

Affinity chromatography exploits the high selectivity displayed by enzymes and other proteins for ligands such as substrates, products, cofactors, inhibitors, or analogues thereof.

A protein mixture is applied to a matrix derivatized with a particular ligand, and only those proteins that bind that ligand will adhere.

Bound proteins are eluted either by competition with free, soluble ligand or by disrupting protein-ligand interactions using high-salt concentrations or other agents.

Recombinantly expressed proteins are usually purified by fusing to their cloned gene an additional segment of DNA that encodes a convenient ligand-binding domain.

## Protein Purity Is Assessed by Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE)

The most widely used method for determining a sample's protein composition is SDS-PAGE, which stands for polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis in the presence of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). SDS-PAGE separates proteins by their molecular mass. SDS causes proteins to unfold or denature. SDS binds to proteins at a ratio of one SDS molecule for every two peptide bonds on average. The anionic charge of SDS overwhelms the natural charges of amino acid functional groups in polypeptides. The anionic charge of SDS results in an approximately equal charge-to-mass ratio for each SDS-polypeptide complex. Larger polypeptides encounter more physical resistance as they move through the acrylamide matrix. Consequently, SDS-PAGE separates polypeptides based on their relative molecular mass. After separation, polypeptides are visualized by staining with dyes such as Coomassie Blue (Figure 4–4).

## Isoelectric Focusing (IEF)

Isoelectric focusing (IEF) uses polyionic buffers called ampholytes and an applied electric field to establish a pH gradient within a polyacrylamide matrix. Proteins migrate within this matrix until they reach a region where their net charge is zero, which occurs at their isoelectric point (pI).

IEF is often combined with SDS-PAGE for two-dimensional electrophoresis. Two-dimensional electrophoresis separates polypeptides based on their isoelectric point in one dimension and on their molecular weight in the second dimension. Two-dimensional electrophoresis is particularly effective for separating complex mixtures of proteins.

An example of two-dimensional SDS-PAGE subjected to isoelectric focusing is shown in Figure 4-5. In this figure, a crude bacterial extract was first subjected to IEF in a pH 3-10 gradient. The IEF gel was then placed on top of an SDS-PAGE gel, and the proteins were further resolved by SDS-PAGE. Two-dimensional electrophoresis results in greatly improved resolution of distinct polypeptides compared to ordinary SDS-PAGE (Figure 4-5).

# SANGER WAS THE FIRST TO DETERMINE THE SEQUENCE OF A POLYPEPTIDE

Scientists found it relatively simple to determine the amino acid composition of purified proteins and peptides.

Deriving the order or sequence of the amino acids proved difficult.

Frederick Sanger was the first to determine the sequence of a polypeptide, insulin, earning him a Nobel Prize in 1958.

Mature insulin consists of the 21-residue A chain and the 30-residue B chain linked by disulfide bonds.

Sanger reduced the disulfide bonds to separate the A and B chains.

Sanger used proteolytic enzymes trypsin, chymotrypsin, and pepsin to cleave each chain into smaller peptides.

Sanger also incubated the peptides with hydrochloric acid.

Each peptide in the mixture was isolated and treated with 1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene, known as Sanger reagent.

Sanger reagent reacts with the exposed α-amino groups of the amino-terminal residues.

The reaction with Sanger reagent allows the amino-terminal amino acid of each peptide to be identified.

The ε-amino group of lysine also reacts with Sanger reagent.

An amino-terminal lysine reacts with two molecules of Sanger reagent, distinguishing it from a lysine from the interior of a peptide.

Sanger worked from di- and tripeptides up through progressively larger fragments to reconstruct the complete sequence of insulin.

Sanger later developed the dideoxy method for sequencing DNA, earning him a second Nobel Prize in 1980.

# EDMAN DEVISED THE FIRST PRACTICAL METHOD FOR PEPTIDE SEQUENCING

Sanger’s approach to peptide sequencing was labor-intensive. Sanger’s approach to peptide sequencing was impractical for anything but the smallest polypeptides. Pehr Edman discovered phenyl isothiocyanate, known as Edman reagent. Edman reagent can selectively label the amino-terminal residue of a peptide as its phenylthiohydantoin (PTH) derivative. Edman reagent allows the PTH derivative to be removed under mild conditions. The new amino terminal residue can then be treated with Edman reagent. The process can be repeated to permit each successive residue in a peptide to be derivatized (Figure 4–6).

[FIGURE:fig-4-6]

The Edman reaction. Phenyl isothiocyanate derivatizes the amino-terminal residue of a peptide as a phenylthiohydantoic acid. Treatment with acid in a nonhydroxylic solvent releases a phenylthiohydantoin, which is subsequently identified by its chromatographic mobility, and a peptide one residue shorter. The process is then repeated.

The formation of PTH derivatives using Edman reagent occurs at less than 100% efficiency. Some unreacted peptide remains after each cycle. Some unreacted peptide makes it difficult to distinguish the PTH amino acid for that position in the peptide from out-of-phase contaminants. Consequently, the read length for Edman sequencing varies from 5 to 30 amino acid residues.

To determine the complete sequence of a polypeptide several hundred residues in length using the Edman method, the protein must be cleaved into smaller peptides. The smaller peptides are then analyzed by Edman sequencing. The individual peptide sequences must be placed in the correct order to determine the complete sequence of the protein. The process of placing the individual peptide sequences in the correct order is achieved by cleaving the protein at different sites, generally using proteases, and sequencing them. Peptides with overlapping sequences generated by alternative cleavage methods are identified to piece together larger segments of the sequence. The process of identifying peptides with overlapping sequences typically requires multiple different cleavage reagents. The process of identifying peptides with overlapping sequences typically requires large quantities of purified protein. The process of identifying peptides with overlapping sequences makes it time-consuming. Consequently, the determination of a complete amino acid sequence via the Edman method is generally restricted to highly abundant, readily purified proteins.

# MOLECULAR BIOLOGY REVOLUTIONIZED THE DETERMINATION OF PRIMARY STRUCTURE

An organism’s genome encodes the sequence of the polypeptides that serve as the foundation for its many mature proteins. Molecular biology revolutionized amino acid sequence analysis by enabling scientists to decode the sequences of polypeptides directly from their polynucleotides blueprints. Recombinant techniques permit researchers to manufacture a virtually infinite supply of DNA from as little as a single molecule of the original template. DNA sequencing is far more efficient than the Edman technique. If one could determine just a portion of the sequence of a polypeptide of interest by Edman analysis, one could use it to identify, clone, and sequence the DNA that encodes it.

# GENOMICS ENABLES PROTEINS TO BE IDENTIFIED FROM SMALL AMOUNTS OF SEQUENCE DATA

The Human Genome Project was launched in 1989.

The Human Genome Project resulted in technical advances that made DNA sequencing rapid and routine.

Scientists have access to complete genome sequences for hundreds of thousands of organisms, including numerous microorganisms, plants, animals, and Homo sapiens.

The Human Genome Project is no longer necessary to clone the gene for a protein to solve its sequence.

As little as five or six residues of amino acid sequence can be used to recover the DNA for the corresponding protein from a genome databank.

The process of identifying proteins has been reversed in many instances.

One searches the genome for a gene whose protein product appears to possess the desired attributes.

The gene is cloned and the encoded protein is expressed to test if it performs the function of interest.

Mass spectrometric (MS) methods have superior sensitivity, speed, and the capacity to analyze impure samples.

Mass spectrometry has replaced the Edman technique as the method of choice for protein identification.

# MASS SPECTROMETERS COME IN VARIOUS CONFIGURATIONS

In a single quadrupole (Q) mass spectrometer, a sample is placed under vacuum and allowed to vaporize in the presence of a proton donor to impart a positive charge. An electrical field propels the cations toward a curved flight tube where they encounter a magnetic field, which deflects them at a right angle to their original direction of flight. The current powering the electromagnet is gradually increased until the path of each ion is bent sufficiently to strike a detector mounted at the end of the flight tube. For ions of identical net charge, the force required to bend their path to the same extent is proportionate to their mass.

Time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometers employ a linear flight tube. Following vaporization of the sample in the presence of a proton donor, an electric field is briefly applied to accelerate the ions toward a detector at the end of the flight tube. For molecules of identical charge, the velocity at which they are accelerated, and hence the time required to reach the detector, is inversely proportional to their mass.

Quadrupole mass spectrometers are generally used to determine the masses of molecules of 4000 Da or less. TOF mass spectrometers are used to determine the large masses of complete proteins. Various combinations of multiple quadrupoles, or reflection of ions back down the linear flight tube of a TOF mass spectrometer, are used to create more sophisticated instruments.

A mixture of molecules, represented by a red circle, green triangle, and blue diamond, is vaporized in an ionized state in the sample chamber (Figure 4-7). A mixture of molecules is then accelerated down the flight tube by an electrical potential applied to the accelerator grid. An adjustable field strength electromagnet applies a magnetic field that deflects the flight of the individual ions until they strike the detector. The greater the mass of the ion, the higher the magnetic field required to focus it onto the detector.

## Peptides Can Be Volatilized for Analysis by Electrospray Ionization or Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption

For many years, the analysis of peptides and proteins by mass spectrometry (MS) was hindered by difficulties in volatilizing these large organic molecules. Small organic molecules could be readily vaporized by heating in a vacuum (Figure 4–8). Proteins, oligonucleotides, etc., decomposed on heating. Reliable techniques for dispersing peptides, proteins, and other large biomolecules into the vapor phase were needed to apply MS for their structural analysis and sequence determination.

[FIGURE:fig-4-8]

Three common methods for vaporizing molecules in the sample chamber of a mass spectrometer.

Three commonly used methods for dispersion into the vapor phase are electrospray ionization, matrix-assisted laser desorption and ionization (MALDI), and fast atom bombardment (FAB). In electrospray ionization, the molecules to be analyzed are dissolved in a volatile solvent. In electrospray ionization, the molecules are introduced into the sample chamber in a minute stream through a charged capillary probe (see Figure 4–8). As the droplet of liquid emerges into the sample chamber, the charged probe ionizes the sample. As the droplet of liquid emerges into the sample chamber, the solvent rapidly disperses. The macromolecule is left suspended in the gaseous phase after the solvent disperses. Electrospray ionization is frequently used to analyze peptides and proteins as they elute from an HPLC or other chromatography column.

In MALDI, the sample is mixed with a liquid matrix containing a light-absorbing dye and a source of protons. In the sample chamber, the mixture is excited using a laser. The laser causes the surrounding matrix to disperse into the vapor phase so rapidly as to avoid heating embedded peptides or proteins (see Figure 4-8). In FAB, large macromolecules are dispersed in glycerol or another protonic matrix. In FAB, the macromolecules are bombarded by a stream of neutral atoms, for example, xenon, that have been accelerated to a high velocity. 'Soft' ionization by FAB is frequently applied to volatilize large macromolecules intact.

Peptides inside the mass spectrometer can be broken down into smaller units by colliding them with neutral helium or argon atoms (collision-induced dissociation). The masses of the individual fragments of peptides can be determined. Peptide bonds are more vulnerable to rupture than carbon-carbon bonds. Most fragments of peptides will differ from one another by increments of one or two amino acids. The molecular mass of each amino acid is unique, with the exceptions of leucine and isoleucine and glutamine and lysine. The sequence of the peptide can be reconstructed from the masses of its fragments.

## Tandem Mass Spectrometry

Tandem mass spectrometry analyzes complex peptide mixtures without prior purification. Tandem mass spectrometry employs two mass spectrometers linked in series. Analysis by tandem instruments is often referred to as MS-MS or MS2. The first mass spectrometer separates individual peptides based on their differences in mass. The field strength of the first magnet can be adjusted to direct a single peptide into the second mass spectrometer. Fragments are generated in the second mass spectrometer and their masses are determined. Peptide fragments can be held in an electromagnetic ion trap between the two quadrupoles. Fragments held in the ion trap are selectively delivered to the second quadrupole. Fragments are not lost when the first quadrupole is set to select ions of a different mass. Tandem MS can be used to screen blood samples from newborns for amino acids, fatty acids, and other metabolites. Abnormal metabolite levels can serve as diagnostic indicators for genetic disorders. Phenylketonuria is a genetic disorder that can be diagnosed using tandem MS. Ethylmalonic encephalopathy is a genetic disorder that can be diagnosed using tandem MS. Glutaric acidemia type 1 is a genetic disorder that can be diagnosed using tandem MS. A new generation of tandem mass spectrometers, called Q-TOF-MS, has appeared. Q-TOF-MS couples Q and TOF technologies together. Q-TOF-MS harnesses the best aspects of each technology it combines.

## The Goal of Proteomics Is to Identify the Entire Complement of Proteins Elaborated by a Cell Under Diverse Conditions

The sequence of the human genome is known, but the picture it provides is static and incomplete.

Genes are switched on and off, and mRNA molecules are customized via alternative splicing.

The spectrum of proteins synthesized varies by particular cell type, stages of growth or differentiation, and in response to external stimuli.

Muscle cells express proteins not expressed by neural cells.

The types of subunits present in the hemoglobin tetramer change pre- and postpartum.

Many proteins undergo posttranslational modifications during maturation into functionally competent forms or as a means of regulating their properties.

To obtain a more complete and dynamic molecular description of living organisms, scientists are working to determine the proteome.

The proteome refers to the identity, abundance, and state of modification of the entire suite of proteins expressed by an individual cell at a particular time.

The proteome for each component cell of an organism is distinct and changes with time and circumstances.

The ultimate, comprehensive human proteome constitutes a target of formidable size and complexity.

## Simultaneous Determination of Hundreds of Proteins Is Technically Challenging

A key goal of proteomics is to identify proteins whose expression or modification levels correlate with medically significant events. Protein biomarkers may serve as diagnostic indicators. Protein biomarkers provide insights into the causes and mechanisms of physiologic conditions or diseases. First-generation proteomics used SDS-PAGE to separate proteins in a biologic sample. First-generation proteomics used two-dimensional electrophoresis to separate proteins in a biologic sample. The Edman method was used to determine the amino acid sequence of the amino terminus of proteins after separation. Protein identities were determined by matching the N-terminal sequence and Mr. For 2D gels, protein identities were determined by matching pI with available polypeptide sequences.

Early proteomics efforts were limited by the scarcity of polypeptide sequences. Early proteomics efforts were limited by the difficulty in isolating sufficient quantities of polypeptides from gels for Edman analysis. Increasing gel size to enhance resolving power and sample yield was only marginally successful. The development of mass spectrometry techniques improved protein sequence determination sensitivity. Mass spectrometry techniques made it compatible with electrophoretic separation methods. Knowledge of the genome sequence greatly facilitated protein identification by providing a comprehensive set of DNA-encoded polypeptide sequences. The genome sequence enabled the construction of gene arrays, or DNA chips, containing numerous oligonucleotide probes for detecting mRNAs with complementary sequences. Gene arrays were less technically demanding than first-generation proteomic approaches. Gene arrays were more sensitive than first-generation proteomic approaches, particularly for low-abundance proteins.

Second-generation proteomics combined newly developed nanoscale chromatographic techniques with MS. Proteins in a biologic sample are treated with a protease to hydrolyze them into smaller peptides. The peptides are then subjected to reversed-phase, ion-exchange, or size-exclusion chromatography to divide them into smaller subsets for analysis. The subsets are analyzed by injecting the column eluent directly into a double Q or TOF mass spectrometer. Multidimensional protein identification technology (MudPIT) uses successive rounds of chromatography to resolve peptides from a complex biologic sample into simpler fractions for separate MS analysis. Advances in tandem mass spectrometers now allow direct analysis of complex samples. These advances eliminate the need for prior proteolytic or chromatographic preparation. These advancements will soon make it feasible to analyze the proteome of an individual cell.
