# Overview

Glycolysis is a metabolic pathway that converts glucose into pyruvate.

The oxidation of pyruvate is a critical step in cellular respiration.

Owen P. McGuinness is the author of the chapter on glycolysis and the oxidation of pyruvate.

Glycolysis occurs in the cytoplasm of cells.

The process of glycolysis produces ATP and NADH.

Pyruvate is the end product of glycolysis in many organisms.

In the presence of oxygen, pyruvate can be oxidized to form acetyl-CoA.

Acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle in the mitochondria.

The oxidation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA is catalyzed by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex.

The oxidation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA also produces carbon dioxide and reduces NAD+ to NADH.

The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is a large, multi-enzyme complex.

Regulation of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex involves phosphorylation by pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase.

Phosphorylation inhibits the activity of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex.

ATP and NADH are allosteric inhibitors of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex.

Calcium ions can activate the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex.

Glycolysis and the oxidation of pyruvate are essential for energy production in cells.

Disruptions in these pathways can lead to metabolic disorders.

# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Most tissues have at least some requirement for glucose. The brain is predominantly reliant on glucose for its energy needs. In prolonged fasting, ketone bodies can meet about 20% of the brain's energy needs. Glycolysis is the main pathway through which cells metabolize glucose and other carbohydrates. Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol of cells. Glycolysis can function either aerobically or anaerobically depending on the availability of oxygen and the activity of the electron transport chain. Erythrocytes, which lack mitochondria, are completely reliant on glucose as their metabolic fuel. Erythrocytes metabolize glucose by anaerobic glycolysis. The ability of glycolysis to provide ATP in the absence of oxygen allows skeletal muscle to perform at very high levels of work output when oxygen supply is insufficient. The ability of glycolysis to provide ATP in the absence of oxygen allows tissues to survive anoxic episodes. Heart muscle, which is adapted for aerobic performance, has relatively low glycolytic activity. Heart muscle has poor survival under conditions of impaired perfusion and consequent oxygen delivery to a tissue (ischemia). Diseases in which enzymes of glycolysis, such as pyruvate kinase, are deficient are mainly seen as hemolytic anemias. If the defect affects skeletal muscle, such as phosphofructokinase, it can lead to fatigue. In fast-growing cancer cells, glycolysis proceeds at a high rate. Fast-growing cancer cells form large amounts of pyruvate, which is reduced to lactate and released. The lactate is used for gluconeogenesis in the liver. Secondary to the overall inflammatory and hypermetabolic state seen with cancer cachexia, there is a marked increase in hepatic protein synthesis. Cancer cachexia increases the delivery of amino acids from the catabolic muscle, which further fuels gluconeogenesis. Lactic acidosis can be of two types. Type A lactic acidosis is the most common and is due to impaired tissue perfusion or hypoxia, such as in sepsis and hypovolemia. Type B lactic acidosis is due to the impaired ability to metabolize lactate, such as in liver disease and thiamine deficiency. Thiamin (vitamin B1) deficiency impairs the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase.

# GLYCOLYSIS CAN FUNCTION UNDER ANAEROBIC CONDITIONS

Glycolysis can function under anaerobic conditions. When a muscle contracts without oxygen, glycogen disappears. When a muscle contracts without oxygen, lactate appears. If oxygen becomes available, aerobic recovery occurs. If oxygen becomes available, lactate release is diminished. Under aerobic conditions, muscle contraction leads to diminished lactate release because pyruvate and NADH, the products of glycolysis, do not accumulate.

When oxygen is in short supply, mitochondrial reoxidation of NADH formed during glycolysis is impaired. There is a limited supply of NAD to sustain glycolysis. NADH must be reoxidized. NADH is reoxidized by reducing pyruvate to lactate. Reducing pyruvate to lactate allows NAD to be available for the early steps in the pathway. Reducing pyruvate to lactate permits glycolysis to continue. While glycolysis can occur under anaerobic conditions, it limits the amount of ATP formed per mole of glucose oxidized. Consequently, much more glucose must be metabolized under anaerobic conditions to supply the same quantity of ATP for cellular work (Table 17–1).

Using glycogen rather than glucose for anaerobic glycolysis in muscle is advantageous because the product of glycogen phosphorylase is glucose-1-phosphate. Glucose-1-phosphate is interconvertible with glucose-6-phosphate. Using glycogen rather than glucose saves the ATP that would otherwise be used by hexokinase, increasing the net yield of ATP from 2 to 3 per glucose. Although the initial formation of glycogen requires ATP, in a net sense, glucose first going to glycogen and then coming back out for oxidation via pyruvate is the same. Using glycogen rather than glucose just provides more ATP at a time where ATP is in short supply.

# GLYCOLYSIS CONSTITUTES THE MAIN PATHWAY OF GLUCOSE UTILIZATION

Glycolysis is the main pathway for glucose utilization. The overall equation for glycolysis from glucose to lactate is: Glucose + 2 ADP + 2 P → 2 Lactate + 2 ATP + 2 H O. All enzymes of glycolysis are cytosolic. The process of ATP generation during glycolysis involves rearranging a phosphorylated monosaccharide glucose-6-phosphate. Rearranging glucose-6-phosphate creates compounds with a high potential to transfer their phosphate groups. Triosephosphates transfer their phosphate to ADP to form ATP. Triosephosphates transfer their phosphate to ADP to form ATP is called substrate-level phosphorylation. The pathway uses ADP and produces ATP. ADP is required to be regenerated by performing metabolic work to sustain glycolysis.

Glucose enters glycolysis by being phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate. The phosphorylation of glucose to glucose-6-phosphate is catalyzed by hexokinase. ATP is used as the phosphate donor in the phosphorylation of glucose. The phosphorylation of glucose to glucose-6-phosphate can be regarded as irreversible under physiologic conditions. Hexokinase is inhibited allosterically by its product, glucose-6-phosphate. In muscle and adipose tissue, glucose transport is stimulated by insulin. In muscle, glucose is used for glycolysis. In muscle, glucose is used for glycogen synthesis. In adipose tissue, glucose is used for lipogenesis. The hexokinase in most tissues has a high affinity for glucose. Hexokinase in most tissues is allosterically inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate. Transport activity is an important determinant of the overall rate of glycolysis. The concentration of plasma glucose and cellular ATP demand are the primary determinants of glycolysis in an aerobic environment. In the presence of insulin, transport activity increases. Increased transport activity lowers the barrier to glucose entry. If transport activity increases 10-fold, glycolysis will not increase 10-fold. An increase in glucose-6-phosphate would inhibit hexokinase. An increase in glucose-6-phosphate shifts the control of glycolysis to pathways downstream of hexokinase. In muscle, hexokinase is found bound to the outer mitochondrial membrane. The binding of hexokinase to the outer mitochondrial membrane creates a coupling between hexokinase activity and mitochondrial ATP generation.

In the liver, transport activity is not regulated. A different isozyme of hexokinase, glucokinase, is expressed in the liver. Glucokinase is not inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate. The intracellular glucose in the liver is very similar to plasma glucose. The liver can be both a consumer and producer of glucose. In the fed state, glucokinase removes glucose from the hepatic portal blood following a meal. Glucokinase limits the quantity of glucose available to peripheral tissues in the fed state. Glucokinase in the fasted state is inactive in the nucleus bound to a glucokinase regulatory protein (GKRP). In response to signals during a meal, glucokinase moves to the cytosol where it is active. In individuals with diabetes, the inability to augment glucose uptake exacerbates hyperglycemia. In individuals with diabetes, the inability to augment glycogen synthesis in the liver exacerbates hyperglycemia.

Glucose-6-phosphate is an important compound at the junction of several metabolic pathways: glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, glycogenesis, and glycogenolysis. In glycolysis, glucose-6-phosphate is converted to fructose-6-phosphate by phosphohexose isomerase. The reaction catalyzed by phosphohexose isomerase is followed by another phosphorylation catalyzed by phosphofructokinase-1, forming fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. The phosphofructokinase reaction is irreversible under physiologic conditions. Phosphofructokinase is inducible. Phosphofructokinase is subject to allosteric regulation. Phosphofructokinase has a major role in regulating the rate of glycolysis. Up to this point in the pathway, no ATP is generated; ATP is only being consumed. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate is cleaved by aldolase into two triose phosphates: glyceraldehyde-3 phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate. Glyceraldehyde-3 phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate are interconverted by the enzyme phosphotriose isomerase.

Glycolysis continues with the oxidation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate and the formation of NADH. The enzyme catalyzing this oxidation, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, is NAD dependent. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase consists of four identical polypeptides forming a tetramer. Four —SH groups are present on each polypeptide, derived from cysteine residues within the polypeptide chain. One of the —SH groups is found at the active site of the enzyme. The substrate initially combines with the —SH group at the active site, forming a thiohemiacetal that is oxidized to a thiol ester. The hydrogens removed in this oxidation are transferred to NAD+. The thiol ester undergoes phosphorolysis; inorganic phosphate (P ) is added, forming 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate and the free —SH group. The enzyme is inhibited by the — SH poison iodoacetate, which is thus able to inhibit glycolysis. The NADH produced on the enzyme is not so firmly bound to the enzyme as is NAD+. Consequently, NADH is easily displaced by another molecule of NAD+. (Figure 17–3)

Two ATP molecules are formed per glucose molecule in this reaction during glycolysis. Arsenic is toxic because arsenate competes with inorganic phosphate. The reaction catalyzed by pyruvate kinase is essentially irreversible under physiological conditions. This irreversibility is due to the large free-energy change and the spontaneous isomerization of the immediate product, enolpyruvate, to pyruvate.

The fate of pyruvate depends on the availability of oxygen. Under anaerobic conditions, pyruvate is reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase. Under anaerobic conditions, lactate dehydrogenase allows NADH to be reoxidized to NAD. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate is transported into the mitochondria. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate is converted to acetyl-CoA. Under aerobic conditions, acetyl-CoA is oxidized to CO2 in the citric acid cycle. The reducing equivalents from NADH formed in glycolysis are transported into mitochondria via the malate-aspartate shuttle. # TISSUES THAT FUNCTION UNDER HYPOXIC CONDITIONS OR HAVE INTRISICALLY HIGH RATES OF GLUCOSE OXIDATION PRODUCE LACTATE

In organs where glucose is the major substrate for oxidation, a variable fraction of the glucose uptake can be diverted to lactate depending on the metabolic state of the tissue. In tissues or metabolic states where pyruvate oxidation is absent or impaired, pyruvate is diverted to lactate and released so as to recycle the NAD to support glycolysis and the generation of ATP to support cellular metabolic work. In erythrocytes, pyruvate always terminates in lactate. Erythrocytes lack mitochondria and thus cannot oxidize pyruvate. Some skeletal muscle, particularly the white fibers, have low mitochondrial content. During states of high-work output, these muscles mobilize muscle glycogen stores. Muscles lack glucose-6-phosphatase, so the glucose-6-phosphate mobilized from glycogen traverses down the glycolytic pathway. Due to relatively low rates of pyruvate oxidation, these muscles release lactate. The need for ATP formation in working muscle may exceed the rate at which oxygen is delivered, especially during high-intensity exercise where you are at or above 50% of your maximal metabolic capacity. In such conditions, the mitochondria cannot oxidize the NADH produced by glycolysis, resulting in a rise in cytosolic NADH and a shift to the release of lactate. Some tissues rely on anaerobic glycolysis to support metabolism, such as tumors, the retina, the renal medulla, and the skin. Lactate production is also increased in septic shock due to both impaired oxygen delivery and shifts in metabolic capacity due to cellular injury. The brain normally derives much of its energy from glucose oxidation but produces some lactate, about 3-5% of total glycolytic flux, because of the high glycolytic rates. The liver, kidneys, oxidative skeletal muscle, and heart have very high capacity to oxidize multiple fuels. The liver, kidneys, oxidative skeletal muscle, and heart normally take up lactate and oxidize it but can produce it under hypoxic conditions. When lactate production is high, as in vigorous exercise, septic shock, and cancer cachexia, lactate is used in the liver for gluconeogenesis. Lactate production leads to an increase in metabolic rate to provide the ATP and GTP needed to synthesize glucose. This increase may contribute to the prolongation of the increased metabolic rate after cessation of vigorous exercise. Under some states, lactate can be directly transported into the mitochondria to generate pyruvate and NADH. This allows for the transfer of reducing equivalents, such as NADH, from the cytosol into the mitochondrion for the electron transport chain in addition to the glycerophosphate and malate-aspartate shuttles.

# GLYCOLYSIS IS REGULATED AT THREE NONEQUILIBRIUM REACTIONS

Most reactions of glycolysis are freely reversible. Three reactions in glycolysis are markedly exergonic and physiologically irreversible. Three reactions are catalyzed by hexokinase and glucokinase, phosphofructokinase-1, and pyruvate kinase. Three reactions are the major sites of regulation of glycolysis. Phosphofructokinase-1 is significantly inhibited at normal intracellular concentrations of ATP. 5' AMP relieves the inhibition of phosphofructokinase-1 when ADP begins to accumulate. The failure to rapidly convert ADP to ATP signals the need for an increased rate of glycolysis. Fructose 2,6 bisphosphate is a potent allosteric activator of phosphofructokinase-1. Fructose 2,6 bisphosphate relieves inhibition of phosphofructokinase-1 by ATP. Fructose 2,6 bisphosphate increases the affinity of phosphofructokinase-1 for fructose-6-phosphate. The availability of fructose 2,6 bisphosphate is determined by phosphofructose kinase-2, also known as 6-phosphofructo-2 kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase. Phosphofructose kinase-2 has both kinase and phosphatase activity, which is regulated. The ratio of kinase and phosphatase activities determines the quantity of fructose 2,6 bisphosphate available. Fructose 2,6 bisphosphate regulates phosphofructokinase-1 in glycolysis. Fructose 2,6 bisphosphate regulates fructose 1,6-bisphosphatase in gluconeogenesis. Cells capable of gluconeogenesis have different enzymes to bypass the irreversible steps of glycolysis. These enzymes include glucose-6-phosphatase and fructose 1,6 bisphosphatase. Pyruvate carboxylase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase are involved in reversing the reaction of pyruvate kinase. Fructose enters glycolysis by phosphorylation to fructose-1-phosphate. This bypasses the main regulatory steps, resulting in more pyruvate and acetyl-CoA than is required for ATP formation. Increases in fructose-1-phosphate in the liver activate glucokinase. This competes for binding with the glucokinase regulatory protein, amplifying hepatic glucose uptake. Increased hepatic glucose uptake increases hepatic lipogenesis and predisposes to hepatic steatosis.

## In Erythrocytes, the First Site of ATP Formation in Glycolysis May Be Bypassed

In erythrocytes, phosphoglycerate kinase catalyzes a reaction that may be bypassed by the reaction of bisphosphoglycerate mutase. Bisphosphoglycerate mutase catalyzes the conversion of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate. 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate is then hydrolyzed to 3-phosphoglycerate and P by 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate phosphatase (Figure 17-4).

# THE OXIDATION OF PYRUVATE TO ACETYL-COA IS THE IRREVERSIBLE ROUTE FROMGLYCOLYSIS TO THE CITRIC ACID CYCLE

Pyruvate is transported into the mitochondrion by a proton symporter. Pyruvate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation to acetyl-CoA. A multienzyme complex catalyzes the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate. The multienzyme complex is associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is analogous to the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex of the citric acid cycle. Pyruvate is decarboxylated by the pyruvate dehydrogenase component of the enzyme complex. The decarboxylation of pyruvate results in a hydroxyethyl derivative of the thiazole ring of enzyme-bound thiamin diphosphate. The hydroxyethyl derivative reacts with oxidized lipoamide. Oxidized lipoamide is the prosthetic group of dihydrolipoyl transacetylase. The reaction forms acetyl lipoamide from the hydroxyethyl derivative and oxidized lipoamide. Impaired pyruvate oxidation leads to significant lactic and pyruvic acidosis. Acetyl lipoamide reacts with coenzyme A to form acetyl-CoA and reduced lipoamide. Reduced lipoamide is reoxidized by a flavoprotein, dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase, containing flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD). The reduced flavoprotein is oxidized by NAD+. NAD+ transfers reducing equivalents to the respiratory chain. The overall reaction is: Pyruvate + NAD+ + CoA → Acetyl-CoA + NADH + H+ + CO2. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex consists of a number of polypeptide chains of each of the three component enzymes. The intermediates in the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex do not dissociate but are channeled from one enzyme site to the next. Channeling intermediates increases the rate of reaction and prevents side reactions. Lipoic acid is joined by an amide link to a lysine residue of the transacetylase component of the enzyme complex. The amide link allows lipoic acid to form a long flexible arm. The flexible arm of lipoic acid allows it to rotate sequentially between the active sites of each of the enzymes of the complex (Figure 17–5).

## Inhibition of Pyruvate Metabolism Leads to Lactic Acidosis

Arsenite reacts with the —SH groups of lipoic acid and inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase. Mercuric ions react with the —SH groups of lipoic acid and inhibit pyruvate dehydrogenase. A dietary deficiency of thiamin inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase. Pyruvate accumulates when pyruvate dehydrogenase is inhibited. Many alcoholics are thiamin deficient. Alcohol inhibits thiamin absorption. Thiamin deficiency in alcoholics may lead to pyruvic and lactic acidosis. Pyruvic and lactic acidosis in alcoholics can be potentially fatal. Inherited pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency results in lactic acidosis. Defects in components of the enzyme complex can cause inherited pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency. Lactic acidosis in individuals with pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency is particularly evident after a glucose load. The brain depends on glucose as a fuel. Metabolic defects that affect glucose utilization can cause neurologic disturbances. Erythrocytes require glycolysis to maintain membrane function and integrity. Inherited aldolase A deficiency in erythrocytes causes hemolytic anemia. Pyruvate kinase deficiency in erythrocytes causes hemolytic anemia. Muscle phosphofructokinase deficiency lowers exercise capacity. High-carbohydrate diets exacerbate the low exercise capacity in patients with muscle phosphofructokinase deficiency. Robust carbohydrate oxidation is required for patients with muscle phosphofructokinase deficiency on high-carbohydrate diets.

Glycolysis is in the cytosolic pathway in all mammalian cells for the metabolism of glucose (or glycogen) to pyruvate or lactate.
It can function anaerobically by regenerating oxidized NAD+ (required in the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase reaction), by reducing pyruvate to lactate.
Lactate is the end product of glycolysis under anaerobic conditions (eg, in exercising muscle) and in erythrocytes, where there are no mitochondria to permit further oxidation of pyruvate.
Glycolysis is regulated by glucose transport and by three enzymes catalyzing nonequilibrium reactions: hexokinase, phosphofructokinase, and pyruvate kinase.
Pyruvate is oxidized to acetyl-CoA by a multienzyme complex, pyruvate dehydrogenase, which is dependent on the vitamin-derived cofactor thiamin diphosphate.
Conditions that impair pyruvate metabolism frequently lead to lactic acidosis.
