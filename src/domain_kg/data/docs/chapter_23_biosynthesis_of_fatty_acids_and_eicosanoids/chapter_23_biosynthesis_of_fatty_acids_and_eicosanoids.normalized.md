# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Fatty acids are synthesized by an extramitochondrial system. The extramitochondrial system is responsible for the complete synthesis of palmitate from acetyl-CoA in the cytosol. In most mammals, glucose is the primary substrate for lipogenesis. Critical diseases of the lipogenesis pathway have not been reported in humans. Inhibition of lipogenesis occurs in type 1 diabetes mellitus. Variations in the activity of lipogenesis affect the nature and extent of obesity.

Unsaturated fatty acids in phospholipids of the cell membrane are important in maintaining membrane fluidity. A high ratio of polyunsaturated fatty acids to saturated fatty acids in the diet is beneficial in preventing coronary heart disease. Animal tissues have limited capacity for desaturating fatty acids. Certain dietary polyunsaturated fatty acids are required, which are derived from plants. Eicosanoic fatty acids give rise to eicosanoids such as prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes, and lipoxins. Prostaglandins mediate inflammation. Prostaglandins mediate pain. Prostaglandins induce sleep. Prostaglandins regulate blood coagulation. Prostaglandins regulate reproduction. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin and ibuprofen inhibit prostaglandin synthesis. Leukotrienes have muscle contractant properties. Leukotrienes have chemotactic properties. Leukotrienes are important in allergic reactions. Leukotrienes are important in inflammation.

# THE MAIN PATHWAY FOR DE NOVO SYNTHESIS OF FATTY ACIDS (LIPOGENESIS) OCCURS IN THE CYTOSOL

The main pathway for de novo synthesis of fatty acids occurs in the cytosol.

The main pathway for de novo synthesis of fatty acids is present in many tissues, including liver, kidney, brain, lung, mammary gland, and adipose tissue.

Acetyl-CoA is the immediate substrate for fatty acid synthesis.

Free palmitate is the end product of fatty acid synthesis.

## Production of Malonyl-CoA Is the Initial & Controlling Step in Fatty Acid Synthesis

The initial step in fatty acid synthesis is the carboxylation of acetyl-CoA to form malonyl-CoA by acetyl-CoA carboxylase. The reaction requires ATP and the B vitamin biotin. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase is a multienzyme protein containing biotin carboxylase, a carboxyl transferase, and a biotin carrier protein which binds biotin. The reaction takes place in two steps. Step 1 is catalyzed by biotin carboxylase, resulting in the carboxylation of biotin and uses ATP. Step 2 is catalyzed by carboxyl transferase, resulting in the transfer of the carboxyl group to acetyl-CoA, forming the product, malonyl-CoA (Figure 23-1). Acetyl-CoA carboxylase has a major role in the regulation of fatty acid synthesis. Biotin is covalently linked to the biotin carrier protein.

## The Fatty Acid Synthase Complex Is a Homodimer of Two Polypeptide Chains Containing Six Enzyme Activities & the Acyl Carrier Protein

The fatty acid synthase complex is a homodimer of two identical polypeptide chains. Each chain of the fatty acid synthase complex contains six enzyme activities and the acyl carrier protein (ACP). The fatty acid synthase complex is arranged in an X shape, as shown by X-ray crystallography. The enzymes in the fatty acid synthase complex are linked in a specific sequence in the primary structure of the protein. The acyl carrier protein (ACP) contains 4'-phosphopantetheine, which includes the vitamin pantothenic acid. The structure of the fatty acid synthase complex allows for the compartmentalization of the fatty acid synthesis process within the cell without the need for permeability barriers. All enzymes in the fatty acid synthase complex are synthesized coordinately because they are encoded by a single gene.

Fatty acid synthesis begins with the combination of acetyl-CoA with a cysteine —SH group on the ACP of one monomer of the fatty acid synthase complex (Figure 23–3, reaction 1a). Malonyl-CoA combines with the adjacent —SH on the 4'-phosphopantetheine of the ACP of the other monomer (reaction 1b). These reactions are catalyzed by malonyl acetyl transacylase. The reaction forms 3-ketoacyl enzyme (acetoacetyl enzyme). Decarboxylation facilitates the reaction's completion by pulling the sequence of reactions forward. The 3-ketoacyl group undergoes reduction by 3-ketoacylreductase. The 3-ketoacyl group undergoes dehydration by dehydratase. The 3-ketoacyl group undergoes further reduction by enoyl reductase to form a saturated acyl enzyme. The saturated acyl residue transfers from the —SH of the 4' phosphopantetheine to the free cysteine —SH group. The transfer makes way for a new malonyl-CoA. This cycle repeats six more times until a saturated 16-carbon acyl radical (palmitoyl) is assembled. The palmitoyl is released from the enzyme complex by thioesterase. Free palmitate must be activated to acyl-CoA to proceed in other metabolic pathways. Possible fates for palmitate include esterification into acylglycerols, chain elongation, desaturation, or esterification into cholesteryl ester.

## The Main Source of NADPH for Lipogenesis Is the Pentose Phosphate Pathway

NADPH is involved as a donor of reducing equivalents in the reduction of the 3-ketoacyl derivatives (see Figure 23-3, reactions 3 and 5). NADPH is involved as a donor of reducing equivalents in the reduction of the 2,3-unsaturated acyl derivatives (see Figure 23-3, reactions 3 and 5).

The oxidative reactions of the pentose phosphate pathway are the chief source of the hydrogen required for the synthesis of fatty acids. Tissues specializing in active lipogenesis, such as the liver, adipose tissue, and the lactating mammary gland, possess an active pentose phosphate pathway. Both the pentose phosphate pathway and lipogenesis occur in the cytosol of the cell, eliminating membranes or permeability barriers against the transfer of NADPH.

Other sources of NADPH include the reaction that converts malate to pyruvate catalyzed by the NADP malate dehydrogenase (malic enzyme) (Figure 23-4). The extramitochondrial isocitrate dehydrogenase reaction is another source of NADPH, particularly in ruminants.

## The provision of Acetyl-CoA for Lipogenesis Requires a Special Transport Mechanism

Acetyl-CoA is formed from glucose via the oxidation of pyruvate in the matrix of the mitochondria.

Acetyl-CoA does not diffuse readily across the mitochondrial membranes.

Citrate is used for the transport of acetyl-CoA into the cytosol.

Citrate is produced by the condensation of acetyl-CoA with oxaloacetate in the citric acid cycle within mitochondria.

Citrate can be translocated into the extramitochondrial compartment via the tricarboxylate transporter.

In the presence of CoA and ATP, citrate undergoes cleavage to acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate by ATP-citrate lyase.

ATP-citrate lyase increases in activity in the well-fed state.

The acetyl-CoA is then available for malonyl-CoA formation and synthesis of fatty acids.

Oxaloacetate can form malate via NADH-linked malate dehydrogenase.

Malate can generate NADPH and pyruvate via the malic enzyme.

NADPH becomes available in the cytosol for lipogenesis.

Pyruvate can be used to regenerate acetyl-CoA after transport into the mitochondrion.

The pathway for transferring reducing equivalents from extramitochondrial NADH to NADP to form NADPH involves the conversion of malate to oxaloacetate and the generation of NADPH for lipogenesis.

The citrate (tricarboxylate) transporter in the mitochondrial membrane requires malate to exchange with citrate.

Malate can be transported into the mitochondrion, where it is able to reform oxaloacetate.

## Elongation of Fatty Acid Chains Occurs in the Endoplasmic Reticulum

The microsomal system elongates saturated and unsaturated fatty acyl-CoAs by two carbons.

The microsomal system uses malonyl-CoA as the acetyl donor.

The microsomal system uses NADPH as the reductant.

The microsomal fatty acid elongase system of enzymes catalyzes the elongation process.

# THE NUTRITIONAL STATE REGULATES LIPOGENESIS

Excess carbohydrate is stored as fat in many animals in anticipation of periods of caloric deficiency such as starvation, hibernation, etc.

Lipogenesis converts surplus glucose and intermediates such as pyruvate, lactate, and acetyl-CoA to fat.

The nutritional state of the organism is the main factor regulating the rate of lipogenesis.

The rate of lipogenesis is high in the well-fed animal whose diet contains a high proportion of carbohydrate.

The rate of lipogenesis is depressed by restricted caloric intake, high-fat diet, or a deficiency of insulin, as in diabetes mellitus.

Restricted caloric intake, high-fat diet, or a deficiency of insulin, as in diabetes mellitus, are associated with increased concentrations of plasma-free fatty acids.

An inverse relationship has been demonstrated between hepatic lipogenesis and the concentration of serum-free fatty acids.

Lipogenesis is increased when sucrose is fed instead of glucose because fructose bypasses the phosphofructokinase control point in glycolysis and floods the lipogenic pathway.

# SHORT- & LONG-TERM MECHANISMS REGULATE LIPOGENESIS

Long-chain fatty acid synthesis is controlled in the short term by allosteric modification of enzymes.

Long-chain fatty acid synthesis is controlled in the short term by covalent modification of enzymes.

Long-chain fatty acid synthesis is controlled in the long term by changes in gene expression.

Changes in gene expression govern the rates of synthesis of enzymes.

## Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase Is the Most Important Enzyme in the Regulation of Lipogenesis

Acetyl-CoA carboxylase is an allosteric enzyme. Citrate activates acetyl-CoA carboxylase. Citrate increases in concentration in the well-fed state. Citrate indicates a plentiful supply of acetyl-CoA. Citrate promotes the conversion of acetyl-CoA carboxylase from an inactive dimer to an active polymeric form. The active polymeric form of acetyl-CoA carboxylase has a molecular mass of several million. Phosphorylation of the enzyme promotes inactivation. Long-chain acyl-CoA molecules promote inactivation. Long-chain acyl-CoA molecules provide negative feedback inhibition. Negative feedback inhibition occurs when a product of a reaction inhibits the enzyme. Acyl-CoA reduces the rate of synthesis of new fatty acid if it accumulates. Acyl-CoA accumulates if it is not esterified quickly enough. Acyl-CoA accumulates because of increased lipolysis. Acyl-CoA accumulates due to an influx of free fatty acids into the tissue. Acyl-CoA inhibits the mitochondrial tricarboxylate transporter. Inhibition of the mitochondrial tricarboxylate transporter prevents activation of the enzyme by citrate. Inhibition of the mitochondrial tricarboxylate transporter decreases the citrate concentration in the cytosol. Decreased citrate concentration in the cytosol favors inactivation of the enzyme (Figure 23–5).

## Insulin Also Regulates Lipogenesis by Other Mechanisms

Insulin stimulates lipogenesis by increasing acetyl-CoA carboxylase activity. Insulin increases the transport of glucose into the cell in adipose tissue. Insulin increases the availability of pyruvate and acetyl-CoA for fatty acid synthesis. Insulin also increases the availability of glycerol-3-phosphate for triacylglycerol synthesis. Insulin converts the inactive form of pyruvate dehydrogenase to the active form in adipose tissue. Insulin does not convert the inactive form of pyruvate dehydrogenase to the active form in the liver. Insulin inhibits lipolysis in adipose tissue by depressing the level of intracellular cAMP. Insulin reduces the concentration of plasma-free fatty acids. Insulin reduces the concentration of long-chain acyl-CoA. Long-chain acyl-CoA inhibits lipogenesis. Plasma-free fatty acids inhibit lipogenesis.

## The Fatty Acid Synthase Complex & Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase Are Adaptive Enzymes

The Fatty Acid Synthase Complex and Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase adapt to the body's physiologic needs through changes in gene expression.

The changes lead to increases in the total amount of enzyme protein present in the fed state.

Changes in gene expression lead to decreases in the total amount of enzyme protein during intake of a high-fat diet.

Changes in gene expression lead to decreases in the total amount of enzyme protein in conditions such as starvation and diabetes mellitus.

Insulin plays an important role in promoting gene expression and induction of enzyme biosynthesis.

Glucagon, via cAMP, antagonizes the effect of insulin on gene expression and enzyme biosynthesis.

Feeding fats containing polyunsaturated fatty acids regulates the inhibition of expression of key enzymes of glycolysis and lipogenesis.

Mechanisms for longer-term regulation of lipogenesis take several days to become fully manifested.

Mechanisms for longer-term regulation of lipogenesis augment the direct and immediate effect of free fatty acids and hormones such as insulin and glucagon.

# THE ESSENTIAL FATTY ACIDS (EFA) HAVE IMPORTANT FUNCTIONS IN THE BODY

Rats fed a purified nonlipid diet containing vitamins A and D exhibit a reduced growth rate. Rats fed a purified nonlipid diet containing vitamins A and D exhibit reproductive deficiency. The addition of linoleic acid to the diet cures the deficiency. The addition of arachidonic acid to the diet cures the deficiency. Essential fatty acids are required for the formation of prostaglandin. Essential fatty acids are required for the formation of thromboxane. Essential fatty acids are required for the formation of leukotriene. Essential fatty acids are required for the formation of lipoxin. Essential fatty acids are found in the structural lipids of the cell, often in position 2 of phospholipids. Essential fatty acids are concerned with the structural integrity of the mitochondrial membrane. Arachidonic acid is present in membranes. # EICOSANOIDS ARE FORMED FROM C20 POLYUNSATURATED FATTY ACIDS

Arachidonate and other C20 polyunsaturated fatty acids give rise to eicosanoids. Eicosanoids include prostaglandins (PG), thromboxanes (TX), leukotrienes (LT), and lipoxins (LX). Eicosanoids are physiologically and pharmacologically active compounds. Eicosanoids are considered to act as local hormones. Eicosanoids function through G-protein–linked receptors to elicit their biochemical effects. There are three groups of eicosanoids synthesized from C20 eicosanoic acids. C20 eicosanoic acids are derived from the essential fatty acids linoleate and α-linolenate. Eicosanoids are also derived from dietary arachidonate and eicosapentaenoate. Arachidonate is usually derived from the position 2 of phospholipids in the plasma membrane. Phospholipase A2 acts on phospholipids to release arachidonate. Arachidonate is the substrate for the synthesis of PG2 and TX2 series by the cyclooxygenase pathway. Arachidonate is also the substrate for the synthesis of LT4 and LX4 series by the lipoxygenase pathway. The cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways compete for arachidonate. The subscript in eicosanoid names denotes the total number of double bonds in the molecule and the series to which the compound belongs. (Figure 23–10)

# THE CYCLOOXYGENASE PATHWAY IS RESPONSIBLE FOR PROSTANOID SYNTHESIS

Prostanoids are synthesized by the cyclooxygenase pathway. Cyclooxygenase (COX), also known as prostaglandin H synthase, catalyzes the first reaction in this pathway. COX exists as two isoenzymes: COX-1 and COX-2. The product of the COX reaction is an endoperoxide called PGH.

Prostanoids are synthesized by many different cell types, but each cell type produces only one type of prostanoid. Cyclooxygenase is a 'suicide enzyme,' meaning it undergoes self-catalyzed destruction, which contributes to the 'switching off' of prostaglandin activity. Prostaglandins are rapidly inactivated by 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase. Sulfasalazine and indomethacin can block this enzyme, thereby prolonging the half-life of prostaglandins in the body.

## Prostanoids Are Potent, Biologically Active Substances

Thromboxanes are synthesized in platelets. Thromboxanes cause vasoconstriction. Thromboxanes cause platelet aggregation. Low-dose aspirin specifically inhibits the synthesis of thromboxanes. Thromboxanes and prostacyclins are antagonistic.

As little as 1 ng/mL of plasma prostaglandins causes contraction of smooth muscle in animals.

## Essential Fatty Acids Do Not Exert All Their Physiologic Effects via Prostaglandin Synthesis

The role of essential fatty acids in membrane formation is unrelated to prostaglandin formation.

Prostaglandins do not relieve symptoms of essential fatty acid deficiency.

An essential fatty acid deficiency is not caused by inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis.

## Essential Fatty Acids Play a Role in the Prevention of Diseases in Humans

Dietary intake of essential fatty acids is associated with a reduction in the development of cardiovascular disease.

Dietary intake of essential fatty acids is associated with a reduction in the development of cancer.

Dietary intake of essential fatty acids is associated with a reduction in the development of arthritis.

Dietary intake of essential fatty acids is associated with a reduction in the development of diabetes mellitus.

Dietary intake of essential fatty acids is associated with a reduction in the development of various neurologic conditions.

Changes in gene expression are believed to be involved in the mechanisms of essential fatty acids.

Prostanoid production is believed to be involved in the mechanisms of essential fatty acids.

Changes in membrane composition are believed to be involved in the mechanisms of essential fatty acids.

## Symptoms of Essential Fatty Acid Deficiency in Humans Include Skin Lesions & Impairment of Lipid Transport

In adults subsisting on ordinary diets, no signs of essential fatty acid deficiencies have been reported.

Infants receiving formula diets low in fat show deficiency symptoms that can be prevented by an essential fatty acid intake of 1 to 2% of the total caloric requirement.

Patients maintained for long periods exclusively by intravenous nutrition low in essential fatty acids show deficiency symptoms that can be prevented by an essential fatty acid intake of 1 to 2% of the total caloric requirement.

Diets with a high P:S (polyunsaturated:saturated fatty acid) ratio reduce serum cholesterol levels.

Diets with a high P:S (polyunsaturated:saturated fatty acid) ratio are beneficial in terms of the risk of development of coronary heart disease.

## Trans Fatty Acids Are Implicated in Various Disorders

Small amounts of trans-unsaturated fatty acids are found in ruminant fat. Butter fat contains 2-7% trans-unsaturated fatty acids. Trans-unsaturated fatty acids arise from the action of microorganisms in the rumen. The main source of trans fatty acids in the human diet is partially hydrogenated vegetable oils. Margarine is an example of a partially hydrogenated vegetable oil.

Trans fatty acids compete with essential fatty acids. Trans fatty acids may exacerbate essential fatty acid deficiency. Trans fatty acids are structurally similar to saturated fatty acids. Trans fatty acids have effects comparable to saturated fatty acids. Trans fatty acids promote hypercholesterolemia. Trans fatty acids promote atherosclerosis.

## Nonsteroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs Inhibit COX

Aspirin is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that inhibits COX-1 and COX-2.

Indomethacin is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID).

Ibuprofen is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID).

Ibuprofen inhibits cyclooxygenases by competing with arachidonate.

Inhibition of COX-1 causes stomach irritation often associated with taking NSAIDs.

Transcription of COX-1 is not inhibited by anti-inflammatory corticosteroids.

## Leukotrienes & Lipoxins Are Potent Regulators of Many Disease Processes

Slow-reacting substance of anaphylaxis (SRS-A) is a potent constrictor of the bronchial airway musculature.

Leukotrienes are important regulators in many diseases involving inflammatory or immediate hypersensitivity reactions, such as asthma.

Leukotrienes are vasoactive.

5-lipoxygenase has been found in arterial walls.

Lipoxins have an anti-inflammatory role in vasoactive and immunoregulatory function.

Lipoxins act as counterregulatory compounds (chalones) of the immune response.

The synthesis of long-chain fatty acids (lipogenesis) is carried out by two enzyme systems: acetyl-CoA carboxylase and fatty acid synthase.
Acetyl-CoA carboxylase converts acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA, and then fatty acid synthase, a multienzyme complex consisting of two identical polypeptide chains, each containing six separate enzymatic activities and ACP, catalyzes the formation of palmitate from one acetyl-CoA and seven malonyl-CoA molecules.
Lipogenesis is regulated at the acetyl-CoA carboxylase step by allosteric modifiers, phosphorylation/dephosphorylation, and induction and repression of enzyme synthesis. The enzyme is allosterically activated by citrate and deactivated by long-chain acyl-CoA. Dephosphorylation (eg, by insulin) promotes its activity, while phosphorylation (eg, by glucagon or epinephrine) is inhibitory.
Biosynthesis of unsaturated long-chain fatty acids is achieved by desaturase and elongase enzymes, which introduce double bonds and lengthen existing acyl chains, respectively.
