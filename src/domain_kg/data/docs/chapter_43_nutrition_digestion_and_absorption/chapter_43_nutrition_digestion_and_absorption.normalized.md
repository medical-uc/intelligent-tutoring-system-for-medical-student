# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

The diet must provide metabolic fuels, mainly carbohydrates and lipids. The diet must provide protein for growth and turnover of tissue proteins, as well as a source of metabolic fuel. The diet must provide fiber for bulk in the intestinal lumen. The diet must provide minerals containing elements with specific metabolic functions. The diet must provide vitamins and essential fatty acids, organic compounds needed in smaller amounts for other metabolic and physiological functions. Polysaccharides must be hydrolyzed to their constituent monosaccharides before absorption and utilization. Triacylglycerols must be hydrolyzed to their constituent fatty acids before absorption and utilization. Proteins must be hydrolyzed to their constituent amino acids before absorption and utilization. Minerals must be released from the complex matrix of food before absorption and utilization. Vitamins must be released from the complex matrix of food before absorption and utilization.

Undernutrition is widespread globally, leading to impaired growth. Undernutrition leads to a defective immune system. Undernutrition leads to reduced work capacity. Excessive calorie consumption in developed countries leads to obesity. Excessive calorie consumption in developing countries leads to obesity. Obesity increases the risk of developing diabetes. Obesity increases the risk of developing cardiovascular disease. Obesity increases the risk of developing some cancers. Worldwide, there are more overweight and obese people than undernourished people. Micronutrient deficiencies of vitamin A, iron, and iodine pose major health concerns in many countries. Deficiencies of other vitamins and minerals are a major cause of ill health. In developed countries, nutrient deficiency is rare. In developed countries, there are vulnerable sections of the population at risk of nutrient deficiency. Intakes of minerals and vitamins that are adequate to prevent deficiency may be inadequate to promote optimum health and longevity.

Inappropriate gastrointestinal secretions of solutes or enzymes can lead to impaired nutrient absorption. Inappropriate gastrointestinal secretions of solutes or enzymes can lead to tissue damage. Excessive secretion of gastric acid, associated with Helicobacter pylori infection, can result in the development of gastric and duodenal ulcers. Small changes in the composition of bile can result in crystallization of cholesterol as gallstones. Failure of exocrine pancreatic secretion leads to malabsorption. Failure of exocrine pancreatic secretion leads to undernutrition. Failure of exocrine pancreatic secretion leads to steatorrhea. Cystic fibrosis leads to failure of exocrine pancreatic secretion. Lactose intolerance is caused by inadequate production of the enzyme lactase by the enterocytes in the intestine. Lactose intolerance leads to diarrhea when lactose is consumed. Lactose intolerance leads to intestinal discomfort when lactose is consumed. Absorption of intact peptides in some people can stimulate antibody responses. Absorption of intact peptides can cause allergic reactions. Celiac disease is an allergic reaction to wheat gluten.

# DIGESTION & ABSORPTION OF CARBOHYDRATES

Carbohydrates make up about 50% of the calories in the western diet. Carbohydrates can be simple sugars, starch, or fiber. Dietary carbohydrates like starch need to be broken down into monosaccharides before absorption by the small intestine. The initial breakdown of polysaccharides into oligosaccharides occurs in the intestinal lumen by enzymes in saliva or from the exocrine pancreas. Oligosaccharides are degraded by enzymes at the brush border of the intestinal epithelium to form monosaccharides. Monosaccharides are absorbed by the intestinal epithelia.

In healthy individuals, plasma glucose levels are well-controlled. People with diabetes or prediabetes struggle to control their glucose levels, especially after a carbohydrate-rich meal. People with diabetes or prediabetes need to be aware of the composition of the carbohydrates they consume because different carbohydrates can impact plasma glucose differently. The glycemic index measures the increase in blood glucose after consuming a carbohydrate compared to an equivalent amount of glucose. Glucose has a glycemic index of 1 or 100%. Galactose has a glycemic index of 1 or 100%. Lactose has a glycemic index of 1 or 100%. Maltose has a glycemic index of 1 or 100%. Isomaltose has a glycemic index of 1 or 100%. Trehalose has a glycemic index of 1 or 100%. Fructose has a lower glycemic index. Sugar alcohols have a lower glycemic index. Sucrose has a lower glycemic index. The glycemic index of starch can vary from 1 to 0, depending on the hydrolysis rate. The glycemic index of nonstarch polysaccharides like cellulose is 0. Foods with a low glycemic index are more beneficial because they cause less fluctuation in glucose and insulin secretion.

Starch provides substrates for bacterial fermentation in the large intestine. Nonstarch polysaccharides that resist hydrolysis provide substrates for bacterial fermentation in the large intestine. Butyrate is a significant energy source for intestinal enterocytes. Other short-chain fatty acids from fermentation are a significant energy source for intestinal enterocytes. Butyrate has antiproliferative activity. Butyrate provides protection against colorectal cancer.

## Amylases Catalyze the Hydrolysis of Starch

Salivary amylase catalyzes the hydrolysis of starch. Pancreatic amylase catalyzes the hydrolysis of starch. The hydrolysis of starch yields dextrins. The hydrolysis of starch yields a mixture of glucose, maltose, and maltotriose. The hydrolysis of starch yields small branched dextrins. Small branched dextrins come from the branchpoints in amylopectin.

## Disaccharidases Are Brush Border Enzymes

The disaccharidases, maltase, sucrase-isomaltase, lactase, and trehalase are located on the brush border of the intestinal mucosal cells. The resultant monosaccharides from these enzymes and those directly ingested are absorbed in the intestinal mucosal cells. Congenital deficiency of lactase occurs rarely in infants. Congenital deficiency of lactase leads to lactose intolerance. Lactose intolerance causes failure to thrive when infants are fed on breast milk or normal infant formula. Congenital deficiency of sucrase-isomaltase occurs among the Inuit. Congenital deficiency of sucrase-isomaltase leads to sucrose intolerance. Sucrose intolerance causes persistent diarrhea and failure to thrive when the diet contains sucrose. In most mammals, lactase activity begins to fall after weaning. In most human beings, lactase activity is almost completely lost by late adolescence. The loss of lactase activity leads to lactose intolerance. Lactose remains in the intestinal lumen when lactase activity is lost. Lactose is a substrate for bacterial fermentation to lactate. Bacterial fermentation to lactate results in abdominal discomfort and diarrhea after consumption of relatively large amounts of lactose. In people of north European origin, lactase persists after weaning and into adult life. In nomadic tribes of sub-Saharan Africa and Arabia, lactase persists after weaning and into adult life. Marine mammals secrete a high-fat milk that contains no carbohydrate. The pups of marine mammals lack lactase.

Glucose and galactose compete with each other for intestinal absorption. Fructose and sugar alcohols are absorbed by carrier-mediated diffusion. Fructose and sugar alcohols are not actively transported. Fructose and sugar alcohols are absorbed down their concentration gradient. After a moderately high intake, fructose and sugar alcohols may remain in the intestinal lumen. Remaining fructose and sugar alcohols act as a substrate for bacterial fermentation. Large intakes of fructose and sugar alcohols can lead to osmotic diarrhea.

# DIGESTION & ABSORPTION OF LIPIDS

The major lipids in the diet are triacylglycerols and phospholipids. Triacylglycerols and phospholipids are hydrophobic and need to be hydrolyzed and emulsified into small droplets called micelles before absorption. The fat-soluble vitamins, A, D, E, and K, are absorbed in these lipid micelles. Cholesterol is absorbed in these lipid micelles. Carotenes are absorbed in these lipid micelles. Absorption of carotenes is impaired on a very low-fat diet. Absorption of fat-soluble vitamins is impaired on a very low-fat diet.

Hydrolysis of triacylglycerols is initiated by lingual and gastric lipases. Lingual and gastric lipases attack the sn-3 ester bond. The attack by lingual and gastric lipases forms 1,2-diacylglycerols and free fatty acids. Free fatty acids act as emulsifying agents. Lingual and gastric lipases prefer short- and medium-chain triglycerides. Short- and medium-chain triglycerides are the major lipids in breast milk. Pancreatic lipase is secreted into the small intestine. Pancreatic lipase requires colipase for activity. Pancreatic lipase targets the primary ester links at positions 1 and 3 in triacylglycerols. The targeting by pancreatic lipase results in 2-monoacylglycerols and free fatty acids as the major end products. Inhibitors of pancreatic lipase are used to treat severe obesity by inhibiting triacylglycerol hydrolysis. Neonates rely more on gastric lipase because their pancreatic lipase production is not fully developed.

Bile salts are formed in the liver and secreted in the bile. Bile salts emulsify dietary fat into large droplets. Emulsification by bile salts increases the surface area for lipase action. Pancreatic phospholipase A2 hydrolyzes the phospholipid coat on the droplet. Hydrolysis by pancreatic phospholipase A2 releases fatty acids and lysophosphatidylcholine. Water solubility of micelles allows the products of lipase activity, such as fatty acids and monoglycerides, and fat-soluble vitamins to diffuse through the intestinal lumen to the brush border of the mucosal cells. Most lipid absorption occurs in the proximal two-thirds of the jejunum. Bile salts remain in the intestinal lumen. Bile salts are absorbed in the ileum into the enterohepatic circulation.

Within the intestinal epithelium, 1-monoacylglycerols are hydrolyzed to fatty acids and glycerol. Within the intestinal epithelium, 2-monoacylglycerols are reacylated to triacylglycerols via the monoacylglycerol pathway. Glycerol released in the intestinal lumen is absorbed into the hepatic portal vein. Glycerol released within the epithelium is reused for triacylglycerol synthesis via the phosphatidic acid pathway. Long-chain fatty acids are esterified to triacylglycerol in the mucosal cells. Long-chain fatty acids are secreted as chylomicrons into the lymphatics. Short- and medium-chain fatty acids are mainly absorbed into the hepatic portal vein as free fatty acids.

Cholesterol is absorbed dissolved in lipid micelles. Cholesterol is mainly esterified in the intestinal mucosa before being incorporated into chylomicrons. Plant sterols inhibit the absorption of both dietary and biliary cholesterol. Stanols inhibit the absorption of both dietary and biliary cholesterol. Inhibition by plant sterols and stanols lowers the whole body cholesterol content and plasma cholesterol concentration.

# DIGESTION & ABSORPTION OF PROTEINS

Native proteins are resistant to digestion.

Few peptide bonds are accessible to proteolytic enzymes without prior denaturation.

Heat in cooking causes denaturation of proteins.

Gastric acid causes denaturation of proteins.

## Several Groups of Enzymes Catalyze the Digestion of Proteins

There are two main classes of proteolytic digestive enzymes (proteases). Endopeptidases hydrolyze peptide bonds between specific amino acids throughout the molecule. Pepsin in the gastric juice catalyzes hydrolysis of peptide bonds adjacent to amino acids with bulky side-chains. Trypsin, chymotrypsin, and elastase are secreted into the small intestine by the pancreas. Trypsin catalyzes hydrolysis of lysine and arginine amides. Chymotrypsin catalyzes hydrolysis of amides of aromatic amino acids. Elastase catalyzes hydrolysis of amides of small neutral aliphatic amino acids. Exopeptidases catalyze the hydrolysis of peptide bonds, one at a time, from the ends of peptides. Carboxypeptidases, secreted in the pancreatic juice, release amino acids from the free carboxyl terminal. Aminopeptidases, secreted by the intestinal mucosal cells, release amino acids from the amino terminal. Dipeptidases and tripeptidases in the brush border of intestinal mucosal cells catalyze the hydrolysis of di- and tripeptides. Di- and tripeptides are not substrates for amino- and carboxypeptidases. The proteases are secreted as inactive zymogens. The active site of the enzyme is masked by a small region of the peptide chain that is removed by hydrolysis of a specific peptide bond. The secretion of inactive zymogens that need to be activated in the intestinal or gastric lumen protects against autodigestion of the tissues secreting the protease. In the gastric lumen, pepsinogen is activated to pepsin by gastric acid. Activated pepsin can also activate pepsinogen. In the small intestine, trypsinogen, the precursor of trypsin, is activated by enteropeptidase. Enteropeptidase is secreted by the duodenal epithelial cells. Once trypsin is active in the lumen, it can then activate the other zymogens secreted by the pancreas. Trypsin activates chymotrypsinogen to chymotrypsin. Trypsin activates proelastase to elastase. Trypsin activates procarboxypeptidase to carboxypeptidase. Trypsin activates proaminopeptidase to aminopeptidase.

## Free Amino Acids & Small Peptides Are Absorbed by Different Mechanisms

The end product of the action of endopeptidases and exopeptidases is a mixture of free amino acids, di- and tripeptides, and oligopeptides. All of these are absorbed.

Carbohydrate digestion results in the absorption of only monosaccharides.

Free amino acids are absorbed across the intestinal mucosa by sodium-dependent active transport.

Several different amino acid transporters exist. Each amino acid transporter has specificity for the nature of the amino acid side chain, such as large or small, neutral, acidic, or basic.

Amino acids carried by any one transporter compete with each other for absorption. Amino acids carried by any one transporter compete with each other for tissue uptake.

Dipeptides enter the brush border of the intestinal mucosal cells using human peptide transporters, such as PEPT1 and PET2. Tripeptides enter the brush border of the intestinal mucosal cells using human peptide transporters, such as PEPT1 and PET2.

In the mucosal cell, transported peptides are hydrolyzed to free amino acids. Free amino acids are then transported into the hepatic portal vein.

Drugs such as angiotensin-converting-enzyme (ACE) inhibitors use this transport system. Some antibiotics use this transport system.

Relatively large peptides may be absorbed intact by uptake into mucosal epithelial cells (transcellular). Relatively large peptides may be absorbed intact by passing between epithelial cells (paracellular).

Peptides are large enough to stimulate antibody formation.

Environmental factors might be the basis of allergic reactions to foods. Changes in the intestinal microbiome might be the basis of allergic reactions to foods.

In the neonatal period, large polypeptide macromolecules can cross the intestine. In the neonatal period, the intestine is less developed. In the neonatal period, immunoglobulins from the mother can cross the intestine.

# DIGESTION & ABSORPTION OF VITAMINS & MINERALS

Vitamins and minerals are released from food during digestion.

The availability of vitamins and minerals depends on the type of food.

The availability of minerals is especially influenced by the presence of chelating compounds.

Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed in the lipid micelles that result from fat digestion.

Water-soluble vitamins are absorbed from the small intestine either by active transport or by carrier-mediated diffusion.

Most mineral salts are absorbed from the small intestine either by active transport or by carrier-mediated diffusion.

Water-soluble vitamins bind to intracellular proteins to achieve concentrative uptake.

Most mineral salts bind to intracellular proteins to achieve concentrative uptake.

Calcium absorption is dependent on vitamin D.

Zinc absorption requires a zinc-binding ligand secreted by the exocrine pancreas.

The absorption of iron is limited.

## Calcium Absorption Is Dependent on Vitamin D

Vitamin D is required for the intestinal absorption of calcium. Approximately 1/3 of dietary calcium is absorbed. 50% of absorbed calcium is regulated by the active form of vitamin D, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D or calcitriol. Individuals with kidney disease have an impaired ability to hydroxylate vitamin D to make calcitriol. Individuals with kidney disease are prone to abnormal calcium metabolism. Synthesis of the intracellular calcium-binding protein, calbindin, required for calcium absorption, is induced by calcitriol. Calcitriol acts to recruit calcium transporters to the cell surface, so increasing calcium absorption rapidly. Calcitriol acts to recruit calcium transporters to the cell surface, so increasing calcium absorption rapidly. This process is independent of new protein synthesis.

Calcium bioavailability can be inhibited by substances in the diet. Phytic acid (inositol hexaphosphate) in cereals binds calcium in the intestinal lumen, preventing its absorption. Phytic acid (inositol hexaphosphate) also chelates other minerals, including zinc. Phytic acid (inositol hexaphosphate) is mainly a problem among people who consume large amounts of unleavened whole-wheat products. Yeast contains an enzyme, phytase, that dephosphorylates phytate, rendering it inactive. High concentrations of fatty acids in the intestinal lumen, because of impaired fat absorption, can also reduce calcium absorption by forming insoluble calcium salts. A high intake of oxalate can sometimes cause deficiency since calcium oxalate is insoluble.

## Iron Absorption Is Limited & Strictly Controlled by Hepcidin

Iron is an essential element. Most iron is insoluble and poorly bioavailable. Humans conserve total body iron stores. The majority of total body iron stores resides in the hemoglobin of red blood cells. About 0.1% of total body iron stores is lost daily. Dietary iron absorption replaces the lost iron. Iron deficiency commonly manifests as anemia. Iron deficiency is a prevalent issue in both developed and developing countries. Blood loss is the main cause of iron deficiency. Ulcers can cause blood loss leading to iron deficiency. Injury can cause blood loss leading to iron deficiency. Intestinal bleeding secondary to malignancy can cause blood loss leading to iron deficiency. Inadequate dietary iron absorption is not the main cause of iron deficiency. About 10% of the population is genetically at risk of iron overload, known as hemochromatosis. Mutations in the hereditary hemochromatosis gene (HFE) regulate hepcidin. Hemochromatosis is more common in the white population of European backgrounds. Iron overload increases the risk of adverse effects from nonenzymatic generation of free radicals by iron salts. Strict regulation of iron absorption is necessary due to the risk of iron overload.

Inorganic iron is transported into the mucosal cell by a proton-linked divalent metal ion transporter. Inorganic iron accumulates intracellularly by binding to ferritin. Iron leaves the mucosal cell via the transport protein ferroportin. Iron leaves the mucosal cell only if there is free transferrin in the plasma to bind to. Once transferrin is saturated with iron, any iron that has accumulated in the mucosal cells is lost to the feces when the cells are shed. The expression of the ferroportin gene is downregulated by hepcidin, a peptide secreted by the liver. The expression of the gene for the divalent metal ion transporter is possibly downregulated by hepcidin. This is a classic negative feedback loop. When body iron reserves are adequate, hepcidin increases to limit absorption. In response to increased iron demand due to increased red blood cell synthesis, the synthesis of hepcidin is reduced. Reduced hepcidin synthesis leads to increased synthesis of ferroportin. Increased synthesis of ferroportin leads to increased iron absorption (Figure 43-3).

The presence of reducing agents enhances absorption of inorganic iron. Vitamin C is the most effective reducing compound for enhancing iron absorption. Recent clinical trials suggest that increasing vitamin C intake may not provide added benefit in treating anemia beyond taking additional iron. # ENERGY BALANCE: OVER- & UNDERNUTRITION

The body's first requirement after water is for metabolic fuels, which include fats, carbohydrates, and amino acids from proteins.

Food intake in excess of energy expenditure leads to obesity.

Food intake less than energy expenditure leads to emaciation and wasting.

Both obesity and severe undernutrition are associated with increased mortality.

The body mass index is calculated as weight (in kg) divided by height squared (in m).

A desirable range for body mass index is between 20 and 25.

## Energy Requirements Are Estimated by Measurement of Energy Expenditure

Daily energy expenditure can be determined by measuring heat output from the body. Daily energy expenditure is normally estimated indirectly by measuring the rate of oxygen consumption. Indirect calorimetry is used to estimate daily energy expenditure. There is an energy expenditure of ~20 kJ/L of oxygen consumed. The energy expenditure per liter of oxygen consumed is the same regardless of the fuel being metabolized, whether it is carbohydrate, fat, or protein. The amount of carbon dioxide produced per volume of oxygen consumed differs between nutrients. The respiratory quotient (RQ) is the ratio of the volume of carbon dioxide produced to the volume of oxygen consumed. The RQ indicates the mixture of metabolic fuels being oxidized.

Water is used in many metabolic reactions. The tracer will enter metabolic pathways. The difference in the rate of loss of the two labels permits estimation of total carbon dioxide production over a few weeks. If a person is weight stable during that period, they are in neutral energy balance (calorie intake = energy expenditure). If one knows the average composition of the diet being consumed and thus being oxidized, one can estimate the RQ of the mixture of substrates being oxidized.

Basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the energy expenditure by the body when at rest, but not asleep, under controlled conditions of thermal neutrality, measured about 12 hours after the last meal. BMR depends on weight, age, and gender. Total energy expenditure during a 24-hour period is the sum of the BMR, the energy required for physical activity, and the energy cost of synthesizing reserves in the fed state (thermic effect of food). BMR contributes about 50-65% to total energy expenditure in the average individual. The thermic effect of food contributes about 10-15% to total energy expenditure in the average individual. Physical activity contributes about 20% to total energy expenditure in the average individual. The contribution of the thermic effect of food can be estimated from the calories consumed. Body weight, age, and gender are large determinants of BMR. It is possible to estimate an individual's energy requirement from body weight, age, gender, and level of physical activity. Body weight affects BMR because there is a greater amount of active tissue (lean muscle mass) in a larger body. The decrease in BMR with increasing age, even when body weight remains constant, is the result of muscle tissue replacement by adipose tissue, which is metabolically less active. Women have a significantly lower BMR than do men of the same body weight and age because women's bodies contain proportionally more adipose tissue.

## Energy Requirements Increase With Activity

The most useful way of expressing the energy cost of physical activities is as a multiple of BMR. The most useful way of expressing the energy cost of physical activities is known as the physical activity ratio (PAR) or metabolic equivalent of the task (MET). Sedentary activities use only about 1.1 to 1.2 × BMR. Vigorous exertion, such as climbing stairs, cross-country walking uphill, etc, may use 6 to 8 × BMR. The overall physical activity level (PAL) is the sum of the PAR of different activities, multiplied by the time taken for that activity, divided by 24 hours.

## Ten Percent of the Energy Yield of a Meal Is Expended in Forming Reserves

There is a considerable increase in metabolic rate after a meal. The increase in metabolic rate after a meal is known as the thermic effect of food. The thermic effect of food accounts for about 10% of the calories ingested. A small part of this energy is used for secreting digestive enzymes. A small part of this energy is used for active transport of the products of digestion. The major part of this energy is used for synthesizing reserves of glycogen. The major part of this energy is used for synthesizing reserves of triacylglycerol. The major part of this energy is used for synthesizing reserves of protein.

## There Are Two Extreme Forms of Undernutrition

Marasmus can occur in both adults and children. Marasmus occurs in vulnerable groups of all populations. Marasmus occurs primarily due to calorie deficiency with accompanying nutrient deficiency. Kwashiorkor affects only children. Kwashiorkor is seen in children after weaning from breast milk to a diet with adequate calories but very low in protein. Kwashiorkor has been reported only in developing countries. Kwashiorkor is primarily due to protein malnutrition. Kwashiorkor is distinguished by fluid retention, leading to edema. Kwashiorkor involves fatty infiltration of the liver. Marasmus is a state of extreme emaciation. Marasmus is the outcome of prolonged negative energy balance. In marasmus, the body's fat reserves have been exhausted. In marasmus, there is wastage of muscle. In marasmus, as the condition progresses, there is loss of protein from the heart, liver, and kidneys. In marasmus, the amino acids released by the catabolism of tissue proteins are used as a source of metabolic fuel. In marasmus, the amino acids released by the catabolism of tissue proteins are used as substrates for gluconeogenesis. In marasmus, gluconeogenesis maintains a supply of glucose for the brain and red blood cells. In marasmus, the reduced net synthesis of proteins results in an impaired immune response. In marasmus, the impaired immune response increases the risk of infection. In marasmus, impairment of cell proliferation in the normally rapidly dividing intestinal mucosa occurs. In marasmus, the reduction in the surface area of the intestinal mucosa reduces nutrient absorption.

## Patients With Advanced Disease Are Often Malnourished

Patients with advanced cancer, advanced heart failure, HIV infection and AIDS, and other chronic diseases are frequently undernourished. Cachexia involves more loss of body protein than occurs in starvation. In cachexia, total body weight is not necessarily decreased because fat mass can be increased. In marasmus, protein synthesis is reduced but catabolism is unaffected. In cachexia, cytokines increase the catabolism of tissue protein by the ATP-dependent ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Cytokines lead to increased stimulation of mitochondrial uncoupling proteins, resulting in thermogenesis and increased oxidation of metabolic fuels in some tissues. Patients with advanced disease can be hypermetabolic, meaning they have a considerably increased basal metabolic rate (BMR).

In cachexia, protein synthesis is impaired in muscle. In cachexia, protein synthesis is impaired in the intestine. The liver shows marked augmentation in the synthesis of specific proteins like fibronectin. This protein synthesis is partly driven by the high inflammatory state seen in these diseases. Cytokines amplify glucose conversion to lactate. Lactate is then used for gluconeogenesis in the liver. Gluconeogenesis in the liver is an energy-consuming process. This creates a futile cycle between the liver and peripheral tissues. Futile cycling of lipids occurs because hormone-sensitive lipase is activated by increased autonomic tone to adipose tissue. Futile cycling of lipids occurs because of impaired insulin action. Impaired insulin action should normally suppress lipolysis. This results in the liberation of fatty acids from adipose tissue. Fatty acids are reesterified to triacylglycerols in the liver. Reesterification of fatty acids to triacylglycerols is an ATP-expensive process. The liver exports these triacylglycerols in very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL).

Cachectic patients are catabolic even when they are taking in calories. Catabolism in cachectic patients is exacerbated by accompanying insulin resistance. Insulin resistance is necessary for maintaining protein stores in cachectic patients. The ubiquitin-proteasome pathway of protein catabolism is activated in cachectic patients.

## Protein Requirements Can Be Determined by Measuring Nitrogen Balance

The state of protein nutrition can be determined by measuring the dietary intake and output of nitrogenous compounds from the body. Protein is the major dietary source of nitrogen. Measurement of total nitrogen intake gives a good estimate of protein intake. Nitrogen is 16% of most proteins' mass. The output of nitrogen from the body is mainly in urea. The output of nitrogen from the body is in smaller quantities of other compounds in urine. The output of nitrogen from the body is in undigested protein in feces. The output of nitrogen from the body may also be lost in sweat and shed skin. The difference between intake and output of nitrogenous compounds is known as nitrogen balance.

In a healthy adult, there is nitrogen equilibrium. In a healthy adult, intake equals output. In a healthy adult, there is no change in the total body content of protein. In a growing child, there is a positive nitrogen balance. In a pregnant woman, there is a positive nitrogen balance. In a person in recovery from protein loss, there is a positive nitrogen balance. In positive nitrogen balance, the excretion of nitrogenous compounds is less than the dietary intake. In positive nitrogen balance, there is net retention of nitrogen in the body as protein. In response to trauma, there is a negative nitrogen balance. In response to infection, there is a negative nitrogen balance. If the intake of protein is inadequate to meet requirements, there is a negative nitrogen balance. In negative nitrogen balance, there is a net loss of protein nitrogen from the body.

Except when replacing protein losses, nitrogen equilibrium can be maintained at any level of protein intake above requirements. A high intake of protein does not necessarily lead to positive nitrogen balance. A high intake of protein may increase the rate of protein synthesis in some tissues, such as liver or skin repair in a burn patient. A high intake of protein does not necessarily increase the rate of protein synthesis in lean muscle tissues that are catabolic. As protein catabolism persists, nitrogen equilibrium is maintained. The cost of maintaining nitrogen equilibrium is a higher rate of protein turnover. Both protein synthesis and catabolism are ATP expensive. The increased rate of protein turnover explains the increased diet-induced thermogenesis seen in people consuming a high-protein diet.

The continual catabolism of tissue proteins creates the requirement for dietary protein, even in an adult who is not growing. Some of the amino acids released can be reutilized. Much of the carbon backbone is used for gluconeogenesis. Nitrogen balance studies show that the average daily requirement is 0.66 g of protein per kg body weight. A reference intake of 0.825 g of protein/kg body weight allows for individual variation. The average daily protein requirement is approximately 55 g/day. The average daily protein requirement is 8 to 9% of energy intake. Average intakes of protein in developed countries are of the order of 80 to 100 g/day. Average intakes of protein in developed countries are 14 to 15% of energy intake.

Because body protein increases in growing children, they have a proportionally greater requirement than adults. Growing children should be in positive nitrogen balance. The need for protein in growing children is relatively small compared with the requirement for protein turnover. In some countries, protein intake is inadequate to meet these requirements, resulting in stunting of growth. There is little or no evidence that athletes and body builders require large amounts of protein. Simply consuming more of a normal diet providing about 14% of energy from protein will provide more than enough protein for increased muscle protein synthesis, assuming the proteins are of high quality. The main requirement for increased muscle protein synthesis is an increased energy intake.

## There Is a Loss of Body Protein in Response to Trauma & Infection

A major trauma, such as a burn, a broken limb, or surgery, increases the net catabolism of tissue proteins. Cytokines and glucocorticoid hormones contribute to this increase in protein catabolism. Excessive utilization of threonine and cysteine in the synthesis of acute-phase proteins leads to catabolism of lean body tissues. As much as 6 to 7% of the total body protein may be lost over 10 days following a major trauma.

Prolonged bed rest results in considerable loss of protein due to muscle atrophy. Protein catabolism may be increased in response to cytokines during prolonged bed rest. Exercise is necessary to completely replace lost protein, and without it, replacement is not complete.

During convalescence, lost protein is replaced when there is positive nitrogen balance. A normal diet is adequate to permit this replacement protein synthesis, similar to the protein synthesis needs of athletes.

## The Requirement Is Not Just for Protein, but for Specific Amino Acids

Not all proteins are nutritionally equivalent. Different proteins contain different amounts of various amino acids. The body's requirement is for amino acids in the correct proportions to replace tissue proteins. Amino acids can be divided into two groups: essential and nonessential. There are nine essential or indispensable amino acids: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, and valine. These essential amino acids cannot be synthesized in the body. If one of these essential amino acids is lacking or inadequate, it will not be possible to maintain nitrogen balance. Nitrogen balance cannot be maintained without enough of an essential amino acid for protein synthesis. Cysteine and tyrosine can be synthesized in the body, but only from essential amino acid precursors. Cysteine is synthesized from methionine. Tyrosine is synthesized from phenylalanine. The dietary intakes of cysteine and tyrosine affect the requirements for methionine and phenylalanine. The remaining 11 amino acids in proteins are considered nonessential or dispensable. Nonessential amino acids can be synthesized as long as there is enough total protein in the diet. If one of these nonessential amino acids is omitted from the diet, nitrogen balance can still be maintained. Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate are truly dispensable. Alanine is synthesized by transamination of pyruvate. Aspartate is synthesized by transamination of oxaloacetate. Glutamate is synthesized by transamination of ketoglutarate. The remaining amino acids are considered nonessential, but under some circumstances, the requirement may outstrip the capacity for their synthesis. For the elderly, loss of lean body mass leads to poor outcomes. Supplementing with high-quality protein that is full of essential amino acids helps limit the loss of lean mass.

Digestion involves hydrolyzing food molecules into smaller molecules for absorption through the gastrointestinal epithelium. Polysaccharides are absorbed as monosaccharides, triacylglycerols as 2-monoacylglycerols, fatty acids and glycerol, and proteins as amino acids and small peptides.
In addition to water, the diet must provide metabolic fuels (carbohydrate and fat) for body growth and activity, protein for synthesis of tissue proteins, fiber for bulk in the intestinal contents, minerals for specific metabolic functions (see Chapter 44), polyunsaturated fatty acids of the n-3 and n-6 families, and vitamins—organic compounds needed in small amounts for other essential functions (see Chapter 44).
Undernutrition occurs in two extreme forms: marasmus, in adults and children, and kwashiorkor in children. Chronic illness can also lead to undernutrition and protein wasting (cachexia).
Overnutrition leads to excess energy intake and is associated with chronic noncommunicable diseases such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, atherosclerosis, cancer, and hypertension.
\- Twenty different amino acids are required for protein synthesis, of which nine are essential in the human diet. The quantity of protein required can be determined by studies of nitrogen balance and is affected by protein quality—the amounts of essential amino acids present in dietary proteins compared with the amounts required for tissue protein synthesis.
