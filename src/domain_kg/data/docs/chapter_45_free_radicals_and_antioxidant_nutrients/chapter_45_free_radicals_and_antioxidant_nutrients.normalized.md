# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Damage by reactive oxygen species (ROS) to lipids, nucleic acids, and proteins limits lifespan. Damage by reactive oxygen species (ROS) contributes to the development of diseases, including atherosclerosis, cancer, and autoimmune diseases. Living organisms generate ROS in physiologic processes including energy metabolism. Living organisms generate ROS in innate immune cells responses to pathogens. ROS can also be generated by exposure to environmental sources. The body deploys a broad network of antioxidant defenses to limit the accumulation of oxidative damage by ROS. Antioxidant defenses include antioxidant enzymes. Antioxidant defenses include nonenzymatic antioxidants. Antioxidant defenses include repair enzymes. Therapeutic targeting of ROS or oxidative damage has been largely unsuccessful to date in ameliorating disease. Therapeutic targeting of ROS or oxidative damage has been largely unsuccessful to date in extending lifespan.

## Aerobic Metabolism as a Source of ROS

Aerobic organisms use molecular oxygen for oxidative metabolism through the mitochondrial electron transport chain. The coupling increases the protonmotive force gradient, which is then used to generate ATP. Inefficiencies in electron transfers at various catalytic sites along the electron transport chain allow leakage of reducing equivalents directly to molecular oxygen.

The electron leakage tends to increase when the protonmotive force gradient is very high. High gradients occur when cells are saturated with energy substrates and consequently reducing equivalents. Some 3 to 5% of the roughly 30 mol of oxygen consumed daily by an adult human is converted to ROS. Some 3 to 5% of the roughly 30 mol of oxygen consumed daily by an adult human results in a daily production of about 1.5 mol of ROS. Without an extensive antioxidant network, this amount of ROS would cause tissue dysfunction and death.

Mitochondrial ROS formation plays important roles in cell signaling. Mitochondrial ROS formation is necessary for cells to adapt to varying bioenergetic conditions. Completely eliminating mitochondrial ROS formation harms cells.

## Innate Immune Cells as a Source of ROS

Innate immune cells use ROS formation to kill invading pathogens. Innate immune cells express ROS-producing enzymes like NADPH oxidases (NOXs), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and myeloperoxidase (MPO). NOXs use NADPH to produce superoxide. iNOS uses NADPH to produce nitric oxide. Superoxide and nitric oxide can combine to form peroxynitrite. Phagocytes increase their glucose utilization via the pentose phosphate pathway to reduce NADP+ back to NADPH. Reducing NADP+ back to NADPH enables large amounts of ROS generation in a respiratory burst. Neutrophils store MPO in their granules. Macrophages store MPO in their granules. MPO uses hydrogen peroxide and halides to produce hypohalous acids. Hypohalous acids are highly damaging ROS.

## Environmental Sources of ROS and ROS Generators

Humans are exposed to various environmental sources of ROS.

Ionizing radiation is emitted by x-ray machines and computed tomography (CT) scanners.

Ultraviolet radiation is a form of sunlight.

Increased adiposity and saturated fatty acids in the diet contribute to ROS generation.

Absorption of light energy by iron clusters and organic compounds in the skin and underlying tissue forms ROS.

Cigarette smoke contains high concentrations of various free radical species including ROS.

# ROS CAUSE OXIDATIVE DAMAGE TO LIPID, DNA, & PROTEINS WHICH LEADS TO DISEASE

Oxidative damage by reactive oxygen species (ROS) causes lipids, nucleic acids, and proteins to lose their normal function or to act in unintended ways that harm the organism. ROS damage these macromolecules and contribute to the development of diseases such as atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, autoimmune diseases, and cancer, as well as the aging process. Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) have multiple double bonds and are essential for membrane fluidity, which is crucial for proper cell function. Lipid peroxidation involves initiation, peroxidation, propagation, and termination steps. Reactive lipid carbonyls chemically modify proteins and nucleic acids, significantly altering their function. Excessive lipid peroxidation and formation of oxidized phospholipids drive ferroptosis, a form of cell death that may play a role in various diseases. (Figure 45–3)

## Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease

Reactive lipid carbonyls such as MDA modify apoB, the main apolipoprotein of plasma low-density lipoprotein (LDL).

Reactive lipid carbonyls such as MDA modify apoB, the main apolipoprotein of plasma low-density lipoprotein (LDL).

LDL is normally taken up by the LDL receptor expressed in the liver.

The LDL receptor helps dispose of cholesterol and reduces cholesterol synthesis by the liver.

Instead of being taken up by the liver LDL receptor, MDA-modified LDL is taken up by scavenger receptors on macrophages.

MDA-modified LDL uptake causes macrophages to become engorged with cholesterol.

Engorged macrophages are known as foam cells.

Foam cells are an initial step in the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced oxidative damage drives many other important steps in the development of atherosclerotic lesions.

## Autoimmune Diseases

When cellular proteins are normally recognized by adaptive immune cells as self-proteins, they are not targeted for an immune response. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) can modify these cellular proteins. Reactive lipid carbonyls can also modify these cellular proteins. Modification by ROS or reactive lipid carbonyls can cause these proteins to be identified as foreign proteins. When modified self-proteins are identified as foreign, they are called neo-antigens. The adaptive immune cells attack and kill cells expressing these neo-antigens. Adaptive immune cells attack by adaptive immune cells initiates an autoimmune response. ROS-induced neo-antigen formation contributes to the development of systemic lupus erythematosus. ROS-induced neo-antigen formation contributes to the development of rheumatoid arthritis. ROS-induced neo-antigen formation contributes to the development of type 1 diabetes. ROS-induced neo-antigen formation contributes to the development of some forms of hypertension.

## Cancer

DNA modification by ROS or reactive lipid carbonyls can cause this modified DNA to be misread by transcriptional machinery.

DNA modification by ROS or reactive lipid carbonyls can be transmitted into daughter cells if DNA repair machinery is unable to properly restore the original base code sequence, resulting in permanent mutations.

Exposure to exogenous producers of ROS can drive cancer cell formation, as demonstrated by the effect of UV exposure on skin cancer and cigarette smoking on lung cancer.

There is also evidence for endogenous ROS production contributing to carcinogenesis.

Cancer cells typically highly upregulate their antioxidant defenses.

Endogenous ROS production by the immune system plays an important role in limiting cancer development and progression.

## Aging

Senescent cells in older adults accumulate lipofuscin, the so-called pigment of aging. Lipofuscin forms through the aggregation of oxidized lipids and proteins. Reductions in antioxidant defenses hasten the onset of many aging phenotypes. Accumulation of sufficient oxidative damage limits cell function. Accumulation of sufficient oxidative damage causes cell death. Whether accumulation of oxidative damage is the primary driver of biological aging has not yet been adequately established.

# ANTIOXIDANT DEFENSES

Without multiple layers of antioxidant defenses, we would quickly succumb to oxidative damage inflicted by ROS. Enzymatic antioxidants provide the first layer of defense. Nonenzymatic antioxidants provide a second important layer of defense. Nonenzymatic antioxidants are derived from our diets. Nonenzymatic antioxidants serve as sacrificial substrates for oxidation. Nonenzymatic antioxidants break radical chain reactions. Nonenzymatic antioxidants are less efficient than enzymes. Enzymes repair or remove oxidatively damaged nucleic acids and proteins, providing a final layer of defense. Experimental evidence suggests that significant defects in any of these defensive layers can lead to oxidative damage and disease.

## Enzymatic Antioxidants

Superoxide dismutases (SODs) catalyze the dismutation of superoxide radical to hydrogen peroxide. Mutations in one isoform of SOD (SOD1) cause amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).

Peroxidases that inactivate hydrogen peroxide include catalase and glutathione peroxidases (GPXs). Catalase is a ubiquitously expressed heme-containing peroxidase. Catalase is predominantly found in peroxisomes. Catalase catalyzes the conversion of hydrogen peroxide to water and molecular oxygen. Catalase has one of the highest turnover rates ever measured for an enzyme.

Most glutathione peroxidase (GPX) isoforms utilize selenocysteine rather than cysteine as their catalytic residue. The selenol (R-SeH) moiety in glutathione peroxidase (GPX) undergoes oxidation to selenenic acid (R-Se-OH) by hydrogen peroxide or lipid peroxides. To complete the catalytic cycle, glutathione peroxidase (GPX) utilizes two molecules of glutathione (GSH) to convert the selenenic acid back to the selenol. The conversion of selenenic acid back to the selenol simultaneously produces an oxidized glutathione dimer (GS-SG). The glutathione dimer can be recycled back to GSH using NADPH generated from the pentose phosphate pathway and glutathione reductase. The reaction catalyzed by glutathione peroxidase (GPXs) is roughly 50,000,000 times faster than the reaction of free glutathione with hydrogen peroxide.

Deletion of GPX4 is embryonic lethal. Mutations that reduce GPX4 activity increase the risk of many age-related diseases. Chemical inhibition of GPX4 induces ferroptosis. Ferroptosis is a form of cell death characterized by a rapid rise in oxidized phospholipids that is iron dependent. Ferroptosis appears to play critical roles in Alzheimer disease. Ferroptosis appears to play critical roles in Parkinson disease. Ferroptosis appears to play critical roles in ischemia/reperfusion injury.

## Nonenzymatic Antioxidants

Some nonenzymatic antioxidants are synthesized endogenously by humans, such as glutathione and polyamines. Various plant-derived polyphenols, such as quercetin or gallic acid, contribute to the body's capacity to buffer against ROS. Plant-derived polyphenols are not considered essential. Dietary polyphenols can enhance antioxidant defenses by triggering xenobiotic responses, such as the Keap1/NRF2-mediated pathways, which induce the expression of antioxidant and oxidative repair enzymes.

Endogenous antioxidants include glutathione and polyamines. Polyamines are synthesized using arginine and methionine. Polyamines are synthesized through the actions of arginase, ornithine decarboxylase, spermidine synthase, and spermine synthase. Polyamines can also be obtained from the diet. Polyamines react with reactive lipid carbonyls such as MDA. Polyamines react with ROS. Glutathione is synthesized from cysteine, glutamate, and cysteine by glutamate cysteine ligase and glutathione synthetase. Glutathione is present at concentrations of 1 to 10 mM in cells. Glutathione serves as a co-factor for many oxidoreductase enzymes besides GPXs. Glutathione can spontaneously react with ROS or reactive lipid carbonyls like 4-hydroxynonenal. Glutathione differs from most other antioxidants because its reactions with ROS convert its reduced form (GSH) to its disulfide-oxidized dimer (GS-SG).

Nonenzymatic antioxidants are converted to radicals themselves. Antioxidant radicals are relatively stable. Antioxidant radicals are less likely than ROS radicals to abstract a hydrogen from PUFA, DNA, or proteins. Dietary antioxidants often work together. Ascorbic acid (AscH) is polar and water-soluble.

## Oxidative Damage Repair/Degradation Enzymes

Oxidative damage repair systems limit the accumulation of oxidative damage in tissues. The autophagy pathway is one of the repair systems. The 20S proteasome is another repair system. The 20S proteasome recognizes and degrades oxidized proteins. The 20S proteasome degrades oxidized proteins in a manner similar to misfolded proteins. DNA repair enzymes are part of the repair systems. Base excision repair is one of the DNA repair mechanisms.

# POTENTIAL INTERVENTIONS TO REDUCE OXIDATIVE DAMAGE & DISEASES ASSOCIATED WITH ROS

Effective interventions to limit oxidative damage remain limited, despite substantial efforts. Effective interventions to reverse oxidative damage remain limited, despite substantial efforts.

Developing interventions to reduce oxidative damage is challenging. Maintaining physiologic functions that arise from ROS-producing systems is necessary.

Strategies to mitigate risk include minimizing exposure to harmful environmental ROS and ROS generators such as cigarette and vaping smoke. Strategies to mitigate risk include minimizing exposure to harmful environmental ROS and ROS generators such as air and water pollution. Strategies to mitigate risk include minimizing exposure to harmful environmental ROS and ROS generators such as excess sun exposure.

Regular physical activity is a strategy to reduce oxidative damage. Exercise training is a strategy to reduce oxidative damage.

Acute bouts of intensive exercise can temporarily increase ROS generation. Individuals who routinely engage in strenuous physical activity generally have lower rates of oxidative damage than sedentary individuals.

Physiologic adaptations from regular exercise include an increased number of mitochondria. Physiologic adaptations from regular exercise include more efficient coupling of these mitochondria. Physiologic adaptations from regular exercise include higher expression of various enzymes in the antioxidant network.

Maintaining healthy levels of body fat is important because obesity correlates with increased ROS formation. Obesity correlates with lipid peroxidation. Obesity correlates with oxidative damage.

Increased oxidative damage in obesity is due to increased inflammation and immune cell activation. Increased oxidative damage in obesity is due to a reduced number and quality of mitochondria. Increased oxidative damage in obesity is due to poorer mitochondrial coupling efficiency.

Supplementation with dietary antioxidants has mixed outcomes in clinical trials.

Diets rich in fruits and vegetables are associated with better health outcomes. Diets rich in fruits and vegetables are associated with a lower burden of oxidative damage in some cases.

Low vitamin A levels increase the risk for lung cancer. Low vitamin A levels increase the risk for other cancers. Epidemiologic evidence supports the link between low vitamin A levels and increased cancer risk.

Epidemiologic evidence suggests that individuals with low vitamin E levels are at higher risk for atherosclerosis. Epidemiologic evidence suggests that individuals with low vitamin E levels are at higher risk for cardiovascular disease. Meta-analysis of vitamin E intervention trials showed increased mortality among those taking high dose supplements.

Correcting dietary insufficiencies of vitamin antioxidants is important. Consuming supraphysiologic doses using dietary supplements seems of little benefit. Consuming supraphysiologic doses using dietary supplements may be harmful.

Supraphysiologic doses of dietary antioxidants may block essential cell signaling. Supraphysiologic doses of dietary antioxidants may weaken defenses against cancer cells and pathogens.

Excess consumption of antioxidants may not significantly increase systemic levels. For ascorbate, once plasma concentration reaches about 30 mmol/L, additional ascorbate is excreted in the urine.

Supraphysiologic doses of antioxidants may have a paradoxical pro-oxidant effect.

Omega-3 PUFAs have received significant attention in recent years. Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) is an Omega-3 PUFA. Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) is an Omega-3 PUFA.

Omega-3 PUFAs are named for the last double bond in the carbon chain being three carbons from the tail end of the fatty acid.

Omega-6 PUFAs have their last double bond six carbons from the tail end. Linoleic acid is an Omega-6 PUFA. Arachidonic acid is an Omega-6 PUFA.

Until about 100 years ago, most people consumed roughly equal levels of omega-3 PUFAs. Until about 100 years ago, most people consumed roughly equal levels of omega-6 PUFAs.

Commercial poultry and cattle feedlots shifted to corn-based diets. Corn mostly contains linoleic acid. The omega-3 to omega-6 ratio in commercial poultry and cattle feedlots skewed toward omega-6 PUFAs.

Clinical trials with DHA supplementation have shown significant benefits related to cardiovascular disease. Clinical trials with EPA supplementation have shown significant benefits related to cardiovascular disease.

Cyclooxygenases convert arachidonic acid to proinflammatory signaling lipids such as prostaglandins. Lipoxygenases convert arachidonic acid to proinflammatory signaling lipids such as leukotrienes.

Cyclooxygenases and lipoxygenases convert DHA to far less inflammatory signaling lipids. Cyclooxygenases and lipoxygenases convert EPA to far less inflammatory signaling lipids. The conversion of DHA and EPA potentially resolves inflammation.

Saturated fatty acids like palmitate are strong inducers of ROS generation via NOXs. Saturated fatty acids like palmitate are strong inducers of ROS generation via the electron transfer chain.

Replacing saturated fatty acids with omega-3 PUFAs in one's diet may reduce total oxidative damage. Omega-3 PUFAs are liable to undergo lipid peroxidation.

Major ROS include superoxide radical, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical, peroxynitrite, and hypochlorous acid.
Physiologic processes that form ROS include the electron transport chain, other energy producing pathways, and innate immune cells enzymes including NADPH oxidases, inducible nitric oxide synthetase, and MPOs.
Environmental sources that increase ROS include ionizing radiation, ultraviolet light, cigarette smoke pollution, and saturated lipids in diet.
ROS damage lipids, proteins, and DNA, thereby altering their function. Diseases where oxidative damage plays important roles include cardiovascular diseases, autoimmune diseases, cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and biological aging.
Antioxidant defenses against ROS and oxidative damage include enzymes such as SOD, catalase, and GPX, nonenzymatic antioxidants derived from the diet such as vitamins A, C, and E, and repair enzymes.
Effective strategies to limit excess ROS formation and oxidative damage include minimizing exposure to environmental sources of ROS, eating diets rich in fruits and vegetables, maintaining physical fitness and activity, and managing body weight.
To date, clinical trials utilizing dietary supplementation to treat ROS-associated diseases show no benefit and often potential harm.
