# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

We are exposed to a wide variety of compounds that are foreign to the body, known as xenobiotics, from the Greek xenos = foreign.

Xenobiotics include naturally occurring compounds in plant foods and synthetic compounds in medicines, food additives, and environmental pollutants.

Knowledge of the metabolism of xenobiotics is essential for understanding pharmacology, therapeutics, and toxicology.

Some xenobiotics in plant foods have potentially beneficial effects, such as acting as antioxidants or as prebiotics for gut bacteria.

# WE ENCOUNTER MANY XENOBIOTICS THAT MUST BE METABOLIZED BEFORE BEING EXCRETED

The main xenobiotics of medical relevance are drugs, chemical carcinogens, naturally occurring compounds in plant foods, and industrial compounds such as polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), insecticides, and pesticides. Most of these compounds are metabolized in the liver. The metabolism of xenobiotics is generally considered a detoxification process. Sometimes, the metabolism of inert or harmless compounds renders them biologically active. The metabolism of inert or harmless compounds can be desirable, as in the activation of a prodrug to an active compound. The metabolism of inert or harmless compounds can be undesirable, as in the formation of a carcinogen or mutagen from an inert precursor.

The metabolism of xenobiotics is often framed as occurring in two phases. Phase 1, also known as the oxidation/reduction phase, involves hydroxylation, deamination, dehalogenation, desulfuration, epoxidation, peroxidation, and reduction reactions. Phase 1 metabolism often introduces groups that can be conjugated in phase 2. Phase 2, the conjugation/hydrolysis phase, produces polar compounds that are water soluble and can be excreted in urine or bile. Not all compounds undergo both phases. Compounds do not always undergo the phases in the specified order.

In some cases, phase 1 metabolic reactions convert xenobiotics from inactive to biologically active compounds. The original xenobiotic may be referred to as a prodrug or procarcinogen. An example of phase 1 metabolism activating a xenobiotic is the conversion of vitamin D to calcitriol. Sometimes, subsequent phase 1 reactions convert these active compounds into less active or inactive forms before conjugation. In other cases, phase 2 conjugation reactions convert the products activated by phase 1 reactions to inactive compounds for excretion.

Enzymes that catalyze the metabolism of xenobiotics are present in various tissues. The concentrations of these enzymes are highest in the liver and intestinal tract. The concentrations of these enzymes being highest in the liver and intestinal tract leads to first-pass metabolism for many xenobiotics absorbed from the intestinal tract. All absorbed xenobiotics must pass through the liver via the portal vein to reach systemic circulation. Rapid metabolism by liver enzymes can significantly reduce the amount of xenobiotic that reaches systemic circulation. Rapid metabolism by intestinal enzymes can significantly reduce the amount of xenobiotic that reaches systemic circulation. The high concentration of xenobiotic metabolizing enzymes in the liver and intestine is likely an evolutionary adaptation to detoxify dietary xenobiotics.

# ISOFORMS OF CYTOCHROME P450 ACT ON A WIDE VARIETY OF XENOBIOTICS

Cytochrome P450s are heme-containing monooxygenase enzymes that catalyze many phase 1 reactions. Monoamine oxidases, aldehyde reductases, and alcohol dehydrogenases are additional important phase 1 enzymes. The name cytochrome P450 comes from a characteristic absorption peak at 450 nm observed in reduced microsomes exposed to carbon monoxide. There are at least 57 genes encoding cytochrome P450s in the human genome. A systematic nomenclature for cytochrome P450s is based on amino acid sequence homology. The root CYP denotes a cytochrome P450. Cytochromes P450 are included in the same family if they exhibit 40% or more amino acid sequence identity. P450s are in the same subfamily if they exhibit more than 55% sequence identity. Individual P450s are assigned numbers within their subfamily. CYP1A1 denotes a cytochrome P450 that is a member of family 1 and subfamily A and is the first individual member of that subfamily. The nomenclature for the genes encoding cytochromes P450 is the same as for the proteins, except that italics are used. The gene encoding CYP1A1 is CYP1A1. Cytochrome P450s have overlapping substrate specificities, allowing them to metabolize a broad range of xenobiotics. Many compounds are metabolized by two or more cytochrome P450s. Seven cytochrome P450s—CYP3A4, CYP3A5, CYP2D6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP1A2, and CYP2C19—are responsible for metabolizing over half of the drugs in the current medicinal pharmacopeia. Cytochrome P450s play crucial roles in xenobiotic metabolism and in the biosynthesis and metabolism of endogenous compounds, such as steroid hormones. In the liver and most other tissues, cytochrome P450s are mainly present in the membranes of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum. In hepatic microsomes, cytochromes P450 can comprise as much as 20% of the total protein. In the adrenal gland, cytochrome P450s are involved in cholesterol and steroid hormone biosynthesis and are found in mitochondria as well as in the endoplasmic reticulum. A representative reaction catalyzed by cytochrome P450 is RH + O2 + NADPH + H+ → R-OH + H2O + NADP+. NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase catalyzes the transfer of electrons from NADPH to cytochrome P450. Reduced cytochrome P450 catalyzes the reductive activation of molecular oxygen, with one atom becoming the hydroxyl group in the substrate and the other reduced to water. Cytochrome b5 may be involved as an electron donor in some cases.

## Cytochrome P450s often Mediate Drug-Drug and Food-Drug Interactions

Most isoforms of cytochrome P450 are inducible. Phenobarbital or other drugs cause hypertrophy of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum. Phenobarbital or other drugs cause a three- to fourfold increase in the amount of cytochrome P450 within a few days. Induction of cytochrome P450s often involves increased transcription to messenger RNA (mRNA). In some cases, induction involves stabilization of mRNA. In some cases, induction involves stabilization of the enzyme protein itself. In some cases, induction involves increased translation of existing mRNA.

Induction of cytochrome P450s is an important form of drug-drug interactions. Warfarin is metabolized by CYP2C9. CYP2C9 is induced by phenobarbital. Induction of CYP2C9 increases the metabolism of warfarin. Increased metabolism of warfarin reduces the efficacy of a given dose. The dose of warfarin must be increased to maintain efficacy. CYP2E1 catabolizes the metabolism of some widely used solvents. CYP2E1 catabolizes the metabolism of compounds found in tobacco smoke. Many compounds in tobacco smoke are procarcinogens. CYP2E1 is induced by ethanol. Ethanol consumption increases the risk of carcinogenicity.

Grapefruit contains a variety of furanocoumarins. Furanocoumarins in grapefruit inhibit cytochrome P450. Inhibition of cytochrome P450 affects the metabolism of many drugs. Some drugs are activated by cytochrome P450. Grapefruit reduces the activity of drugs activated by cytochrome P450. Other drugs are inactivated by cytochrome P450. Grapefruit increases the activity of drugs inactivated by cytochrome P450. Drugs affected by grapefruit include statins. Drugs affected by grapefruit include omeprazole. Drugs affected by grapefruit include antihistamines. Drugs affected by grapefruit include benzodiazepine antidepressants.

Polymorphism of cytochromes P450 explains much of the variation in drug responses by different patients. Variants with low catalytic activity lead to slower metabolism of the substrate. Slower metabolism of the substrate leads to prolonged drug action. CYP2A6 is involved in the metabolism of nicotine to cotinine. Three CYP2A6 alleles have been identified. The three CYP2A6 alleles include a wild type. The three CYP2A6 alleles include two inactive alleles. Individuals with the null alleles have impaired metabolism of nicotine. Individuals with the null alleles are protected against becoming tobacco-dependent smokers. Individuals with the null alleles smoke less. Individuals with the null alleles have elevated nicotine concentrations longer than those with the wild-type allele. Inhibiting CYP2A6 may provide a novel way to help smoking cessation.

# CONJUGATION REACTIONS IN PHASE 2 METABOLISM PREPARE XENOBIOTICS FOR EXCRETION

In phase 1 reactions, xenobiotics are converted to more polar, hydroxylated derivatives. In phase 2 reactions, derivatives are conjugated with molecules such as glucuronic acid, sulfate, or glutathione. Conjugation in phase 2 reactions renders derivatives even more water soluble. Increased water solubility facilitates eventual excretion in the urine or bile.

Xenobiotics are glucuronidated in the same way as bilirubin, using UDP-glucuronic acid. Glucuronosyltransferases are present in the endoplasmic reticulum. Glucuronosyltransferases are present in the cytosol. Molecules such as 2-acetylaminofluorene, aniline, benzoic acid, meprobamate, phenol, and many steroid hormones are excreted as glucuronides. The glucuronide may be attached to oxygen groups of the substrates. The glucuronide may be attached to nitrogen groups of the substrates. The glucuronide may be attached to sulfur groups of the substrates.

Glutathione S-transferases catalyze conjugation with the tripeptide glutathione. Glutathione S-transferases especially target electrophilic compounds. Glutathione S-conjugates are formed by glutathione S-transferases. Glutathione S-conjugates are excreted in urine and bile. There are four classes of cytosolic glutathione S-transferase. There are two classes of membrane-bound microsomal enzymes. A structurally distinct κ class is found in mitochondria and peroxisomes. Glutathione S-transferases exist as homo- or heterodimers. Glutathione S-transferases are constructed from at least seven different types of subunit. Different subunits are induced by different xenobiotics. The liver exhibits a very high level of glutathione Stransferase activity. In vitro, the entire pool of glutathione can be depleted within minutes on exposure to some xenobiotic substrates. The activity of glutathione S-transferase is upregulated in many tumors. The upregulation of glutathione S-transferase activity leads to resistance to chemotherapy.

Glutathione conjugates may be transported out of the liver. Glutathione conjugates are substrates for extracellular γ-glutamyltranspeptidase and some dipeptidases. Some hepatic glutathione S-conjugates enter the bile canaliculi. Glutathione S-conjugates in the bile canaliculi are broken down to cysteine S-conjugates. Cysteine S-conjugates are taken up into the liver for N-acetylation. Cysteine S-conjugates are reexcreted in the bile after N-acetylation.

# OTHER REACTIONS ARE ALSO INVOLVED IN PHASE 2 METABOLISM

Acetylation and methylation are the two most important reactions other than conjugation in phase 2 metabolism.

Acetylation involves the reaction X + Acetyl-CoA → Acetyl-X + CoA, where X represents a xenobiotic or its metabolite.

Acetyl-CoA acts as the acetyl donor in acetylation reactions.

Acetylation reactions are catalyzed by acetyltransferases present in the cytosol of various tissues, particularly the liver.

Isoniazid, a drug used in the treatment of tuberculosis, undergoes acetylation.

Polymorphism of acetyltransferases results in individuals being classified as slow or fast acetylators.

Slow acetylators are more susceptible to the toxic effects of isoniazid because the drug persists longer in their system.

Methylation involves reactions where xenobiotics are methylated by methyltransferases.

S-adenosylmethionine serves as the methyl donor in methylation reactions.

# RESPONSES TO XENOBIOTICS INCLUDE TOXIC, IMMUNOLOGICAL, & CARCINOGENIC EFFECTS

There are very few xenobiotics, including drugs, that do not have some toxic effects if the dose is large enough.

Xenobiotics can exert toxic effects by multiple mechanisms.

Xenobiotics can be consolidated into three general headings.

The first two of these headings are quite similar to the effects of reactive lipids discussed in Chapter 45.

## Altered Function

Xenobiotics can bind to cellular macromolecules including nucleic acids and proteins by covalent modifications, disrupting normal function.

Disruption of normal function often occurs after biotransformation of the xenobiotic by cytochrome P450s or other phase 1 enzymes to a chemically reactive species.

Cytochrome P450s oxidize benzo[α]pyrene, creating reactive intermediates that modify DNA.

Cytochrome P450s oxidize benzo[α]pyrene, creating reactive intermediates that modify DNA. These modifications lead to indirect carcinogenesis.

DNA repair enzymes attempt to repair the modified DNA, resulting in DNA sequences that differ from the original sequence before modification.

If gene mutations occur in tumor repressor genes, it can promote tumorogenesis.

## Initiation of Immune Responses

Covalent modification of proteins normally recognized as “self” proteins can create a neo-antigen perceived by immune cells as a “foreign” protein. Inflammation or other signals can cause immune cells to respond to the neo-antigen as a potential threat. B and T cells that recognize this neo-antigen will proliferate and become activated. The production of antibodies that recognize the metabolite-modified versions of the protein leads to the production of antibodies that recognize the metabolite-modified versions of the protein. Antibodies may also cross-react with unmodified versions of the protein. Cross-reaction with unmodified proteins can potentially initiate autoimmune disease.

Xenobiotics can bind as ligands to receptors or enzymes, antagonizing or hyperactivating cell signaling pathways. Endocrine disruptors such as bisphenol A are xenoestrogens that activate estrogen signaling. Certain PCBs bind the thyroid hormone receptor. Many different aromatic xenobiotics bind the aryl hydrocarbon receptor. Aryl hydrocarbon receptor transcription factors initiate gene expression that may be wholly inappropriate and lead to disease and dysfunction.

Xenobiotics are chemical compounds foreign to the body, including drugs, food additives, and environmental pollutants, as well as naturally occurring compounds in plant foods.
Xenobiotics are metabolized in two phases. Phase 1 reactions are oxidation or reduction reactions, with many oxidation reactions catalyzed by monooxygenases known as the cytochromes P450s. Phase 2 are conjugation/hydrolysis reactions, with conjugation to glucuronic acid, sulfate, or glutathione being important examples. The combined operation of these two phases converts lipophilic compounds into water-solubl compounds that can be excreted in urine or bile
Typically, cytochromes P450 catalyze reactions that introduce one atom of oxygen derived from molecular oxygen into the substrate, yielding a hydroxylated product, and the other into water. NADPH and NADPH cytochrome P450 reductase are involved in the reaction mechanism.
Cytochromes P450 are hemoproteins and generally have a wide substrate specificity, acting on many exogenous and endogenous substrates. At least 57 cytochrome P450 genes are found in the human genome
Cytochromes P450 are generally located in the endoplasmic reticulum of cells, especially in the liver.
Many cytochromes P450 are inducible. This has important implications for interactions between drugs.
Phase 2 conjugation reactions are catalyzed by enzymes such as glucuronyltransferases, sulfotransferases, and glutathione S-transferases.
