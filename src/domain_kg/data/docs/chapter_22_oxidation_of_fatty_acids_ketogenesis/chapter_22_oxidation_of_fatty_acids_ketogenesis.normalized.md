# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Fatty acids are broken down in mitochondria by oxidation to acetyl-CoA in a process that generates large amounts of energy. Acetoacetate, D-3-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone are collectively known as the ketone bodies. The liver produces ketone bodies when fatty acid oxidation is proceeding at a high rate. Acetoacetate and D-3-hydroxybutyrate are used as fuels by extrahepatic tissues in normal metabolism. Overproduction of ketone bodies causes ketosis. Increased fatty acid oxidation leads to ketosis. Starvation is a condition that increases fatty acid oxidation. Diabetes mellitus is a condition that increases fatty acid oxidation. Ketone bodies are acidic. Excess production of ketone bodies over long periods causes ketoacidosis. Ketoacidosis is potentially life-threatening. Ketoacidosis occurs in diabetes. Gluconeogenesis is dependent on fatty acid oxidation. Impairment in fatty acid oxidation leads to hypoglycemia. Carnitine deficiency is a state that impairs fatty acid oxidation. Deficiency of essential enzymes in fatty acid oxidation leads to hypoglycemia. Carnitine palmitoyltransferase deficiency is an example of an enzyme deficiency that impairs fatty acid oxidation. Hypoglycin is a poison that inhibits fatty acid oxidation. Inhibition of fatty acid oxidation by hypoglycin leads to hypoglycemia.

# OXIDATION OF FATTY ACIDS OCCURS IN MITOCHONDRIA

Acetyl-CoA is an end point of fatty acid catabolism. Acetyl-CoA is the starting substrate for fatty acid synthesis. Fatty acid breakdown is not the reverse of the biosynthetic pathway. Fatty acid breakdown is an entirely separate process. Fatty acid oxidation occurs in mitochondria. Fatty acid biosynthesis occurs in the cytosol. Each process is individually controlled. Each process is integrated with tissue requirements.

Each step in fatty acid oxidation involves acyl-CoA derivatives. Each step in fatty acid oxidation is catalyzed by separate enzymes. Each step utilizes FAD as a coenzyme. Each step generates ATP. Fatty acid oxidation is an aerobic process. Fatty acid oxidation requires the presence of oxygen.

## Fatty Acids Are Transported in the Blood as Free Fatty Acids

Free fatty acids (FFAs) are in the unesterified state.

Free fatty acids are also called unesterified (UFA) or nonesterified (NEFA) fatty acids.

In plasma, longer-chain FFA are combined with albumin.

In the cell, longer-chain FFA are attached to a fatty acid-binding protein.

Shorter-chain fatty acids are more water soluble.

Shorter-chain fatty acids exist as the unionized acid or as a fatty acid anion.

## Fatty Acids Are Activated Before Being Catabolized

Fatty acids must be converted to an active intermediate before they can be catabolized. The conversion of fatty acids to an active intermediate is the only step in the complete degradation of a fatty acid that requires energy from ATP. In the presence of ATP and coenzyme A, the enzyme acyl-CoA synthetase (thiokinase) catalyzes the conversion of a free fatty acid to an 'active FFA' or acyl-CoA. Acyl-CoA synthetases are found on the outer membrane of mitochondria and also in the endoplasmic reticulum and peroxisomes. Long-chain acyl-CoA freely enters the intermembrane space. For transport across the inner mitochondrial membrane, acyl groups are transferred from CoA to carnitine by carnitine palmitoyltransferase-I. The acylcarnitine formed is carried into the matrix by a translocase enzyme in exchange for a free carnitine. Acyl-CoA is reformed by carnitine palmitoyltransferase-II in the matrix (Figure 22–1).

## Long-Chain Fatty Acids Cross the Inner Mitochondrial Membrane as Carnitine Derivatives

Carnitine is a compound that is widely distributed in the body and particularly abundant in muscle.

Acyl-CoAs are able to enter the intermembrane space but require carnitine to cross the inner mitochondrial membrane into the matrix where fatty acid breakdown takes place.

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase-I is an enzyme located in the outer mitochondrial membrane.

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase-I transfers the long-chain acyl group from CoA to carnitine, forming acylcarnitine and releasing CoA.

Carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase is an enzyme found in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase binds acylcarnitine and transports it across the membrane in exchange for carnitine.

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase-II is located on the inside of the inner membrane.

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase-II catalyzes the transfer of the acyl group to CoA, reforming acyl-CoA and releasing carnitine.

# β-OXIDATION OF FATTY ACIDS INVOLVES SUCCESSIVE CLEAVAGE WITH RELEASE OF ACETYL-COA

In the pathway for the oxidation of fatty acids, two carbons at a time are cleaved from acyl-CoA molecules, starting at the carboxyl end. The two-carbon units formed are acetyl-CoA. Palmitoyl(C16)-CoA forms eight acetyl-CoA molecules.

## Peroxisomes Oxidize Very-Long-Chain Fatty Acids

Peroxisomes perform a modified form of β-oxidation. Peroxisomes break down very-long-chain fatty acids, such as C20 and C22. The breakdown results in the formation of acetyl-CoA and H_{2}O_{2}. Peroxisomes do not form ATP during this process. Catalase breaks down H_{2}O_{2}.

High-fat diets induce the enzymes involved in peroxisomal oxidation. In some species, hypolipidemic drugs like clofibrate also induce these enzymes.

# KETOGENESIS OCCURS WHEN THERE IS A HIGH RATE OF FATTY ACID OXIDATION IN THE LIVER

Acetoacetate undergoes spontaneous decarboxylation to yield acetone. Acetoacetate, D-3-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone are collectively known as the ketone bodies. Ketone bodies are also called acetone bodies or [incorrectly] 'ketones'.

Acetoacetate and 3-hydroxybutyrate are interconverted by the mitochondrial enzyme D-3-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase.

## Acetoacetyl-CoA Is the Substrate for Ketogenesis

The enzymes responsible for ketone body formation (ketogenesis) are associated mainly with the mitochondria. Acetoacetyl-CoA is formed when two acetyl-CoA molecules produced via fatty acid breakdown condense to form acetoacetyl-CoA by a reversal of the thiolase reaction.

[FIGURE:fig-22-4]

Pathways of ketogenesis in the liver. (FFA, free fatty acids.)

Condensation of acetoacetyl-CoA with another molecule of acetyl-CoA by 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) synthase forms HMG-CoA. HMG-CoA lyase then causes acetyl-CoA to split off from the HMG-CoA, leaving free acetoacetate. Both enzymes must be present in mitochondria for ketogenesis to take place.

In mammals, ketone bodies are formed solely in the liver and in the rumen epithelium. 3-Hydroxybutyrate is formed from acetoacetate. 3-Hydroxybutyrate is quantitatively the predominant ketone body present in the blood and urine in ketosis.

## Ketone Bodies Serve as a Fuel for Extrahepatic Tissues

In the liver, an active enzymatic mechanism produces acetoacetate from acetoacetyl-CoA. Once acetoacetate is formed, it can only be reactivated by linking directly to CoA in the cytosol. The reactivation of acetoacetate is used in a less active pathway as a precursor in cholesterol synthesis. The net production of ketone bodies by the liver is accounted for by this process.

In extrahepatic tissues, acetoacetate is activated to acetoacetyl-CoA by succinyl-CoA-acetoacetate-CoA transferase. CoA is transferred from succinyl-CoA to form acetoacetyl-CoA. The addition of a CoA allows two acetyl-CoA molecules to be formed by the splitting of acetoacetyl-CoA by thiolase. Two acetyl-CoA molecules are oxidized in the citric acid cycle. 3-Hydroxybutyrate is utilized by converting to acetoacetate, which generates an NADH in the process. 1 mol of acetoacetate or 3-hydroxybutyrate yields 19 or 21.5 mol of ATP, respectively, by these pathways. At this stage, a large proportion of oxygen consumption may be accounted for by their oxidation.

In moderate ketonemia, the loss of ketone bodies via the urine is only a few percent of the total ketone body production and utilization. There are renal threshold-like effects that vary between species and individuals. Measurement of ketonemia, not ketonuria, is the preferred method of assessing the severity of ketosis. The term ketones should not be used as there are ketones in blood that are not ketone bodies, for example, pyruvate and fructose. (Figure 22-5)

# KETOGENESIS IS REGULATED AT THREE CRUCIAL STEPS

Ketosis does not occur in vivo unless there is an increase in the level of circulating free fatty acids (FFAs) arising from lipolysis of triacylglycerol in adipose tissue. Free fatty acids (FFAs) are the precursors of ketone bodies in the liver. The liver extracts approximately 30% of the free fatty acids (FFAs) passing through it. At high concentrations, the flux of free fatty acids (FFAs) into the liver is substantial. The factors regulating mobilization of free fatty acids (FFAs) from adipose tissue are important in controlling ketogenesis (Figures 22–6and 25–6).

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase-I (CPT-I) regulates the entry of fatty acids into the oxidative pathway (see Figure 22–1). CPT-I activity is low in the fed state, leading to depression of fatty acid oxidation, and high in starvation, allowing fatty acid oxidation to increase. Malonyl-CoA, the initial intermediate in fatty acid biosynthesis (see Figure 23–1), is a potent inhibitor of CPT-I (Figure 22–7). Only 26 mol of ATP are produced when acetoacetate is the end product. Only 16 mol of ATP are produced when 3-hydroxybutyrate is the end product. Ketogenesis may be regarded as a mechanism that allows the liver to oxidize increasing quantities of fatty acids. Ketogenesis operates within the constraints of a tightly coupled system of oxidative phosphorylation.

A fall in the concentration of oxaloacetate, particularly within the mitochondria, can impair the ability of the citric acid cycle to metabolize acetyl-CoA and divert fatty acid oxidation toward ketogenesis. A fall in the concentration of oxaloacetate also occurs when gluconeogenesis is elevated due to low blood glucose levels. The activation by acetyl-CoA of pyruvate carboxylase, which catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to oxaloacetate, partially alleviates the problem of decreased oxaloacetate concentration. In conditions such as starvation and untreated diabetes mellitus, ketone bodies are overproduced and cause ketosis.

## Impaired Oxidation of Fatty Acids Gives Rise to Diseases Often Associated With Hypoglycemia

Carnitine deficiency occurs particularly in the newborn or preterm infants due to inadequate biosynthesis or renal leakage. Carnitine deficiency can also occur due to losses in hemodialysis. Carnitine deficiency leads to hypoglycemia. Hypoglycemia results from impaired fatty acid oxidation. Carnitine deficiency leads to lipid accumulation with muscular weakness. Treatment for carnitine deficiency is by oral supplementation with carnitine.

Inherited CPT-I deficiency affects only the liver. Inherited CPT-I deficiency leads to reduced fatty acid oxidation and ketogenesis. Inherited CPT-I deficiency causes hypoglycemia. CPT-II deficiency primarily affects skeletal muscle. CPT-II deficiency affects the liver when severe. Sulfonylurea drugs, such as glyburide [glibenclamide] and tolbutamide, are used in treating type 2 diabetes mellitus. Sulfonylurea drugs reduce fatty acid oxidation and hyperglycemia by inhibiting CPT-I.

Defects have been identified in long-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase. Defects have been identified in short-chain 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase. Deficiency of the long-chain enzyme may cause acute fatty liver of pregnancy. 3-Ketoacyl-CoA thiolase deficiency affects the degradation of leucine. HMG-CoA lyase deficiency affects the degradation of leucine. Leucine is a ketogenic amino acid.

Jamaican vomiting sickness is caused by eating the unripe fruit of the akee tree. The unripe fruit of the akee tree contains the toxin hypoglycin. Hypoglycin inactivates medium- and short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase. Dicarboxylic aciduria is caused by a lack of mitochondrial medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase.

Refsum disease is a rare neurologic disorder that results in the accumulation of phytanic acid. Phytanic acid is found in dairy products and ruminant fat and meat. Phytanic acid affects membrane function. Phytanic acid affects protein prenylation. Phytanic acid affects gene expression. Zellweger syndrome occurs in individuals with a rare inherited absence of peroxisomes in all tissues. Zellweger syndrome exhibits a generalized loss of peroxisomal functions. Zellweger syndrome causes severe neurologic symptoms. Most patients with Zellweger syndrome die in the first year of life.

## Ketoacidosis Results From Prolonged Ketosis

Higher than normal quantities of ketone bodies in the blood constitute ketonemia. Higher than normal quantities of ketone bodies in the urine constitute ketonuria. The overall condition of having higher than normal ketone bodies is called ketosis. The basic form of ketosis occurs in starvation. Starvation ketosis involves depletion of available carbohydrate. Starvation ketosis involves mobilization of free fatty acids (FFA). An exaggeration of the general pattern of metabolism in starvation produces pathologic states in diabetes mellitus. Type 2 diabetes mellitus is increasingly common in Western countries. Twin lamb disease is a pathologic state related to exaggerated ketosis. Ketosis in lactating cattle is a pathologic state related to exaggerated ketosis. Nonpathologic forms of ketosis are found under conditions of high-fat feeding. Nonpathologic forms of ketosis occur after severe exercise in the postabsorptive state.

Acetoacetic acid is a moderately strong acid. 3-hydroxybutyric acid is a moderately strong acid. Acetoacetic and 3-hydroxybutyric acids are buffered when present in blood or other tissues. Acetoacetic and 3-hydroxybutyric acids' continual excretion in quantity progressively depletes the alkali reserve. Depletion of the alkali reserve causes ketoacidosis. Ketoacidosis may be fatal in uncontrolled diabetes mellitus.

The acetyl-CoA is oxidized in the citric acid cycle, generating further ATP.
The ketone bodies (acetoacetate, 3-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone) are formed in hepatic mitochondria when there is a high rate of fatty acid oxidation. The pathway of ketogenesis involves synthesis and breakdown of HMG-CoA by two key enzymes: HMG-CoA synthase and HMG-CoA lyase.
Ketone bodies are important fuels in extrahepatic tissues.
Ketogenesis is regulated at three crucial steps: (1) control of FFA mobilization from adipose tissue; (2) the activity of carnitine palmitoyltransferase-I in liver, which determines the proportion of the fatty acid flux that is oxidized rather than esterified; and (3) partition of acetyl-CoA between the pathway of ketogenesis and the citric acid cycle.
Diseases associated with impairment of fatty acid oxidation lead to hypoglycemia, fatty infiltration of organs, and hypoketonemia.
Ketosis is mild in starvation but severe in diabetes mellitus and ruminant ketosis.
