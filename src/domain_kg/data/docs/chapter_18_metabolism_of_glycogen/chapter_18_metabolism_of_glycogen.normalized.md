# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Glycogen is the major storage carbohydrate in animals. Glycogen is a branched polymer of α-D-glucose. Glycogen occurs mainly in liver and muscle, with modest amounts in the brain. The concentration of glycogen in the liver is greater than that in muscle. The total muscle mass of the body is considerably greater than that of the liver. About three-quarters of total body glycogen is in muscle.

Liver glycogen functions as a reserve to maintain the blood glucose concentration in the fasting state. The liver makes about 9 grams of glucose per hour after an overnight fast, of which 6 g/h is used by glucose-requiring tissues such as the brain and red blood cells. The total glycogen mass in the liver is approximately 90 g. If liver glycogen was the only source of glucose, it would last for about 10 hours. However, gluconeogenesis provides another source of glucose in the liver, so glycogen stores do not deplete as rapidly. After fasting for about 6 to 8 hours, liver glycogen stores decrease to about 20 g. Liver glycogen stores decrease to about 20 g depends on when the last meal was eaten, the rate of intestinal absorption of carbohydrates, and the composition of the last meal. If breakfast and lunch are skipped, by dinner time, liver glycogen is almost totally depleted.

Muscle glycogen provides a readily available source of glucose-1-phosphate for glycolysis within the muscle itself. Muscle glycogen breakdown does not directly yield free glucose because muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase. During muscle glycogenolysis, pyruvate formed by glycolysis in muscle can be transaminated to alanine or reduced to lactate. Muscle protein stores can be broken down to release glucogenic amino acids, which can be exported from muscle and used for gluconeogenesis in the liver. After a meal, about 50% of liver glycogen accretion is from diversion of gluconeogenic carbon into glycogen, known as indirect glycogen synthesis.

Glycogen storage diseases are a rare group of inherited disorders, affecting fewer than 1 in 20,000 people. Glycogen storage diseases are characterized by deficient mobilization of glycogen or deposition of abnormal forms of glycogen, leading to liver damage and muscle weakness. Some glycogen storage diseases result in early death. Glycogen storage diseases are inherited in an autosomal recessive fashion, with the exception of type IX and Danon disease, which are X-linked recessive.

Type 0 glycogen storage disease involves a deficiency in glycogen synthase, affecting the genes GYS2 and GYS1. Type 0 glycogen storage disease is characterized by hypoglycemia, hyperketonemia, and early death. Type I glycogen storage disease, known as von Gierke disease, involves a deficiency in glucose-6-phosphatase or the endoplasmic reticulum glucose-6-phosphate transporter, affecting the genes G6PC and SLC37A4. Type I glycogen storage disease is characterized by glycogen accumulation in liver and renal tubule cells, hypoglycemia, lactic acidemia, ketosis, and hyperlipemia. Type II glycogen storage disease, known as Pompe disease, involves a deficiency in lysosomal α1→4 and α1→6 glucosidase (acid maltase), affecting the gene GAA. Type II glycogen storage disease is characterized by the accumulation of glycogen in lysosomes. The juvenile-onset variant leads to muscle hypotonia and death from heart failure by age 2, while the adult-onset variant leads to muscle dystrophy.

Type III glycogen storage disease, known as Cori/Forbes disease, involves a deficiency in the liver and muscle debranching enzyme, affecting the gene AGL. Type III glycogen storage disease is characterized by fasting hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly in infancy, accumulation of characteristic branched polysaccharide (limit dextrin), and variable muscle weakness. Type IV glycogen storage disease, known as Amylopectinosis or Andersen disease, involves a deficiency in the glycogen branching enzyme, affecting the gene GBE1. Type IV glycogen storage disease is characterized by hepatosplenomegaly, accumulation of polysaccharide with few branch points, and death from heart or liver failure before age 5. Type V glycogen storage disease, known as McArdle disease, involves a deficiency in muscle phosphorylase, affecting the gene PYGM. Type V glycogen storage disease is characterized by poor exercise tolerance and abnormally high muscle glycogen (2.5-4%), with very low blood lactate after exercise. Type VI glycogen storage disease, known as Hers disease, involves a deficiency in liver phosphorylase, affecting the gene PYGL. Type VI glycogen storage disease is characterized by hepatomegaly, accumulation of glycogen in the liver, mild hypoglycemia, and generally good prognosis. Type VII glycogen storage disease, known as Tarui disease, involves a deficiency in muscle and erythrocyte phosphofructokinase 1, affecting the gene PFKM. Type VII glycogen storage disease is characterized by poor exercise tolerance, abnormally high muscle glycogen (2.5-4%), very low blood lactate after exercise, and hemolytic anemia.

Type IX glycogen storage disease involves a deficiency in liver and muscle phosphorylase kinase, affecting the genes PHKA2, PHKB, PHKG2, and PHKA1. Type IX glycogen storage disease is characterized by hepatomegaly, accumulation of glycogen in the liver and muscle, mild hypoglycemia, and generally good prognosis. Type X glycogen storage disease involves a deficiency in muscle phosphoglycerate mutase, affecting the gene PGAM2. Type X glycogen storage disease is characterized by hepatomegaly and accumulation of glycogen in the liver. Type XI glycogen storage disease, known as Fanconi-Bickel disease, involves a deficiency in the glucose transporter 2, affecting the gene SLC2A2. Type XI glycogen storage disease is characterized by liver and kidney effects, weak bones, small stature, renal dysfunction, and generally good prognosis. Type XII glycogen storage disease involves a deficiency in Aldolase A, affecting the gene ALDOA. Type XII glycogen storage disease is characterized by myopathy, exercise intolerance, and hemolytic anemia. Type XIII glycogen storage disease involves a deficiency in Beta-enolase, affecting the gene ENO3. Type XIII glycogen storage disease is characterized by exercise intolerance and myalgia. Type XV glycogen storage disease involves a deficiency in Glycogenin-1, affecting the gene GYG1. Type XV glycogen storage disease is characterized by cardiomegaly.

The highly branched structure of glycogen provides a large number of sites for glycogenolysis and synthesis. The highly branched structure increases the surface area to rapidly release glucose-1-phosphate for muscle glycolytic activity or rapid mobilization of glucose from the liver when glucose demand is high, such as during exercise. Endurance athletes adapt muscle metabolism to maintain a slower, more sustained release of glucose-1-phosphate in muscle, to preserve muscle glycogen stores and exercise endurance. Consuming a diet that supplies ample carbohydrates and energy to meet or exceed daily expenditure gradually augments muscle glycogen stores over days and weeks. Some endurance athletes practice carbohydrate loading. Endurance athletes lower muscle glycogen stores either by doing a bout of exercise to exhaustion or by going on a low carbohydrate diet for 3 days. After that, they ingest a high carbohydrate meal to rapidly increase muscle glycogen stores. A modified form is to taper exercise training for 3 days while on a moderate carbohydrate diet, then switch to a very high carbohydrate diet for 2 days. This will augment muscle glycogen synthesis, with fewer branch points than normal and an increase in total muscle stores. Carbohydrate ingestion during exercise prolongs endurance because it helps maintain liver glycogen stores and may spare muscle glycogen, especially in fast twitch muscle cells. For each gram of glycogen, there is 3 g of water. Thus, the early rapid weight loss seen when dieting or switching to a low-carbohydrate diet is partly due to water loss and not as much fat loss.

Small glycogen stores are present in the brain, primarily in astrocytes. Small glycogen stores may help explain some of the neurologic symptoms seen in some glycogen storage diseases.

## Glycogen Biosynthesis Involves UDP-Glucose

Glucose is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate by hexokinase in muscle. Glucose is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate by glucokinase in the liver. Glucose-6-phosphate is isomerized to glucose-1-phosphate by phosphoglucomutase. Phosphoglucomutase itself is phosphorylated. The phosphate group participates in a reversible reaction where glucose 1,6-bisphosphate is an intermediate. Glucose-1-phosphate reacts with uridine triphosphate (UTP) to form uridine diphosphate glucose (UDPGlc) and pyrophosphate. UDPGlc pyrophosphorylase catalyzes the reaction of glucose-1-phosphate with uridine triphosphate (UTP). Pyrophosphatase catalyzes the hydrolysis of pyrophosphate to 2× phosphate. The hydrolysis of pyrophosphate removes one of the reaction products and favors the formation of UDPGlc. UDPGlc pyrophosphorylase is present in relatively large amounts. UDPGlc pyrophosphorylase is not a regulatory step in glycogen synthesis.

The initial steps in glycogen synthesis involve the protein glycogenin, a 37-kDa protein that is glucosylated on a specific tyrosine residue by UDPGlc. Glycogenin catalyzes the transfer of a further seven glucose residues from UDPGlc, in 1 → 4 linkage, to form a glycogen primer that is the substrate for glycogen synthase. The glycogenin remains at the core of the glycogen granule. The self-glycosylation of glycogen and the resulting oligosaccharide primer are required for glycogen synthesis to occur. A genetic defect in this gene (GSD-IV) primarily impairs heart function. Glycogen synthase catalyzes the formation of a glycoside bond between C-1 of the glucose of UDPGlc and C-4 of a terminal glucose residue of glycogen, liberating UDP. The addition of a glucose residue to a preexisting glycogen chain, or “primer,” occurs at the nonreducing, outer end of the molecule, so that the branches of the glycogen molecule become elongated as successive 1 → 4 linkages are formed (Figure 18–3).

## Branching Involves Detachment of Existing Glycogen Chains

When a growing chain is at least 11 glucose residues long, branching enzyme transfers a part of the 1 → 4 chain (at least six glucose residues) to a neighboring chain to form a 1 → 6 linkage, establishing a branch point. The branches grow by further additions of 1 → 4-glucosyl units and further branching.

# GLYCOGENOLYSIS IS NOT THE REVERSE OF GLYCOGENESIS, IT IS A SEPARATE PATHWAY

Glycogen phosphorylase catalyzes the rate-limiting step in glycogenolysis, which involves the phosphorolytic cleavage of the 1 → 4 linkages of glycogen to yield glucose-1-phosphate (Figure 18--4). Different isoenzymes of glycogen phosphorylase exist in liver, muscle, and brain. Each isoenzyme of glycogen phosphorylase is encoded by different genes. Glycogen phosphorylase requires pyridoxal phosphate as its coenzyme. In the context of phosphorylase, the phosphate group of pyridoxal phosphate is catalytically active. In amino acid metabolism, the aldehyde group of pyridoxal phosphate is the reactive group (see Chapter 28).

The terminal glucosyl residues from the outermost chains of the glycogen molecule are removed sequentially until approximately four glucose residues remain on either side of a 1 → 6 branch (see Figure 18–4). The debranching enzyme has two catalytic sites in a single polypeptide chain. One site is a glucan transferase that transfers a trisaccharide unit from one branch to the other, exposing the 1 → 6 branch point. The other site is a 1,6-glycosidase that catalyzes hydrolysis of the 1 → 6-glycoside bond to liberate free glucose. Further phosphorylase action can then proceed. The combined action of phosphorylase and these other enzymes leads to the complete breakdown of glycogen.

The reaction catalyzed by phosphoglucomutase is reversible. Phosphoglucomutase allows glucose-1-phosphate derived from hydrolysis of glycogen to form glucose-6-phosphate. In liver, glucose-6-phosphatase catalyzes hydrolysis of glucose-6-phosphate, yielding glucose that is exported. The export of glucose leads to an increase in the blood glucose concentration. Glucose-6-phosphatase is located in the lumen of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum. Genetic defects of the glucose-6-phosphate transporter can cause a variant of type I glycogen storage disease (online Table 18–2).

Glycogen granules can be engulfed by lysosomes. Acid maltase catalyzes the hydrolysis of glycogen to glucose in lysosomes. The hydrolysis of glycogen to glucose by acid maltase may be especially important in glucose homeostasis in neonates. Genetic lack of lysosomal acid maltase causes type II glycogen storage disease (Pompe disease; see online Table 18–2). The lysosomal catabolism of glycogen is under hormonal control.

# CYCLIC AMP INTEGRATES THE REGULATION OF GLYCOGENOLYSIS & GLYCOGENESIS

Glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase are the principal enzymes controlling glycogen metabolism. Glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase are regulated in opposite directions by allosteric mechanisms, subcellular localization, and covalent modification by reversible phosphorylation and dephosphorylation. Phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase increases its activity. Phosphorylation of glycogen synthase reduces its activity.

Phosphorylation is increased in response to cyclic AMP (cAMP). cAMP is formed from ATP by adenylyl cyclase at the inner surface of cell membranes. Hormones such as epinephrine, norepinephrine, and glucagon trigger the formation of cAMP. cAMP is hydrolyzed by phosphodiesterase, terminating hormone action. In the liver, insulin increases the activity of phosphodiesterase (Figure 18--5).

## Glycogen Phosphorylase Regulation Is Different in Liver & Muscle

In the liver, glycogen provides free glucose for export to maintain blood glucose concentration. In muscle, glycogen provides glucose-6-phosphate for glycolysis in response to ATP needs for muscle contraction. In both liver and muscle, glycogen phosphorylase is activated by phosphorylation catalyzed by phosphorylase kinase, producing phosphorylase a. In both liver and muscle, glycogen phosphorylase is inactivated by dephosphorylation catalyzed by phosphoprotein phosphatase, producing phosphorylase b. Active phosphorylase a in both tissues is allosterically inhibited by ATP and glucose-6 phosphate. In the liver, free glucose also inhibits active phosphorylase a. In muscle, the phosphorylase enzyme differs from the liver isoenzyme by having a binding site for 5' AMP. 5' AMP acts as an allosteric activator of the dephosphorylated b-form of the enzyme in muscle. 5' AMP signals the energy state of the muscle cell. 5' AMP is formed as the concentration of ADP increases, indicating the need for increased substrate metabolism to permit ATP formation. The differing regulation of muscle and liver glycogen phosphorylase reflects the differing roles of glycogen in these tissues. Liver glycogen is released to control plasma glucose. Muscle glycogen is used by the contracting muscle and is sensitive to the energy state of the muscle. In exercise, liver glucose production increases twofold within a few minutes. If exogenous glucose is given, the liver must rapidly stop making glucose and become a glucose consumer to prevent hyperglycemia and maximize glycogen repletion. In muscle, glycogen use is sensitive to the energy state of the muscle, influenced by 5' AMP and calcium.

## Hypoglycemia & Diabetes

In individuals with diabetes, treatments are designed to bring fasting glucose and meal glucose concentration into the normal range to minimize the long-term complications of diabetes.

Hypoglycemia can occur despite the best intentions.

Many individuals with long-standing diabetes develop an unawareness of hypoglycemia.

Nonsevere hypoglycemia events usually generate autonomic and/or neuroglycopenic symptoms, which enable the individual to identify the onset, and to treat the falling blood glucose without requiring assistance as it is occurring.

If individuals are unaware, hypoglycemia can become severe enough to impair their ability to function, requiring external assistance.

Glucagon is one of the rescue medicines if the person is unconscious or unable to swallow and ingest a fast-acting carbohydrate.

Glucagon is rapid in onset and is a potent stimulator of glycogenolysis.

## Glycogen Storage Diseases Are Inherited

Glycogen storage disease is a generic term for a group of inherited disorders characterized by the deposition of an abnormal type or quantity of glycogen in tissues, or failure to mobilize glycogen.

Glycogen storage diseases are heterogenous and rare.

Glycogen storage diseases predominantly affect the liver, muscles, heart, and in rare instances, the brain from infancy to adulthood.

Clinical suspicions can lead to the diagnosis of glycogen storage diseases in primary care and in-patient settings.

Newborn genetic screening is key to detecting glycogen storage diseases.

Many of the medical crises associated with glycogen storage diseases are preventable with simple nutritional measures.

Nutritional measures can limit growth retardation and intellectual disability in individuals with glycogen storage diseases.

Glycogen represents the principal storage carbohydrate in the body, mainly in the liver and muscle.
In the liver, its major function is to provide glucose for extrahepatic tissues. In muscle, it serves mainly as a source of metabolic fuel for use in muscle. Muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase and cannot release free glucose from glycogen.
Glycogen is synthesized from glucose by the pathway of glycogenesis. It is broken down by a separate pathway, glycogenolysis.
Cyclic AMP integrates the regulation of glycogenolysis and glycogenesis by promoting the simultaneous activation of phosphorylase and inhibition of glycogen synthase. Insulin acts reciprocally by inhibiting glycogenolysis and stimulating glycogenesis.
Inherited deficiencies of enzymes of glycogen metabolism in both liver, muscle, and brain cause glycogen storage diseases.
