# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Water is the predominant chemical component of living organisms. Water's dipolar nature and exceptional capacity for forming hydrogen bonds give it unique physical properties. Water's ability to solvate a wide range of organic and inorganic molecules derives from its dipolar nature and capacity for hydrogen bonding. Water influences the structure of solvated biomolecules through its interactions with them. Water acts as an excellent nucleophile and participates as a reactant or product in many metabolic reactions.

Physiologic regulation of water balance depends on hypothalamic mechanisms that control thirst. Water balance regulation involves the retention or excretion of water by the kidneys. Water balance regulation also involves evaporative loss. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus results from the unresponsiveness of renal tubular osmoreceptors to antidiuretic hormone (ADH). Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus involves the inability to concentrate urine. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus involves the inability to adjust to subtle changes in extracellular fluid osmolarity.

Water has a slight propensity to dissociate into hydroxide ions and protons. The concentration of protons, or acidity, in aqueous solutions is reported using the logarithmic pH scale. Bicarbonate and other buffers maintain the pH of extracellular fluid between 7.35 and 7.45. Suspected disturbances of acid-base balance can be verified by measuring the pH of arterial blood. Acidosis occurs when blood pH is less than 7.35. Causes of acidosis include diabetic ketosis and lactic acidosis. Alkalosis occurs when pH is greater than 7.45. Alkalosis may follow vomiting of acidic gastric contents.

## Water Molecules Form Dipoles

A water molecule is an irregular, slightly skewed tetrahedron with oxygen at its center (Figure 2-1). The corners are occupied by the two hydrogens and the unshared electrons of the remaining two sp3-hybridized orbitals of oxygen. The angle between the two hydrogen atoms is 109.5°. The ideal tetrahedral angle is 105°. The strongly electronegative oxygen atom attracts electrons away from the hydrogen nuclei. The hydrogen nuclei have a partial positive charge. The two unshared electron pairs of the oxygen constitute a region of local negative charge. The asymmetric charge distribution is referred to as a dipole.

Water’s strong dipole is responsible for its high dielectric constant. Coulomb’s law describes the strength of interaction F between oppositely charged particles as inversely proportionate to the dielectric constant ε of the surrounding medium. At 25°C, the dielectric constant for a vacuum is essentially unity. At 25°C, the dielectric constant for hexane is 1.9. At 25°C, the dielectric constant for ethanol is 24.3. At 25°C, the dielectric constant for water is 78.5. Water’s high dipole moment diminishes the force of attraction between charged and polar species relative to most other solvents. Water can dissolve large quantities of polar molecules. Water can dissolve charged compounds such as salts.

## Water Molecules Form Hydrogen Bonds

A partially unshielded hydrogen nucleus covalently bound to an electron-withdrawing oxygen or nitrogen atom can interact with an unshared electron pair on another oxygen or nitrogen atom to form a hydrogen bond. Water molecules contain both electron-withdrawing oxygen or nitrogen atoms and unshared electron pairs, which favors their self-association or clustering (Figure 2-2).

[FIGURE:fig-2-2]

Water molecules self-associate via hydrogen bonds. Shown are the association of two water molecules (left) and a hydrogen-bonded cluster of four water molecules (right). Notice that water can serve simultaneously both as a hydrogen donor and as a hydrogen acceptor.

Each molecule in liquid water associates through hydrogen bonds with an average of 3.5 other molecules. Hydrogen bonds are relatively weak and transient, with a half-life of a few picoseconds. Rupturing a hydrogen bond in liquid water requires about 4.5 kcal/mol, which is less than 5% of the energy needed to rupture a covalent O—H bond.

The capacity of water molecules to form hydrogen bonds influences the physical properties of water, including its high viscosity, surface tension, and boiling point. Hydrogen bonding enables water to dissolve many organic biomolecules that contain functional groups capable of participating in hydrogen bonding. The oxygen atoms of aldehydes, ketones, and amides provide lone pairs of electrons that can serve as hydrogen acceptors. Alcohols, carboxylic acids, and amines can serve both as hydrogen acceptors and as donors of unshielded hydrogen atoms for the formation of hydrogen bonds (Figure 2-3).

## In Water, Biomolecules Fold to Position Hydrophobic Groups Within Their Interior

Most biomolecules are amphipathic, possessing regions rich in charged or polar functional groups as well as regions with hydrophobic character.

Proteins tend to fold with the R-groups of amino acids with hydrophobic side chains in the interior.

Amino acids with charged or polar amino acid side chains, such as arginine, glutamate, and serine, generally are present on the surface in contact with water.

In a phospholipid bilayer, the charged 'head groups' of phosphatidylserine or phosphatidylethanolamine contact water while their hydrophobic fatty acyl side chains cluster together, excluding water.

The folding pattern of biomolecules minimizes energetically unfavorable contacts between water and hydrophobic groups.

The pattern of minimizing energetically unfavorable contacts between water and hydrophobic groups maximizes the opportunities for the formation of energetically favorable charge-dipole, dipole-dipole, and hydrogen bonding interactions between polar groups on the biomolecule and water.

## Hydrophobic Interactions

Hydrophobic interactions refer to the tendency of nonpolar compounds to associate together in an aqueous environment. Nonpolar compounds do not associate due to mutual attraction between the hydrophobic solute molecules. The association is driven by the thermodynamics of the water surrounding them. Hydrogen atoms of nonpolar groups, such as methylene groups of hydrocarbons, do not form hydrogen bonds. Hydrogen atoms of nonpolar groups, such as methylene groups of hydrocarbons, affect the structure of water molecules they come in contact with. Water molecules adjacent to a hydrophobic group are restricted in the number of orientations that permit them to maximize hydrogen bonds. Maximal formation of multiple hydrogen bonds is enthalpically favored, which increases the order and decreases the entropy of the adjacent water molecules.

The optimal free energy of a hydrocarbon-water mixture is a function of maximal enthalpy from hydrogen bonding and highest entropy of the solvent. When nonpolar molecules associate together, the resulting droplets minimize their aggregate exposed surface area. The association of nonpolar molecules reduces the number of water molecules whose motional freedom becomes restricted. In living cells, the hydrophobic portions of amphipathic biopolymers tend to fold to bury nonpolar regions within the molecule or become inserted into a lipid bilayer. Both actions minimize their contact with water.

In Figure 2-4, when six hydrophobic cubes are dispersed in water, the surrounding water molecules are forced into entropically unfavorable interactions with all 36 faces of the cubes. When the six hydrophobic cubes aggregate together, the number of exposed faces is reduced to 22. The aggregate's stability is maintained because its dissociation would increase the number of water molecules that are unfavorably affected by nearly 40%. Amphipathic molecules associate together for the same reason. The structure of the resulting complex, such as a micelle or bilayer, is determined by the geometries of the hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions.

## Electrostatic Interactions

Electrostatic interactions between oppositely charged groups within or between biomolecules are termed ionic bonds or salt bridges.

Salt bridges are comparable in strength to hydrogen bonds.

Salt bridges act over larger distances than hydrogen bonds.

Salt bridges facilitate the association of charged molecules to proteins and nucleic acids.

## van der Waals Forces

van der Waals forces arise from attractions between transient dipoles generated by the rapid movement of electrons in all neutral molecules.

van der Waals forces are significantly weaker than hydrogen bonds.

van der Waals forces decrease as the sixth power of the intervening distance.

van der Waals forces act over very short distances, typically 2 to 4 Å.

## Many Metabolic Reactions Involve Group Transfer

Many enzymic reactions are responsible for the synthesis and breakdown of biomolecules. Many enzymic reactions involve the transfer of a chemical group G from a donor D to an acceptor A. The transfer results in the formation of an acceptor group complex, A—G. The reaction can be represented as D single bond G and A in equilibrium with A single bond G and D. Hydrolysis and phosphorolysis of glycogen involve the transfer of glucosyl groups to water or to orthophosphate. The equilibrium constants for these hydrolysis reactions strongly favor the formation of split products. Many group transfer reactions responsible for the biosynthesis of macromolecules are thermodynamically unfavored. Enzyme catalysts play a critical role in surmounting these barriers. Enzymes do this by physically connecting and thermodynamically coupling two normally separate reactions. The free energy change of the coupled reaction is the sum of the values for the two linked reactions. This coupling allows the formation of normally-disfavored covalent bonds required for biopolymer synthesis. The hydrolysis of ATP is an example of a reaction that can be coupled to make such synthesis feasible.

# pH IS THE NEGATIVE LOG OF THE HYDROGEN ION CONCENTRATION

The term pH was introduced in 1909 by Sörensen. Sörensen defined pH as the negative log of the hydrogen ion concentration. The equation for pH is pH equals negative log left bracket H positive right bracket. The definition of pH suffices for most biochemical purposes. To calculate the pH of a solution, calculate the hydrogen ion concentration [H+]. Calculate the base 10 logarithm of [H+]. pH is the negative of the value found in the logarithm calculation. For pure water at 25°C, the pH is 7. The value of pH is also known as the power in English. The value of pH is known as puissant in French. The value of pH is known as potenz in German. The term 'p' comes from the term 'power' in English, 'puissant' in French, or 'potenz' in German.

Low pH values correspond to high concentrations of H+. High pH values correspond to low concentrations of H+. Acids are proton donors. Bases are proton acceptors. Strong acids, such as HCl and H2SO4, completely dissociate into anions and protons even in strongly acidic solutions. Weak acids dissociate only partially in acidic solutions. Strong bases, such as KOH and NaOH, are completely dissociated even at high pH. Weak bases like Ca(OH)2 do not completely dissociate. Many biochemicals are weak acids. Exceptions include phosphorylated intermediates, whose phosphoryl group contains two dissociable protons, the first of which is strongly acidic.

To calculate the pH of a solution with a hydrogen ion concentration of 3.2 × 10-4 mol/L, use the equation pH equals negative log of the concentration. The pH of a solution with a hydroxide ion concentration of 4.0 × 10-4 mol/L is calculated using pOH. pOH is defined as −log[OH−]. The equation for water dissociation is Kw equals [H+][OH−] equals 10-14. log[H+] plus log[OH−] equals log 10-14. pH plus pOH equals 14. To find the pH from pOH, use the equation pH equals 14 minus pOH. For a hydroxide concentration of 4.0 × 10-4 mol/L, the pH is 10.6. The logarithmic pH scale facilitates recording and comparing hydrogen ion concentrations that differ by orders of magnitude.

For the pH values of KOH solutions, consider the OH− from two sources: KOH and water. In a 2.0 × 10-2 mol/L KOH solution, the contribution of water to the total [OH−] is negligible. ## The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation Describes the Behavior of Weak Acids & Buffers

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation describes the behavior of weak acids and buffers. A weak acid, HA, ionizes into H+ and A−. The equilibrium constant for this dissociation is represented by Ka, which equals [H+][A−]/[HA]. Cross-multiplication gives [H+][A−] = Ka[HA]. Dividing both sides by [A−] results in [H+] = Ka[HA]/[A−]. Taking the log of both sides leads to log([H+]) = log(Ka[HA]/[A−]). Multiplying through by −1 gives −log([H+]) = −log(Ka[HA]/[A−]). Substituting pH and pKa for −log([H+]) and −log(Ka), respectively, results in pH = pKa − log([HA]/[A−]). Inverting the last term removes the minus sign, giving the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log([A−]/[HA]).

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation has great predictive value in protonic equilibria. When an acid is exactly half-neutralized, [A−] = [HA]. Under these conditions, pH = pKa. When the ratio [A−]/[HA] is 100:1, the pH is significantly higher than pKa. When the ratio [A−]/[HA] is 1:10, the pH is significantly lower than pKa. Evaluating the equation at ratios ranging from 103 to 10-3 and plotting the calculated pH values describes the titration curve for a weak acid (Figure 2-5).

## Weak Acids Can Be Used to Establish & Maintain the pH of an Aqueous Solution

Solutions of weak acids or bases and their conjugates exhibit buffering, the ability to resist a change in pH following addition of strong acid or base. Many metabolic reactions are accompanied by the release or uptake of protons. Biologic maintenance of a constant pH involves buffering by phosphate, bicarbonate, and proteins, each of which contains one or more functional groups capable of accepting or releasing protons at physiological pH values. For laboratory experiments using tissue extracts or enzymes, it is convenient to avoid using metabolites to maintain a constant pH.
