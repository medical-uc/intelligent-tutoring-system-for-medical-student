# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Heme is synthesized from porphyrins and iron. Bile pigments are produced by heme degradation. The biochemistry of porphyrins and heme is fundamental to understanding the functions of hemoproteins. The porphyrias are a group of diseases caused by abnormalities in the pathway of porphyrin biosynthesis. Jaundice is a common clinical condition that reflects elevated levels of plasma bilirubin. Jaundice can result from the overproduction of bilirubin. Jaundice can also result from failures in the excretion of bilirubin. Jaundice occurs in numerous diseases, including hemolytic anemias, viral hepatitis, and cancer of the pancreas.

# PORPHYRINS

Porphyrins are cyclic compounds formed by the linkage of four pyrrole rings through methine (= HC—) bridges (Figure 31–1).

[FIGURE:fig-31-1]

The porphyrin molecule. Rings are labeled I, II, III, and IV. Substituent positions are labeled 1 through 8. The four methine bridges (=HC—) are labeled α, β, γ, and δ.

The nitrogen atoms of the four pyrrole rings enable porphyrins to coordinately bind metal ions.

Examples of porphyrin-metal ion complexes include iron porphyrins such as the heme of hemoglobin and the magnesium-containing porphyrin chlorophyll, the photosynthetic pigment of plants.

Heme proteins are ubiquitous in biology and serve diverse functions including, but not limited to, oxygen transport and storage (eg, hemoglobin and myoglobin) and electron transport (eg, cytochrome c and cytochrome P450).

Hemes are tetrapyrroles, of which two types, heme b and heme c, predominate (Figure 31–2).

[FIGURE:fig-31-2]

Structures of heme b and heme c.

In heme c the vinyl groups of heme b are replaced by covalent thioether links to an apoprotein, typically via cysteinyl residues.

Thus, unlike heme b, heme c does not readily dissociate from its apoproteins.

# HEME IS SYNTHESIZED FROM SUCCINYL-CoA & GLYCINE

The biosynthesis of heme occurs in most mammalian cells. Mature erythrocytes lack mitochondria, so they do not synthesize heme. Approximately 85% of heme synthesis takes place in erythroid precursor cells in the bone marrow. The remainder of heme synthesis primarily occurs in hepatocytes. Heme synthesis begins with the condensation of succinyl-CoA and glycine. The reaction that begins heme synthesis is dependent on pyridoxal phosphate. Mitochondrial δ-aminolevulinate synthase (ALA synthase) catalyzes the reaction that begins heme synthesis.

Humans express two isozymes of ALA synthase: ALAS1 and ALAS2. ALAS1 is ubiquitously expressed throughout the body. ALAS2 is expressed in erythrocyte precursor cells. The formation of δ-aminolevulinate is the rate-limiting step for porphyrin biosynthesis in the mammalian liver (Figure 31–3).

[FIGURE:fig-31-3]

Synthesis of δ-aminolevulinate (ALA). This mitochondrial reaction is catalyzed by ALA synthase.

Following the formation of δ-aminolevulinate, it exits into the cytosol where ALA dehydratase catalyzes the condensation of two molecules of ALA to form porphobilinogen (Figure 31–4). ALA dehydratase is a zinc metalloprotein sensitive to inhibition by lead. ALA dehydratase requires an Fe-S cofactor for optimal activity. The Fe-S cofactor allows regulation of porphyrin biosynthesis in response to iron availability.

[FIGURE:fig-31-4]

The third reaction, catalyzed by hydroxymethylbilane synthase, involves the head-to-tail condensation of four molecules of porphobilinogen to form the linear tetrapyrrole hydroxymethylbilane (Figure 31–5, top). Hydroxymethylbilane is formed from the condensation of four molecules of porphobilinogen. The subsequent cyclization of hydroxymethylbilane, catalyzed by uroporphyrinogen III synthase, forms uroporphyrinogen III (Figure 31–5, bottom right). Under normal conditions, the uroporphyrinogen formed is almost exclusively the type III isomer. Type I isomers of uroporphyrinogen can be formed in certain porphyrias.

## ALA Synthase Is the Key Regulatory Enzyme in Hepatic Biosynthesis of Heme

ALA synthase 1 (ALAS1) is expressed throughout body tissues. ALAS1 catalyzes the rate-limiting reaction for heme biosynthesis in the liver. ALAS1 has a short half-life. Heme regulates the synthesis of ALAS1 through the Erg-1 aporepressor and one of its NAB corepressors. The absence of heme greatly increases the synthesis of ALAS1. The presence of heme diminishes the synthesis of ALAS1. Heme affects the translation of ALAS1. Heme affects the translocation of ALAS1 from the cytosol to the mitochondria. Many drugs that require cytochrome P450 for metabolism induce enhanced biosynthesis of this hemoprotein. The induction of hemoprotein biosynthesis depletes the intracellular heme pool. The depletion of the heme pool induces the synthesis of ALAS1. The rate of heme synthesis rises to meet metabolic demand. ALAS2 is not feedback regulated by heme. The biosynthesis of ALAS2 is not induced by these drugs.

Cytochrome P450 is an enzyme required for the metabolism of many drugs. Drugs that require cytochrome P450 induce enhanced biosynthesis of hemoproteins. The enhanced biosynthesis of hemoproteins depletes the intracellular heme pool. The depletion of the heme pool induces the synthesis of ALAS1.

# PORPHYRINS ARE COLORED & FLUORESCE

Porphyrinogens are colorless. The various porphyrins are colored. The conjugated double bonds in the pyrrole rings and linking methine groups of porphyrins are responsible for their characteristic absorption and fluorescence spectra. The visible and ultraviolet spectra of porphyrins and porphyrin derivatives are useful for their identification (Figure 31--8).

The sharp absorption band near 400 nm is a distinguishing feature shared by all porphyrins. The sharp absorption band near 400 nm is termed the Soret band, named after its discoverer, the French physicist Charles Soret.

Porphyrins dissolved in strong mineral acids or in organic solvents emit a strong red fluorescence when illuminated by ultraviolet light. The strong red fluorescence is often used to detect small amounts of free porphyrins.

# DISORDERS OF HEME BIOSYNTHESIS

Disorders of heme biosynthesis can be genetic or acquired. Lead poisoning is an example of an acquired defect. Lead inactivates ferrochelatase by binding to essential thiol groups. Lead inactivates ALA dehydratase by binding to essential thiol groups. Lead poisoning causes elevated levels of protoporphyrin in erythrocytes. Lead poisoning causes elevated urinary levels of ALA. Lead poisoning causes elevated urinary levels of coproporphyrin. Genetic disorders of heme metabolism and bilirubin metabolism share features with metabolic disorders of urea biosynthesis. Different mutations in genes that encode a given enzyme can cause similar clinical signs and symptoms. Mutations in genes that encode enzymes catalyzing successive reactions can cause similar clinical signs and symptoms. Rational therapy requires understanding the biochemistry of enzyme-catalyzed reactions in both normal and impaired individuals. Identifying intermediates and side products that accumulate before a metabolic block can provide the basis for metabolic screening tests. Metabolic screening tests can implicate the impaired reaction. Definitive diagnosis involves quantitative assay of the activity of the enzyme(s) suspected to be defective. Translocation of enzymes and intermediates between cellular compartments may involve as yet incompletely identified factors. Comparing the DNA sequence of a gene that encodes a mutant enzyme to that of the wild-type gene can identify specific mutations causing the disease.

Blocks later in the pathway cause the accumulation of porphyrinogens.

The oxidation of these porphyrinogens results in photosensitivity to visible light of about 400-nm wavelength.

The excitation of these porphyrinogens and their reaction with molecular oxygen generate oxygen radicals.

Proteolytic enzymes cause variable degrees of skin damage, including scarring.

# CLASSIFICATION OF THE PORPHYRIAS

Porphyrias are classified as erythropoietic or hepatic based on the organs most affected.

Porphyrias can also be classified as acute or cutaneous based on their clinical features.

Different and variable levels of heme, toxic precursors, or metabolites account for the differential effects of specific porphyrias on individual cell types and organs.

## Drug-Induced Porphyria

Certain drugs, such as barbiturates and griseofulvin, induce elevated production of cytochrome P450. Elevated production of cytochrome P450 can precipitate an attack of porphyria by depleting heme levels. Depletion of heme levels leads to derepression of synthesis of ALAS1. Derepression of synthesis of ALAS1 results in increased levels of potentially harmful heme precursors.

## Possible Treatments for Porphyrias

Present treatment of porphyrias is essentially symptomatic. Avoiding drugs that induce production of cytochrome P450 is part of the treatment for porphyrias. Ingestion of large amounts of carbohydrate is recommended in the treatment of porphyrias. Administration of hematin represses ALAS1 synthesis. Hematin diminishes the production of harmful heme precursors. Patients with porphyrias exhibiting photosensitivity benefit from sunscreens. Administered β-carotene can lessen production of free radicals in patients with porphyrias. Decreased production of free radicals reduces photosensitivity in patients with porphyrias.

# CATABOLISM OF HEME PRODUCES BILIRUBIN

Human adults normally destroy about 200 billion erythrocytes per day. A 70-kg human turns over approximately 6 g of hemoglobin daily. All products are reused. The globin is degraded to its constituent amino acids. The released iron enters the iron pool. The iron-free porphyrin portion of heme is also degraded, mainly in the reticuloendothelial cells of the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. The catabolism of heme from all heme proteins is catalyzed by heme oxygenase, EC 1.14.18.18. Heme oxygenase synthesis is substrate-inducible. Heme serves as both substrate and a cofactor for the reaction. The iron of the heme that reaches heme oxygenase is usually in its oxidized, ferric form (hemin).

The carbon monoxide produced does not severely inhibit heme oxygenase despite its high affinity for heme-Fe2+. In humans, biliverdin reductase (EC 1.3.1.24) reduces the central methylene bridge of biliverdin to a methyl group. In humans, biliverdin reductase (EC 1.3.1.24) produces the yellow-pigment bilirubin (Figure 31–10).

[FIGURE:fig-31-10]

Conversion of ferric heme to biliverdin, and then to bilirubin. (1) Conversion of ferric heme to biliverdin is catalyzed by the heme oxygenase system. (2) Subsequently, biliverdin reductase reduces bilirubin to bilirubin.

Birds excrete the green-colored biliverdin directly. Amphibians excrete the green-colored biliverdin directly. 1 g of hemoglobin yields about 35 mg of bilirubin. Human adults form 250 to 350 mg of bilirubin per day. Bilirubin is derived principally from hemoglobin. Bilirubin is derived with contributions from ineffective erythropoiesis and the catabolism of other heme proteins. Conversion of heme to bilirubin by reticuloendothelial cells can be observed visually. The purple color of the heme in a hematoma slowly converts to the yellow pigment of bilirubin.

## Bilirubin Is Transported to the Liver Bound to Serum Albumin

Bilirubin is only sparingly soluble in water. Bilirubin is bound by serum albumin for transport to the liver. Albumin has both high-affinity and low-affinity sites for bilirubin. The high-affinity site can bind approximately 25 mg of bilirubin per 100 mL of plasma. More loosely bound bilirubin can readily dissociate and diffuse into tissues. Some antibiotics can displace bilirubin from albumin’s high-affinity site. Certain other drugs can displace bilirubin from albumin’s high-affinity site.

## Further Metabolism of Bilirubin Occurs Primarily in the Liver

Hepatic catabolism of bilirubin takes place in three stages: uptake by the liver, conjugation with glucuronic acid, and secretion in the bile.

## Uptake of Bilirubin by Liver Parenchymal Cells

Bilirubin dissociates from albumin and is taken up at the sinusoidal surface of hepatocytes by a large capacity facilitated transport system.

The transport system for bilirubin uptake is saturable, but it does not appear to be rate-limiting for the metabolism of bilirubin even under pathologic conditions.

The net uptake of bilirubin depends on its removal by subsequent metabolism.

Once internalized, bilirubin binds to cytosolic proteins such as glutathione S-transferase, previously known as a ligandin, to prevent bilirubin from reentering the bloodstream.

## Conjugation of Bilirubin With Glucuronate

Bilirubin is nonpolar. Bilirubin would persist in cells if not converted to a more water-soluble form. Conversion to a more polar form occurs by conjugation with two molecules of glucuronic acid. Conversion to a more polar form is catalyzed by a bilirubin-specific UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (EC 2.4.1.17) of the endoplasmic reticulum. The reaction proceeds as follows: Bilirubin reacts with UDP-glucuronate to form bilirubin monoglucuronide and UDP. Bilirubin monoglucuronide reacts with UDP-glucuronate to form bilirubin diglucuronide and UDP. Glucuronate moieties are attached via ester bonds to the two propionate groups of bilirubin (Figure 31-11). Clinically, the diglucuronide is termed 'direct reacting' bilirubin.

## Secretion of Bilirubin Into the Bile

Conjugated bilirubin is secreted into the bile by an active transport mechanism. The active transport mechanism is probably rate-limiting for the overall process of hepatic bilirubin metabolism. The protein involved in this transport is a multispecific organic anion transporter (MOAT), located in the plasma membrane of the bile canaliculi. MOAT is a member of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter family. MOAT transports a number of organic anions. The hepatic transport system for conjugated bilirubin into the bile is inducible by the same drugs that can induce the conjugation of bilirubin. Conjugation and excretion of bilirubin constitute a coordinated functional unit.

Most of the bilirubin excreted in the bile of mammals is bilirubin diglucuronide. Bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity can be induced by several drugs, including phenobarbital. When bilirubin conjugates exist abnormally in human plasma, such as in obstructive jaundice, they are predominantly monoglucuronides. ## Intestinal Bacteria Reduce Conjugated Bilirubin to Urobilinogen

Conjugated bilirubin reaches the terminal ileum and the large intestine. Specific bacterial β-glucuronidases remove the glucuronosyl moieties from conjugated bilirubin. Specific bacterial β-glucuronidases are classified as EC 3.2.1.31. The intestinal microbiota reduces the resulting compounds to form colorless tetrapyrroles called urobilinogens. Small portions of urobilinogens are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum and large intestine. The reabsorbed urobilinogens are reexcreted via the enterohepatic urobilinogen cycle. Under abnormal conditions, excessive bile pigment formation can lead to increased urobilinogen excretion in the urine. Liver disease can disrupt the intrahepatic cycle, leading to increased urobilinogen excretion in the urine. Most of the colorless urobilinogens formed in the colon are oxidized to colored urobilins. The feces are darkened by the oxidation of residual urobilinogens to urobilins upon standing in air.

## Measurement of Bilirubin in Serum

Quantitation of bilirubin employs a colorimetric method based on the reddish-purple color formed when bilirubin reacts with diazotized sulfanilic acid.

An assay conducted in the absence of added methanol measures direct bilirubin, which is bilirubin glucuronide.

An assay conducted in the presence of added methanol measures total bilirubin.

The difference between total bilirubin and direct bilirubin is known as indirect bilirubin, and it is unconjugated bilirubin.

# HYPERBILIRUBINEMIA CAUSES JAUNDICE

Hyperbilirubinemia occurs when the blood level of bilirubin exceeds 1 mg/dL (17 μmol/L). Hyperbilirubinemia can result from the liver producing more bilirubin than it can excrete. Hyperbilirubinemia can also result from a damaged liver failing to excrete normal amounts of bilirubin. Obstruction of the liver's excretory ducts can prevent bilirubin excretion and cause hyperbilirubinemia. Jaundice occurs when the blood concentration of bilirubin reaches 2 to 2.5 mg/dL. Jaundice is also known as icterus. Jaundice is characterized by tissues turning yellow.

## Occurrence of Unconjugated Bilirubin in Blood

Retention hyperbilirubinemia is due to overproduction of bilirubin. Regurgitation hyperbilirubinemia is due to reflux into the bloodstream because of biliary obstruction.

Because of its hydrophobicity, unconjugated bilirubin can cross the blood–brain barrier. Unconjugated bilirubin can enter the central nervous system.

Encephalopathy due to hyperbilirubinemia (kernicterus) occurs only with unconjugated bilirubin. Retention hyperbilirubinemia is a condition where unconjugated bilirubin levels are elevated.

Conjugated bilirubin appears in urine because of its water solubility.

Choluric jaundice occurs only in regurgitation hyperbilirubinemia. Acholuric jaundice occurs only in the presence of an excess of unconjugated bilirubin.

A moderate hyperbilirubinemia accompanies hemolytic anemias.

Hyperbilirubinemia is usually modest (< 4 mg bilirubin/dL; < 68 μmol/L). A healthy liver has a high capacity to metabolize bilirubin.

A stone in the common bile duct is a common cause of obstruction of the biliary tree. Cancer of the head of the pancreas is a common cause of obstruction of the biliary tree.

Liver diseases are frequent causes of predominantly conjugated hyperbilirubinemia. Hepatitis is an example of liver diseases causing predominantly conjugated hyperbilirubinemia.

## Neonatal “Physiologic Jaundice”

Neonatal “physiologic jaundice” results from accelerated hemolysis and an immature hepatic system for the uptake, conjugation, and secretion of bilirubin. In neonatal “physiologic jaundice,” bilirubin-glucuronosyltransferase activity is reduced. In neonatal “physiologic jaundice,” synthesis of UDP-glucuronate is probably also reduced. When the plasma concentration of unconjugated bilirubin exceeds that which can be tightly bound by albumin, it can penetrate the blood–brain barrier. The threshold for unconjugated bilirubin tightly bound by albumin is 20–25 mg/dL. Hyperbilirubinemic toxic encephalopathy, or kernicterus, can result in mental retardation if left untreated. Exposure of jaundiced neonates to blue light promotes hepatic excretion of unconjugated bilirubin. Phototherapy converts a portion of unconjugated bilirubin to derivatives that are excreted in the bile. Phenobarbital may be administered as a promoter of bilirubin metabolism.

## Defects of Bilirubin UDP-Glucuronosyltransferase

Glucuronosyltransferases (EC 2.4.1.17) generate polar conjugates of various drugs and drug metabolites. Glucuronosyltransferases facilitate the excretion of drugs and drug metabolites. Mutations in the gene encoding bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase can result in reduced enzyme activity. Mutations in the gene encoding bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase can result in absent enzyme activity. Gilbert syndrome reflects the severity of impairment in bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity. Crigler-Najjar syndrome type 1 reflects the severity of impairment in bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity. Crigler-Najjar syndrome type 2 reflects the severity of impairment in bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity.

## Gilbert Syndrome

In Gilbert syndrome, bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity is reduced.

In Gilbert syndrome, bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity is not eliminated.

If retained activity equals or exceeds 30% of normal levels, Gilbert syndrome is essentially harmless.

## Type I Crigler-Najjar Syndrome

Type I Crigler-Najjar syndrome causes severe congenital jaundice with bilirubin levels exceeding 20 mg/dL in serum. Type I Crigler-Najjar syndrome results in complete absence of hepatic UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity. The brain damage associated with type I Crigler-Najjar syndrome is a consequence of the severe jaundice. Phototherapy is used to reduce plasma bilirubin levels in type I Crigler-Najjar syndrome. Phenobarbital does not have a beneficial effect on type I Crigler-Najjar syndrome. Type I Crigler-Najjar syndrome is often fatal within the first 15 months of life.

## Type II Crigler-Najjar Syndrome

In type II Crigler-Najjar syndrome, some bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity is retained.

Type II Crigler-Najjar syndrome is more benign than type I syndrome.

Serum bilirubin in type II Crigler-Najjar syndrome tends not to exceed 20 mg/dL of serum.

Patients with type II Crigler-Najjar syndrome respond to treatment with large doses of phenobarbital.

## Toxic Hyperbilirubinemia

Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia can result from toxin-induced liver dysfunction. Chloroform can cause toxin-induced liver dysfunction. Arsphenamines can cause toxin-induced liver dysfunction. Carbon tetrachloride can cause toxin-induced liver dysfunction. Acetaminophen can cause toxin-induced liver dysfunction. Hepatitis virus can cause toxin-induced liver dysfunction. Cirrhosis can cause toxin-induced liver dysfunction. Amanita mushroom poisoning can cause toxin-induced liver dysfunction. Toxin-induced liver dysfunction involves hepatic parenchymal cell damage. Hepatic parenchymal cell damage impairs bilirubin conjugation.

## Obstruction in the Biliary Tree Is the Most Common Cause of Conjugated Hyperbilirubinemia

Conjugated hyperbilirubinemia commonly results from blockage of the hepatic or common bile ducts. Gallstones are a common cause of this blockage. Cancer of the head of the pancreas can also cause this blockage. Bilirubin diglucuronide that cannot be excreted regurgitates into the hepatic veins and lymphatics. Conjugated bilirubin appears in the blood and urine, known as choluric jaundice. The stools typically are a pale color when this occurs.

Cholestatic jaundice includes all cases of extrahepatic obstructive jaundice. Cholestatic jaundice also includes conjugated hyperbilirubinemia due to micro obstruction of intrahepatic biliary ductules. Damaged hepatocytes can cause such micro obstruction. Micro obstruction of intrahepatic biliary ductules can occur in infectious hepatitis.

## Dubin-Johnson Syndrome

Dubin-Johnson syndrome is a benign autosomal recessive disorder.

Dubin-Johnson syndrome involves conjugated hyperbilirubinemia.

Conjugated hyperbilirubinemia in Dubin-Johnson syndrome occurs in childhood or during adult life.

Mutations in the gene encoding the protein involved in the secretion of conjugated bilirubin into bile cause hyperbilirubinemia in Dubin-Johnson syndrome.

## Some Conjugated Bilirubin Can Bind Covalently to Albumin

When high levels of conjugated bilirubin persist in plasma, a portion can become covalently bound to albumin.

δ-bilirubin, termed δ-bilirubin, has a longer half-life in plasma than does conventional conjugated bilirubin.

δ-bilirubin remains elevated during recovery from obstructive jaundice.

Some patients continue to appear jaundiced even after the circulating conjugated bilirubin level has returned to normal.

## Urinary Urobilinogen & Bilirubin Are Clinical Indicators

In complete obstruction of the bile duct, bilirubin has no access to the intestine for conversion to urobilinogen. In complete obstruction of the bile duct, no urobilinogen is present in the urine. The presence of conjugated bilirubin in the urine without urobilinogen suggests intrahepatic or posthepatic obstructive jaundice. In jaundice secondary to hemolysis, the increased production of bilirubin leads to increased production of urobilinogen. In jaundice secondary to hemolysis, increased urobilinogen appears in the urine in large amounts. Bilirubin is not usually found in the urine in hemolytic jaundice. The combination of increased urobilinogen and absence of bilirubin is suggestive of hemolytic jaundice. Increased blood destruction from any cause brings about an increase in urine urobilinogen. The most common causes of obstructive (posthepatic) jaundice are cancer of the head of the pancreas and a gallstone lodged in the common bile duct. The presence of bilirubin in the urine is sometimes referred to as choluria. Hepatitis and obstruction of the common bile duct cause choluric jaundice. The jaundice of hemolytic anemia is referred to as acholuric. The laboratory results in patients with hepatitis are variable, depending on the extent of damage to parenchymal cells and the extent of micro-obstruction to bile ductules. Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase are usually markedly elevated in hepatitis. Serum levels of alkaline phosphatase are elevated in obstructive liver disease.

The heme of hemoproteins such as hemoglobin and the cytochromes is an iron-porphyrin complex. Porphyrin consists of four pyrrole rings joined by methine bridges.
The eight methyl, vinyl, and propionyl substituents on the four pyrrole rings of heme are arranged in a specific sequence. The metal ion (Fe2+ in hemoglobin; Mg2+ in chlorophyll) is linked to the four nitrogen atoms of the pyrrole rings.
Biosynthesis of the heme ring involves eight enzyme-catalyzed reactions, some of which occur in mitochondria, others in the cytosol.
Synthesis of heme commences with the condensation of succinyl-CoA and glycine to form ALA. This reaction is catalyzed by ALAS1, the regulatory enzyme of heme biosynthesis.
Synthesis of ALAS1 increases in response to a low level of available heme. For example, certain drugs (eg, phenobarbital) indirectly trigger enhanced synthesis of ALAS1 by promoting synthesis of the heme protein cytochrome P450, which thereby depletes the heme pool. By contrast, ALAS2 is not regulated by heme levels, and consequently not by drugs that promote synthesis of cytochrome P450.
Genetic abnormalities of seven of the eight enzymes of heme biosynthesis result in inherited porphyrias. Erythrocytes and liver are the major sites of expression of the porphyrias. Photosensitivity and neurologic problems are common complaints. Intake of certain toxins (eg, lead) can cause acquired porphyrias. Increased amounts of porphyrins or their precursors can be detected in blood and urine, facilitating diagnosis.
Catabolism of the heme ring, initiated by the mitochondrial enzyme heme oxygenase, produces the linear tetrapyrrole, biliverdin. Subsequent reduction of biliverdin in the cytosol forms bilirubin.
Bilirubin binds to albumin for transport from peripheral tissues to the liver, where it is taken up by hepatocytes. The iron of heme is released and reutilized.
The water solubility of bilirubin is increased by the addition of two moles of the highly polar glucuronosyl moiety, derived from UDP-glucuronate, per mole of bilirubin. Attachment of the glucuronosyl moieties is catalyzed by bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase, one of a large family of enzymes of differing substrate specificities that increase the polarity of various drugs and drug metabolites, thereby facilitating their excretion.
Mutations in the encoding gene may result in reduced or absent bilirubin UDP-glucuronosyltransferase activity. Clinical presentations that reflect the severity of the mutation(s) include Gilbert syndrome and two types of Crigler-Najjar syndrome, conditions whose severity depends on the extent of remaining glucuronosyltransferase activity.
Following secretion of bilirubin from the bile into the gut, bacterial enzymes convert bilirubin to urobilinogen and urobilin, which are excreted in the feces and urine.
Colorimetric measurement of bilirubin employs the color formed when bilirubin reacts with diazotized sulfanilic acid. Assays conducted in the of added methanol measure “direct bilirubin” (ie, bilirubin glucuronide). Assays conducted in the of added methanol measure total bilirubin. The difference between total bilirubin and direct bilirubin, termed “indirect bilirubin,” is unconjugated bilirubin.
Jaundice results from an elevated level of plasma bilirubin. The causes of jaundice can be distinguished as prehepatic (eg, hemolytic anemias), hepatic (eg, hepatitis), or posthepatic (eg, obstruction of the common bile duct). Measurements of plasma total and nonconjugated bilirubin, of urinary urobilinogen and bilirubin, of the activity of certain serum enzymes, and the analysis of stool samples help distinguish between the causes of jaundice.
