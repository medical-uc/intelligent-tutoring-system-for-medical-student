# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Highly specialized muscle cells, known as myocytes, utilize a contractile apparatus consisting of physically juxtaposed actin and myosin polymers, or fibrils. Muscle tissue is susceptible to a variety of pathologic conditions, including hereditary diseases, malignant hyperthermia, and heart failure. Hereditary diseases encompass Duchenne-type muscular sclerosis, which is often treated with glucocorticoids. Heart failure is a prevalent medical condition with numerous causes. An understanding of the biochemistry of heart muscle is essential for the diagnosis and therapeutic intervention of heart failure. Vasodilators, such as nitroglycerin used in treating angina pectoris, function by increasing the formation of nitrous oxide (NO).

The cytoskeleton maintains the shape of all mammalian cells. The cytoskeleton maintains the integrity of all mammalian cells. The cytoskeleton maintains the internal organization of all mammalian cells. The cytoskeleton serves as a structural and mechanical network. The cytoskeleton mediates motional processes like cytokinesis, endocytosis, exocytosis, secretion, phagocytosis, and diapedesis. Yersinia exploits or manipulates the cytoskeleton of the infected host as part of its virulence mechanisms. Salmonella enterica exploits or manipulates the cytoskeleton of the infected host as part of its virulence mechanisms. Listeria monocytogenes exploits or manipulates the cytoskeleton of the infected host as part of its virulence mechanisms. Shigella exploits or manipulates the cytoskeleton of the infected host as part of its virulence mechanisms.

## Three Types of Muscle Exist: Skeletal, Cardiac, & Smooth

Muscle is a highly specialized tissue configured to convert the chemical energy of ATP potential into mechanical energy on a mass, macroscopic scale.

Three types of muscle are found in vertebrates: skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle.

Skeletal and cardiac muscle display a striated appearance, a consequence of the parallel alignment of the fibrils of their contractile apparatus.

Smooth muscle is devoid of striations, a consequence of the random orientation of its contractile fibrils.

Cardiac and skeletal muscle contract and exert force in only one dimension, like a coil spring.

Contracting smooth muscle exerts force and shortens in all directions, like the polymer skin of an inflated balloon.

Skeletal muscle is under conscious or voluntary nervous control.

Cardiac and smooth muscle work in an unconscious, involuntary manner.

## The Sarcomere Is the Functional Unit of Muscle

Striated muscle is composed of multinucleated muscle fiber cells. Muscle fiber cells may extend the entire length of the muscle. Each muscle fiber cell is surrounded by an electrically excitable plasma membrane called the sarcolemma. Within each muscle fiber cell, there are bundles of parallel myofibrils oriented longitudinally along the length of the cell. Myofibrils consist of interdigitated, overlapping thick and thin filaments embedded in intracellular fluid termed sarcoplasm. When a myofibril is examined by electron microscopy, alternating dark and light bands can be observed (Figure 50-1).

## Thin & Thick Filaments Slide Past Each Other During Contraction

The sliding filament model was based on careful morphologic observations of resting, extended, and contracting muscle.

When muscle contracts, the H zones and the I bands shorten.

The thin and thick filaments remain intact during muscle contraction.

The interdigitated filaments slide past one another during contraction.

The tension developed during muscle contraction is proportionate to the degree of filament overlap.

Contraction involves a dynamic interaction between the filaments.

## Actin & Myosin Account for 75% of the Protein Mass in Muscle

Actin and myosin contribute 20% and 55% of the protein mass in muscle, respectively.

The F-actin fiber, also known as the thin filament, is 6-to 7-nm thick and has a structure that repeats every 35.5 nm.

Myosin-I, which exists in cells as a monomer, links cytoskeletal microfilaments to the plasma membrane.

The predominant form of myosin in contractile tissues is myosin II, hereafter referred to simply as myosin, an asymmetric hexamer.

The hexamer consists of one pair of heavy (H) chains, and two pairs of light (L) chains, referred to as essential and regulatory.

The two heavy chains are intertwined, forming an extended helical tail, each capped by a globular head domain to which the light chains associate.

When skeletal muscle myosin is complexed to actin to form actomyosin complex, the complex exhibits ATPase activity.

## Tropomyosin & the Troponins Are Key Components of the Thin Filaments in Striated Muscle

Tropomyosin is present in all muscle and muscle-like structures. The chains of tropomyosin attach to F-actin in the groove between its filaments. Troponin is unique to striated muscle. The troponin complex consists of three polypeptides. Troponin T (TpT) binds to tropomyosin. Troponin T (TpT) binds to the other two troponin components. Troponin I (Tpl) inhibits the F-actin–myosin interaction. Troponin I (Tpl) binds to the other components of troponin. Troponin C (TpC) is a calcium-binding polypeptide. Troponin C (TpC) is structurally and functionally analogous to calmodulin. Calmodulin is an important calcium-binding protein widely distributed in nature. Up to four calcium ions can bind per molecule of troponin C. Up to four calcium ions can bind per molecule of calmodulin.

# MUSCLE CONVERTS CHEMICAL ENERGY TO MECHANICAL ENERGY

Muscle contraction occurs when thick filaments translocate along adjacent thin filaments by a process analogous to climbing a rope hand over hand. The S-1 domains of the myosin head act as the hands in this process. The S-1 domains ascend via a repeated cycle of attachment, an ATP-powered conformational change or power stroke, and detachment.

The S-1 head binds to actin, forming a cross-bridge linking the thick and thin filaments.

The clusters of cross-bridges at the ends of a thick filament have opposite polarities. The two polar regions of the thick filament are separated by a 150-nm segment called the M band, which does not participate in cross-bridge formation.

The release of the bound ADP is followed by the release of the bound ADP, triggering a large conformational change in the head of myosin in relation to its tail, known as the power stroke. The power stroke pulls the actin about 10 nm toward the center of the sarcomere. The nucleotide-free actin-myosin complex represents its low-energy state.

A molecule of ATP binds to the S-1 myosin head, drastically lowering the affinity of the myosin head for actin. Actin is thus released, or detached.

The thermodynamic efficiency of muscle contraction is about 50%, which compares favorably to the 20% or less of an internal combustion engine. While hydrolysis of ATP ultimately powers the cycle, the release of ADP provides the immediate driver for the conformational change in S-1 that translates chemical energy into the mechanical power stroke.

Rigor mortis, the stiffening of the body that occurs postmortem, happens because the fall in intracellular ATP levels prevents the dissociation of the myosin S-1 head from actin.

# CONTRACTION IS ORCHESTRATED BY THE SECOND MESSENGER Ca 2+

Muscle contraction from all sources occurs by a general mechanism.

Striated muscle relies on actin-based regulation.

Smooth muscle regulation is myosin-based.

## Actin-Based Regulation Occurs in Skeletal Muscle

The contractile apparatus in skeletal muscle is regulated by the troponin system. The troponin system is bound to tropomyosin and F-actin in the thin filament. In resting muscle, Tpl prevents the binding of the myosin head to its F-actin attachment site. Tpl alters the conformation of F-actin via the tropomyosin molecules. Tpl rolls tropomyosin into a position that blocks the sites on F-actin to which the myosin heads attach. Relief of inhibition by Tpl permits the power stroke cycle to proceed. Tpl reestablishes its inhibitory block on the binding of the myosin S-1 head to F-actin. The thick and thin filaments detach from one another. Detachment of the thick and thin filaments allows the muscle to relax.

## Myosin-Based Regulation Occurs in Smooth Muscle

Smooth muscles contract along multiple axes. Smooth muscles have molecular structures similar to those in striated muscle. Smooth muscles lack striations because their sarcomeres are not aligned. Smooth muscles lack the troponin system. In smooth muscle, the light chains prevent the binding of the myosin head to F-actin in the resting state. The inhibitory block is released by the phosphorylation of the regulatory, or type 2, light chains in the myosin S-1 head. Myosin light-chain kinase phosphorylates the regulatory light chains. Myosin light-chain kinase is controlled by the binding of calmodulin. Calmodulin is a small, ubiquitous protein. Calmodulin contains four EF-hand motifs. Each EF-hand motif can bind one molecule of calcium. Binding of calcium to all four sites triggers a conformational change in calmodulin. The dephosphorylation of regulatory light chains leads to muscle relaxation. Rho kinase phosphorylates myosin's regulatory light chains. Rho kinase phosphorylates the phosphatase that dephosphorylates the light chains. Phosphorylation of the phosphatase inhibits its activity. Phosphorylation by the cAMP-dependent protein kinase may dampen smooth muscle contraction. Light chains of myosin are different in striated and smooth muscles. Caldeshmon is a regulatory protein ubiquitous in smooth muscle. Caldeshmon is also present in nonmuscle tissue. Actin is free to bind myosin when caldesmon is released. Caldeshmon is subject to covalent modification by phosphorylation-dephosphorylation. When phosphorylated, caldesmon cannot bind actin. When phosphorylated, actin is free to interact with myosin. ## The Sarcoplasmic Reticulum Regulates Intracellular Levels of Ca2+ in Skeletal Muscle

The SR is a network of fibrous sacks. The SR is linked to the sarcomeres by the transverse channels of the T-tubule system. The pump uses the energy of hydrolysis of ATP to move calcium ions from regions of low to high concentration.

A dramatic fall in ATP concentration can occur by excessive usage during the cycle of contraction-relaxation or by diminished formation, such as might occur in ischemia. When ATP concentration falls, calcium ion levels will remain high.

When the sarcolemma is excited by a nerve impulse, the excitable membranes of the T-tubule system become depolarized.

There are two isoforms of the ryanodine receptor, RYR1 and RYR2. RYR1 is present in skeletal muscle. RYR2 is present in heart muscle. RYR2 is also present in brain tissue. ## Mutations in the Gene Encoding the Ca2+ Release Channel Are Cause of Human Malignant Hyperthermia

Exposure to certain anesthetics, such as halothane, and depolarizing skeletal muscle relaxants, such as succinylcholine, can cause malignant hyperthermia in some genetically predisposed patients. Symptoms of malignant hyperthermia include rigid skeletal muscles, hypermetabolism, and high fever. To prevent complications or death, anesthetic is stopped immediately and the drug dantrolene is administered.

Mutations in the RYR1 gene are associated with central core disease. Central core disease is a rare myopathy presenting in infancy with hypotonia and proximal muscle weakness. Electron microscopy reveals an absence of mitochondria in the center of many type I muscle fibers.

## Regulation of Contraction in Cardiac Muscle Is Also Actin-Based

Cardiac muscle is striated, like skeletal muscle. Cardiac muscle is regulated by an actin-myosin-tropomyosin-troponin system. Cardiac muscle exhibits intrinsic rhythmicity. Individual myocytes in cardiac muscle communicate with each other due to its syncytial nature. The T-tubular system is more developed in cardiac muscle. The sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) is less extensive in cardiac muscle. Cyclic AMP (cAMP) plays a more prominent role in cardiac muscle than in skeletal muscle. Protein kinases phosphorylate various transport proteins in the sarcolemma and SR. Protein kinases target the troponin-tropomyosin regulatory complex. There is a rough correlation between the phosphorylation of Tpl and the increased contraction of cardiac muscle induced by catecholamines. ## Skeletal Muscle Contains Large Quantities of Glycogen

Skeletal muscle contains large stores of glycogen in the sarcoplasm.

Glycogen is located in granules close to the I bands.

The release of glucose from glycogen depends on muscle glycogen phosphorylase.

Phosphorylase b kinase allows glucose mobilization to begin with the initiation of muscle contraction.

## Under Aerobic Conditions, Muscle Generates ATP Mainly by Oxidative Phosphorylation

Glucose is derived from blood glucose or endogenous glycogen.

Fatty acids are derived from the triacylglycerols of adipose tissue.

Glucose and fatty acids are the principal substrates used for aerobic metabolism in muscle.

Oxidative phosphorylation is the primary mechanism of ATP production in aerobic respiration.

Contracting muscles have a high demand for oxygen.

Some muscles are distinguished by their red color.

Red muscles contain the oxygen storage protein myoglobin.

Myoglobin protects against oxygen shortfalls.

## Creatine Phosphate Constitutes a Major Energy Reserve in Muscle

Creatine kinase is a muscle-specific enzyme with clinical utility in the detection of acute or chronic diseases of muscle. Creatine kinase catalyzes the synthesis of creatine phosphate from creatine and ATP. The equilibrium constant for this reaction is near 1. When ATP levels are high, formation of creatine phosphate is favored. When ATP levels drop, the equilibrium shifts in favor of ATP synthesis at the expense of stored creatine phosphate. Creatine phosphate provides a readily available source for a high-energy phosphate group. Creatine phosphate can be used to regenerate ATP from ADP.

## Adenylyl Kinase Serves as a Reserve of Last Resort

ATP synthase is the primary vehicle for regenerating ATP in living cells. ATP synthase can only synthesize ATP from ADP. Adenylyl kinase catalyzes the phosphorylation of AMP to form ADP. ATP serves as the phosphodonor in the reaction catalyzed by adenylyl kinase. Adenylyl kinase generates two molecules of ADP from one molecule each of AMP and ATP. When other means for regenerating ATP are exhausted, the equilibrium shifts in favor of synthesizing ATP at the expense of ADP. The by-product of the reaction catalyzed by adenylyl kinase is AMP. The process of using adenylyl kinase to regenerate ATP is only a temporary expedient. The cell can only cannibalize its total adenine nucleotide pool for so long.

# SKELETAL MUSCLE CONTAINS SLOW (RED) & FAST (WHITE) TWITCH FIBERS

Skeletal muscle contains different types of fibers. One classification subdivides them into type I (slow twitch), type IIA (fast twitch-oxidative), and type IIB (fast twitch-glycolytic). For simplicity, we consider only two types: type I (slow twitch, oxidative) and type II (fast twitch, glycolytic).

Type I fibers contain myoglobin and mitochondria. Type I fibers metabolism is aerobic. Type I fibers maintain relatively sustained contractions. Type II fibers lack myoglobin and contain few mitochondria. Type II fibers derive their energy from anaerobic glycolysis. Type II fibers perform contractions of relatively short duration.

The proportion of these two types of fibers varies among the muscles of the body. The proportion of these two types of fibers depends on function and training. The number of type I fibers in certain leg muscles increases in athletes training for marathons. The number of type II fibers increases in sprinters.

Type I fibers rely heavily on aerobic metabolism to regenerate ATP. Type II fibers rely on oxygen-independent sources such as creatine phosphate. Endurance athletes engage in carbohydrate loading to increase the size of their glycogen stores. Sprinters attempt to increase the pool of creatine phosphate in their muscles by ingesting creatine as a dietary supplement.

## Inherited Cardiomyopathies Can Arise From Disorders of Cardiac Energy Metabolism or Abnormal Myocardial Proteins

A cardiomyopathy is any structural or functional abnormality of the ventricular myocardium. A cardiomyopathy can arise from a number of causes, many of them hereditary. The causes of inherited cardiomyopathies fall into two broad classes. The first class involves disorders of cardiac energy metabolism, mainly reflecting mutations in genes encoding enzymes or proteins involved in fatty acid oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation. The second class involves mutations in genes encoding proteins involved in or affecting myocardial contraction, such as myosin, tropomyosin, the troponins, and cardiac myosin-binding protein C. Mutations in the genes (nuclear or mitochondrial) encoding various proteins, enzymes, or tRNA molecules are the fundamental causes of the inherited cardiomyopathies. ## Mutations in the Cardiac β-Myosin Heavy-Chain Gene Are One Cause of Familial Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy

Familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is one of the most commonly encountered hereditary cardiac diseases. Most cases of familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy are transmitted in an autosomal dominant manner. Some cases of familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy are sporadic. The substitutions cluster in the head and head-rod regions of the myosin heavy chain. One hypothesis is that these heavy chain variants, known as 'poison polypeptides', cause the formation of abnormal myofibrils. Abnormal myofibrils eventually result in compensatory hypertrophy in familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Patients with familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy can show great variation in clinical picture. Variation in clinical picture reflects genetic heterogeneity in familial hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Patients with mutations predicted to alter the charge character of the affected amino acid side chain exhibit a significantly shorter life expectancy. Patients with mutations that do not alter the charge character of the affected amino acid side chain have a longer life expectancy. Mutations in the genes encoding dystrophin, muscle LIM protein, the cyclic AMP response-element binding protein (CREB), desmin, and lamin have been implicated in the causation of dilated cardiomyopathy. Dystrophin and muscle LIM protein help organize the contractile apparatus of cardiac muscle cells. CREB regulates the expression of several genes within cardiac muscle cells.

## Mutations in the Gene Encoding Dystrophin Cause Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy

Titin is the world's largest known protein, and its role is to anchor the ends of myofibrils. Dystrophin is of particular biomedical interest. Mutations in the gene that encodes dystrophin cause Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Mutations in the gene that encodes dystrophin cause Becker muscular dystrophy. Mutations in the gene that encodes dystrophin have been implicated in dilated cardiomyopathy. Dystrophin bridges the actin cytoskeleton to the extracellular matrix at the interior face of the plasma membrane. Formation of this link is necessary for assembly of the synaptic junction. Duchenne muscular dystrophy results from the inability of mutationally altered forms of dystrophin to support formation of functionally competent synaptic junctions. Mutations in genes encoding polypeptide components of the sarcoglycan complex are responsible for certain other congenital forms of muscular dystrophy such as limb-girdle. Syntrophin binds to the carboxyl terminal of dystrophin. The function of the sarcoglycan complex and the nature of the interactions within the complex and between it and the other complexes are not clear. The sarcoglycan complex is formed only in striated muscle. The subunits of the sarcoglycan complex preferentially associate with each other, suggesting that the complex may function as a single unit. Mutations in the genes encoding the various sarcoglycans are responsible for limb-girdle dystrophies. Mutations in genes encoding other muscle proteins cause other types of muscular dystrophy. ## Nitric Oxide Relaxes the Smooth Muscle of Blood Vessels

Acetylcholine triggers the relaxation of the smooth muscle of blood vessels. Acetylcholine binds to its cell surface receptors on the endothelial cells surrounding vascular smooth muscle cells. The binding of acetylcholine triggers the activation of associated phospholipases on the interior surface of the plasma membrane. Phospholipases hydrolyze and release the polyphosphorylated head groups, particularly 3,4,5-triphosphoinositol, from phosphatidylinositol. Phosphatidylinositol is a quantitatively minor but functionally important phospholipid component of the plasma membrane. EDRF diffuses into the adjacent smooth muscle where it causes subsequent relaxation. EDRF was identified as nitrous oxide (NO).

NO synthase catalyzes the five-electron oxidation of a guanidino nitrogen in the side chain of arginine. The reaction yields citrulline and NO (Figure 50–11). The complex reaction utilizes NADPH and four redox-active prosthetic groups: FAD, FMN, heme, and tetrahydrobiopterin.

[FIGURE:fig-50-11]

Formation of nitric oxide (NO) from arginine in endothelial cells, catalyzed by NO synthase.

NO binds to the heme moiety of a soluble guanylyl cyclase upon diffusing into the surrounding vascular smooth muscle cells. This binding activates the enzyme and elevates the intracellular levels of the second messenger 3',5'-cyclic GMP (cGMP). The elevated cGMP levels stimulate the activities of certain cGMP-dependent protein kinases. These protein kinases phosphorylate specific muscle proteins, causing relaxation.

NO subsequently diffuses into adjacent smooth muscle, where it activates guanylyl cyclase. The activation of guanylyl cyclase leads to the formation of cGMP, stimulation of cGMP protein kinases, and subsequent relaxation. Nitroglycerin, a vasodilator, enters the smooth muscle cell, where its metabolism also leads to the formation of NO.

# SKELETAL MUSCLE CONSTITUTES THE MAJOR RESERVE OF PROTEIN IN THE BODY

Skeletal muscle proteins are the major nonfat source of stored energy in humans.

Large losses of muscle mass accompany prolonged caloric undernutrition, particularly in adults.

# THE CYTOSKELETON PERFORMS MULTIPLE CELLULAR FUNCTIONS

All cells perform mechanical work. Muscle cells perform mechanical work of higher physical magnitude than other cells. Cells perform self-propulsion. Cells perform cytokinesis. Cells perform endocytosis. Cells perform exocytosis. Cells perform phagocytosis. The cytoskeleton is the core machinery for these functions. The cytoskeleton comprises filamentous protein polymers. Actin filaments are part of the cytoskeleton. Actin filaments are also known as microfilaments. Microtubules are part of the cytoskeleton. Intermediate filaments are part of the cytoskeleton.

## Nonmuscle Cells Contain Microfilaments Containing G-Actin

G-actin is present in most if not all cells of the body. G-actin monomers spontaneously polymerize to form double helical F-actin filaments under physiologic conditions. F-actin filaments have a diameter of 7-9.5 nm. Actin microfilaments in the cytoskeleton generally exist as bundles within a tangled-appearing meshwork. Stress fibers are prominent bundles that just underlie the plasma membranes of many cells. Stress fibers disappear as cell motility increases. Stress fibers also disappear on malignant transformation of cells.

Actin filaments in nonmuscle cells can interact with myosin to cause cellular movements. Although actin filaments in nonmuscle cells are not organized as in muscle, they can still interact with myosin.

## Microtubules Contain α- & β-Tubulins

Microtubules are an integral component of the cellular cytoskeleton. Microtubules consist of cytoplasmic tubes that are 25 nm in diameter. Cytoplasmic tubes are often of extreme length. Each cylindrical tube is comprised of 13 longitudinally arranged protofilaments. Assembly of microtubules starts with the formation of tubulin dimers. Tubulin dimers assemble into protofilaments. Protofilaments associate with one another in parallel to form sheets and eventually cylinders. GTP is required for assembly. A microtubule-organizing center, located around a pair of centrioles, nucleates the growth of new microtubules. Characteristic arrangements of microtubules are found in centrioles, basal bodies, cilia, and flagellae. Microtubules are necessary for mitotic spindle formation and function. The functions include chromosome segregation during cell division. Microtubules are involved in the intracellular movement of endocytic and exocytic vesicles. Microtubules are structural components of cilia and flagella. Microtubules maintain the structure of axons and dendrites. Microtubules participate in the axoplasmic flow of material along neuronal processes. A variety of microtubule-associated proteins [MAPs], including tau, play important roles in microtubule assembly and stabilization. Microtubules exist in a state of dynamic instability, constantly assembling and disassembling. Microtubules exhibit polarity, with plus and minus ends. Polarity is important for growth from centrioles and directing intracellular movement. In axonal transport, the protein kinesin uses ATP hydrolysis to move vesicles down the axon toward the positive end of the microtubular formation. Cytosolic dynein powers the flow of materials in the opposite direction, toward the negative end. Axonemal dyneins power ciliary and flagellar movement. Dynamin, which uses GTP instead of ATP, is involved in endocytosis. Kinesins, dyneins, dynamin, and myosins are referred to as molecular motors. An absence of dynein in cilia and flagella results in immotile cilia and flagella. Absence of dynein leads to Kartagener syndrome, which can cause male sterility, situs inversus, and chronic respiratory infections. Mutations in genes affecting the synthesis of dynein have been detected in individuals with this syndrome. Pharmaceuticals such as colchicine, vinblastine, paclitaxel (Taxol), and griseofulvin disrupt microtubule assembly or disassembly. Colchicine is used for treatment of acute gouty arthritis. Vinblastine is a vinca alkaloid used for treating certain types of cancer. Paclitaxel (Taxol) is effective against ovarian cancer. Griseofulvin is an antifungal agent. ## Intermediate Filaments Differ From Microfilaments & Microtubules

Intermediate filaments have an axial periodicity of 21 nm. Intermediate filaments have a diameter of 8 to 10 nm, intermediate between that of microfilaments (6 nm) and microtubules (23 nm). Intermediate filaments are composed of elongated, fibrous molecules with a central rod domain, an amino-terminal head, and a carboxyl-terminal tail. Intermediate filaments assemble in a helical manner to form repeating tetrameric units, creating rope-like fibrils. Intermediate filaments are important structural components of cells, serving as relatively stable components of the cytoskeleton. Intermediate filaments do not undergo rapid assembly and disassembly. Intermediate filaments do not disappear during mitosis. Lamins are an exception, as they disassemble at mitosis after phosphorylation and reappear when mitosis ends. Lamins form a meshwork positioned in apposition to the inner nuclear membrane. Lamin A is an important component of the structural scaffolding that maintains the integrity of the cell nucleus.

Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS, progeria) is caused by mutations in the LMNA gene. The LMNA gene encodes lamin A and lamin C. In progeria, a farnesylated form of prelamin A accumulates. A mutation alters the site where the farnesylated portion is normally cleaved by proteases. The accumulation of farnesylated prelamin A destabilizes nuclei. The destabilization of nuclei alters their shape. The altered shape of nuclei predisposes individuals to signs of premature aging. Children affected by progeria often die in their teens from atherosclerosis. A farnesyltransferase inhibitor, lonafarnib, can moderately increase a patient's lifespan. Lonafarnib improves cardiac function. Prelamin A can still accumulate in the presence of farnesyltransferase inhibitors due to additional modifications. Investigations of combination therapies are being conducted in mice and HGPS human cells.

Keratins form a large family consisting of about 30 members. Two major types of keratins assemble into heterodimers made up of one member of each class. Vimentins are widely distributed in mesodermal cells. Desmin, glial fibrillary acidic protein, and peripherin are related to vimentins. All members of the vimentin-like family can copolymerize with each other. Intermediate filaments in nerve cells, called neurofilaments, are classified as low, medium, and high based on their molecular masses. The distribution of intermediate filaments in normal and abnormal cells, such as cancer cells, can be studied using immunofluorescent techniques. Pathologists use antibodies against specific intermediate filaments to determine the origin of certain dedifferentiated malignant tumors.

A number of skin diseases, mainly characterized by blistering, are due to mutations in genes encoding various keratins. Two such disorders are epidermolysis bullosa simplex and epidermolytic palmoplantar keratoderma. The blistering in these disorders likely reflects a diminished capacity of skin layers to resist mechanical stresses. The diminished capacity of skin layers to resist mechanical stresses is due to abnormalities in the keratin structure.

\- The myofibrils of skeletal muscle consist of thick, myosin-based and thin, actin-based filaments.
During contraction, these interdigitating filaments slide past one another with cross-bridges between myosin and actin generating and sustaining tension.
Muscle contraction is driven by the cyclic attachment, conformational shift, and detachment of massive numbers of myosin head domains to adjacent actin fibrils.
The hydrolysis of ATP is used to drive movement of the filaments.
In smooth muscle, the contractile apparatus is held in check by the regulatory light chains in myosin.
