# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Maintenance of human health and vitality requires the ingestion of trace levels of numerous inorganic elements. Inorganic elements include the transition metals iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), zinc (Zn), cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), nickel (Ni), molybdenum (Mo), vanadium (V), and chromium (Cr). Transition metals are sequestered within our bodies in organometallic complexes. Organometallic complexes enable the reactivity of transition metals to be controlled. The complexes direct the reactivity of transition metals where needed. Transition metals are key components of numerous enzymes and electron transport proteins. Transition metals are also components of the oxygen transport proteins hemoglobin and hemocyanin. Zinc finger motifs provide the DNA-binding domains for many transcription factors. Fe-S clusters are found in many of the enzymes that participate in DNA replication and repair. Nutritionally or genetically induced deficiencies of these metals are associated with a variety of pathologic conditions. Pernicious anemia is associated with iron deficiency. Menkes disease is associated with copper deficiency. Sulfite oxidase deficiency is associated with molybdenum deficiency. When ingested in large quantities, most heavy metals are highly toxic. Several of the nutritionally essential transition metals are potentially carcinogenic when ingested in large quantities.

The organic elements oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, sulfur, and phosphorous account for slightly more than 97% of the mass of the human body. Calcium contributes approximately 2% of the human body mass, primarily contained in bones, teeth, and cartilage. The remaining 0.4 to 0.5% of the human body mass is accounted for by numerous inorganic elements.

## Transition Metals Are Multivalent

Metal atoms have a propensity to undergo oxidation. Oxidation is a process in which metal atoms donate one or more electrons from their outer or valence shell to an electronegative acceptor species. Molecular oxygen is an example of an electronegative acceptor species.

Oxidation of alkali or alkaline earth metals results in a single ionized species, for example, Na+, K+, Li+, Mg2+, or Ca2+.

The oxidation of transition metals can yield multiple valence states. Table 10-2shows the valence states of transition metals.

Transition metals can undergo dynamic transitions between valence states. The transitions between valence states in transition metals occur through the addition or donation of electrons.

Transition metals can function as an electron donor during oxidation–reduction (redox) reactions. Transition metals can function as an electron acceptor during oxidation–reduction (redox) reactions. Transition metals can function as an electron carrier in the electron transport chain.

The ability of transition metals to act as acids further expands their biologic roles.

## Transition Metal Ions Are Potent Lewis Acids

In addition to serving as electron carriers, transition metals enhance their functional capabilities by acting as Lewis acids. Protic acids can donate a proton (H+) to an acceptor with a lone pair of electrons. Lewis acids are aprotic. Lewis acids possess empty valence orbitals capable of noncovalently associating with or “accepting” a lone pair of electrons from a donor molecule.

# TOXICITY OF HEAVY METALS

Most heavy metals are toxic. Heavy metals are also defined as metallic elements with atomic numbers greater than 20. Well-known examples of heavy metals include arsenic. Well-known examples of heavy metals include antimony. Well-known examples of heavy metals include lead. Well-known examples of heavy metals include mercury. Well-known examples of heavy metals include cadmium. The toxicity of heavy metals can arise via a number of mechanisms.

## Displacement of an Essential Cation

Some heavy metals can displace functionally essential metals, leading to loss or impairment of enzyme function.

Iron can be displaced by gallium in ribonucleotide reductase and Fe, Cu superoxide dismutase.

## Enzyme Inactivation

Heavy metals form adducts with free sulfhydryl groups. Enzyme inactivation occurs if the affected sulfhydryl resides in the enzyme site. Formation of heavy metal adducts with surface sulfhydryl groups undermines a protein’s structural integrity. Impairment of function follows the undermining of structural integrity. δ-aminolevulinate synthase is inhibited by Pb. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is inactivated by arsenite. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is inactivated by mercury. In pyruvate dehydrogenase, heavy metals react with the sulfhydryl on lipoic acid. Lipoic acid is an essential prosthetic group. The reaction occurs rather than with a peptidyl cysteine.

## Formation of Reactive Oxygen Species

Heavy metals can facilitate the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS can damage DNA, membrane lipids, and other biomolecules. Oxidative damage to DNA can cause genetic mutations. Genetic mutations may lead to cancer or other pathophysiologic conditions. ROS-mediated peroxidation of lipid molecules can lead to the loss of membrane integrity. The loss of membrane integrity can dissipate action potentials. The loss of membrane integrity can disrupt various cross-membrane transport processes. Disruption of these processes can be particularly deleterious to neurologic and neuromuscular functions.

# TOXICITY OF TRANSITION METALS

Several transition metals are nutritionally essential, but can be harmful if present in the body in excess. Higher organisms exert strict control over the uptake and excretion of transition metal ions. The hepcidin system regulates iron (see Figure 51--6).

Control mechanisms can be circumvented when transition metals enter the body via inhalation or absorption through the skin or mucous membranes. High, supraphysiologic levels of ingestion can overwhelm these mechanisms.

Acute poisoning by heavy metals or transition metals typically includes symptoms such as abdominal pain, vomiting, muscle cramps, confusion, and numbness. Treatments for such poisoning include the administration of metal chelating agents, diuretics, or hemodialysis if kidney function is compromised.

## Complexation Enhances Solubility & Controls Reactivity of Transition Metal Ions

Under normal circumstances, the levels of free transition metals in the body are extremely low. The vast majority of transition metals are associated directly with proteins through oxygen, nitrogen, and sulfur atoms found on the side chains of amino acids such as aspartate, glutamate, histidine, or cysteine. Transition metals are also found associated with organic moieties such as porphyrin, corrin, or pterins. The sequestration of transition metals into organometallic complexes provides protection against oxidation. Sequestration of transition metals into organometallic complexes suppresses the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Sequestration of transition metals into organometallic complexes enhances solubility. Sequestration of transition metals into organometallic complexes controls reactivity. ## Significance of Multivalent Capability

The physiologic function of transition metal-containing cofactors and prosthetic groups depends on maintaining the appropriate oxidation state of a multivalent ion. The heme is inserted into the globin polypeptide with its proximal and distal His residues. Methemoglobin is incapable of binding to and transporting oxygen. Incorporation into organometallic complexes preserves the functionally relevant oxidation state of the transition metal. Organometallic complexes protect against the potential for generating harmful ROS.

## Adjacent Ligands Can Modify Redox Potential

In organometallic complexes, the position and identity of the surrounding ligands can modify or tune the redox potential and Lewis acid potency of transition metal ions. The tuning of transition metal ions optimizes them for specific tasks. Cytochrome c and myoglobin are small, 12 to 17 kDa, monomeric proteins that contain a single heme iron. The iron atom in cytochrome c is optimized to cycle between the +2 and +3 valence states. Cytochrome c carries electrons between complexes III and IV of the electron transport chain. Superoxide dismutases (SODs) illustrate how complexation can adapt different transition metals as catalysts for a common chemical reaction. There are four distinct, nonhomologous SODs that contain different transition metals. The atomic symbols of the transition metals are used to designate each family: Fe-SODs, Mn-SODs, Ni-SODs, and Cu, Zn-SODs.

## Complexation Can Organize Multiple Metal Ions in a Single Functional Unit

The formation of organometallic complexes allows multiple metal ions to be assembled together in a single functional unit.

The enzyme urease contains two Ni atoms within its active site.

The Ni atoms enable urease to simultaneously polarize electrons in the C-N bond targeted for hydrolysis and to activate the attacking water molecule.

Cytochrome oxidase, or complex IV of the electron transport chain, contains two Fe and two Cu atoms.

The metal atoms in cytochrome oxidase enable it to accumulate the four electrons needed to reduce oxygen to water.

The bacterial enzyme nitrogenase employs an 8Fe-7S prosthetic group called the P-cluster and a unique Fe, Mo-cofactor.

The arrangement of an 8Fe-7S prosthetic group called the P-cluster and a unique Fe, Mo-cofactor in nitrogenase allows it to carry out the eight-electron reduction of atmospheric nitrogen to ammonia.

## Roles of Iron in Redox Reactions

The iron atoms of many metalloproteins facilitate the catalysis of oxidation-reduction, or redox, reactions. Stearoyl-acyl carrier protein $\Delta ^ { 9 . }$ -desaturase employs hemerythrin-like diiron centers to catalyze the reduction of carbon–carbon double bonds. Type 1 ribonucleotide reductase employs hemerythrin-like diiron centers to catalyze the reduction of an alcohol to methylene groups. Methane monooxygenase uses a similar diiron center for the oxidation of methane to methanol. This is a powerful oxidant that participates in the reduction and neutralization of a broad range of xenobiotics.

## Participation of Iron in Non-Redox Reactions

Purple acid phosphatases are bimetallic enzymes that contain one atom of iron paired with a second metal such as Zn, Mn, Mg, or another Fe. Purple acid phosphatases catalyze the hydrolysis of phosphomonoesters. The reaction generates hypochlorous acid, HOCl. Hypochlorous acid is a potent bacteriocide used by macrophages to kill entrapped microorganisms.

Many enzymes involved in DNA replication and repair contain Fe-S clusters. Enzymes involved in DNA replication and repair include DNA helicase, DNA primase, several DNA polymerases, some glycosylases and endonucleases, and several transcription factors. The elimination of these Fe-S clusters generally results in a loss of protein function. The specific roles performed by these Fe-S centers remain cryptic. Most Fe-S centers are located in the DNA binding domains of these proteins. It has been proposed that Fe-S centers may function as electrochemical detectors for identifying damaged DNA. Some speculate that these clusters serve as redox-sensitive modulators of catalytic activity or DNA binding. Others suggest that these clusters simply stabilize the three-dimensional structure of these proteins.

## Manganese

Humans contain several Mn-containing enzymes, the majority of which are located within the mitochondria.

Manganese-containing enzymes include isocitrate dehydrogenase from the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.

Glutamate synthetase and arginase are key players in nitrogen metabolism.

Pyruvate carboxylase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase are gluconeogenic enzymes.

Isopropyl malate synthase is another Mn-containing enzyme.

The mitochondrial isozyme of superoxide dismutase is also Mn-containing.

In most of these enzymes, Mn is present in the +2 oxidation state and is presumed to act as a Lewis acid.

Some bacterial organisms employ Mn in enzymes responsible for catalyzing redox reactions, where it cycles between the +2 and +3 oxidation states.

Examples of such enzymes include Mn-superoxide dismutase (Mn-SOD).

Mn-ribonucleotide reductase is another example.

Mn-catalase is also an example of an enzyme using Mn in redox reactions.

## Nickel

Nickel-containing enzymes are present in bacterial organisms. Ni, Fe hydrogenase catalyzes redox reactions. Methyl-coenzyme M reductase catalyzes redox reactions. Acetyl-CoA synthase catalyzes a group transfer reaction. Superoxide dismutase catalyzes a disproportionation reaction. Nickel is a key component of urease. ## Catalytic Roles of Molybdopterin

Molybdenum is a key component of the phylogenetically universal cofactor molybdopterin. In animals, molybdopterin serves as a catalytically essential prosthetic group for enzymes such as aldehyde oxidase, sulfite oxidase, and xanthine oxidase. Xanthine oxidase also contains flavin. Xanthine oxidase catalyzes the final two oxidative steps in the pathway by which uric acid is synthesized from purine nucleotides. The oxidation of hypoxanthine to xanthine is one of these steps. The oxidation of xanthine to uric acid is the other step. The bound Mo atom in these enzymes cycles among +4, +5, and +6 valence states. The cycling of the bound Mo atom facilitates the catalysis of the two-stage process. Aldehyde oxidase also contains an Fe-S cluster. Aldehyde oxidase oxidizes a broad range of substrates, including many heterocyclic organic compounds. It has been suggested that aldehyde oxidase participates in the detoxification of xenobiotics, similar to the cytochrome P450 system.

Mutations in the genes MOCS1, MOCS2, or GPNH can lead to sulfite oxidase deficiency. MOCS1, MOCS2, or GPNH encode proteins that catalyze key steps in the synthesis of molybdopterin. Sulfite oxidase deficiency is an autosomal inherited inborn error of metabolism. Individuals with sulfite oxidase deficiency cannot break down the sulfur-containing amino acids cysteine and methionine. The accumulation of these amino acids and their derivatives in neonatal blood and tissues causes severe physical deformities and brain damage. Sulfite oxidase deficiency leads to intractable seizures, severe mental retardation, and often death during early childhood.

## Vanadium

Although nutritionally essential, the role of vanadium in living organisms remains cryptic.

Various plasma proteins are known to bind oxides of vanadium, including albumin. Transferrin binds oxides of vanadium.

Vanadium is a key component of the secreted “glue” with which marine mussels secure themselves to rocks, pilings, and ship’s bottoms.

Vanadate, a phosphate analog, is known to inhibit protein-tyrosine phosphatases in vitro. Vanadate, a phosphate analog, is known to inhibit alkaline phosphatase in vitro. It is unclear whether these interactions are of physiologic significance.

## Chromium

The role of Cr in humans remains unknown. The laboratory effects of this factor implicated Cr as a cofactor in the regulation of glucose metabolism. Decades of research have failed to uncover a Cr-containing biomolecule. Decades of research have failed to uncover a Cr-related genetic disease in animals. Many persons continue to ingest Cr-containing dietary supplements.

## Transition Metals Are Absorbed by Diverse Mechanisms

Intestinal uptake of most transition metals is relatively inefficient. Only a small portion of the transition metals ingested each day is absorbed into our bodies. The perceived 'inefficiency' of intestinal absorption may reflect the combination of the human body's modest requirements for these elements. The perceived 'inefficiency' of intestinal absorption may reflect the need to buffer against the accumulation of excessive amounts of these potentially toxic heavy metals. We understand the pathways by which some transition metals, such as Fe, are taken up in great detail. In other instances, little hard evidence has been uncovered about the pathways by which some transition metals are taken up.

Cobalamin absorption involves a dedicated pathway with two secreted cobalamin-binding proteins, haptocorrin and intrinsic factor. Cobalamin absorption also involves a cell surface receptor, cubilin. In the stomach, cobalamin released from ingested foods binds to haptocorrin. Haptocorrin protects cobalamin from the extreme pH of the gastric surroundings. As the cobalamin-haptocorrin complex moves into the duodenum, the pH rises, inducing dissociation of the complex. The released cobalamin is then bound by intrinsic factor. The cobalamin-intrinsic factor complex is recognized and internalized by cubilin receptors on the surface of intestinal epithelial cells.

Maintenance of human health and vitality requires the dietary uptake of trace quantities of several inorganic elements, including several transition metals.
Paradoxically, many heavy metals, including excess levels of some of the nutritionally essential transition metals, are toxic and potentially carcinogenic.
Acute heavy metal poisoning is treated by ingestion of chelating agents, the administration of diuretics along with the ingestion of water, or hemodialysis.
Most heavy metals, including essential transition metals, can generate ROS in the presence of water and oxygen.
The capacity of transition metals to serve as carriers for electrons and diatomic gases, as well as to facilitate catalysis of a wide range of enzymatic reactions, derives from two factors: their ability to transition between multiple valence states and their Lewis acid properties.
In the body, transition metal ions are rarely encountered in free form. In most instances, they exist in organometallic complexes bound to proteins either directly by amino acid side chains or as part of organometallic prosthetic groups such as hemes, Fe-S clusters, or molybdopterin.
Incorporation into organometallic complexes serves as a means of optimizing the properties of associated transition metals, prevents the collateral generation of ROS, and organizes multiple transition metals into a single functional unit.
In the electron transport chain, many key electron transfer events rely on the ability of Fe atoms present in hemes, Fe-S clusters, and Rieske iron centers to transition between their +2 and +3 oxidation states.
The presence of two Fe and two Cu atoms enables cytochrome oxidase to accumulate the four electrons needed to reduce molecular oxygen to water.
Fe is commonly employed as a prosthetic group in many of the metalloenzymes that catalyze oxidation-reduction reactions.
The majority of the nearly 3000 predicted zinc metalloproteins encoded by the human genome contain a conserved polynucleotide-binding motif, the zinc finger.
Fe-S clusters are present in many of the proteins involved in DNA replication and repair. It has been postulated that these prosthetic groups serve as electrochemical sensors for DNA damage.
The Mo atoms in xanthine oxidase and sulfite oxidase cycle between three different valence states during catalysis.
The buildup of the S-containing amino acids methionine and cysteine in persons suffering from a deficiency in the Fe, Mo-metalloenzyme sulfite oxidase can cause serious developmental defects and death in infancy.
