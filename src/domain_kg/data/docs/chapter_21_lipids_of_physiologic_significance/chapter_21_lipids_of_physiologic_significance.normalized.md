# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Lipids are a heterogeneous group of compounds, including fats, oils, steroids, waxes, and related compounds. Lipids have the common property of being relatively insoluble in water but soluble in nonpolar solvents such as ether and chloroform. Lipids are important dietary constituents because of the high energy value of fats. Essential fatty acids are contained in the fat of natural foods. Fat-soluble vitamins are contained in the fat of natural foods. Other lipophilic micronutrients are contained in the fat of natural foods. Fat is stored in adipose tissue. Adipose tissue serves as a thermal insulator in the subcutaneous tissues. Adipose tissue serves as a thermal insulator around certain organs. Nonpolar lipids act as electrical insulators. Nonpolar lipids allow rapid propagation of depolarization waves along myelinated nerves. Lipids are transported in the blood combined with proteins in lipoprotein particles. Lipids have essential roles in nutrition and health. Knowledge of lipid biochemistry is necessary for understanding obesity. Knowledge of lipid biochemistry is necessary for understanding diabetes mellitus. Knowledge of lipid biochemistry is necessary for understanding atherosclerosis.

# LIPIDS MAY BE SIMPLE, COMPLEX, OR DERIVED

Simple lipids include fats, oils, and waxes that are esters of fatty acids with various alcohols. Fats are esters of fatty acids with glycerol. Oils are fats in the liquid state at room temperature. Waxes are esters of fatty acids with higher molecular weight monohydric alcohols.

Complex lipids are esters of fatty acids that always contain an alcohol and one or more fatty acids. Complex lipids also have other groups. Phospholipids contain a phosphoric acid residue. Phospholipids frequently have nitrogen-containing bases, such as choline, and other substituents. In many phospholipids, the alcohol is glycerol, known as glycerophospholipids. In sphingophospholipids, the alcohol is sphingosine, which contains an amino group. Glycolipids, or glycosphingolipids, contain a fatty acid, sphingosine, and carbohydrate. Other complex lipids include sulfolipids and amino lipids. Lipoproteins may also be placed in this category.

Derived lipids are formed from the hydrolysis of both simple and complex lipids. Derived lipids include fatty acids, glycerol, steroids/sterols, including cholesterol, other alcohols, fatty aldehydes, ketone bodies, hydrocarbons, lipid-soluble vitamins and micronutrients, and hormones. Some derived lipids, such as free fatty acids and glycerol, act as precursor lipids in the formation of simple and complex lipids.

Acylglycerols are termed neutral lipids because they are uncharged. Cholesterol is termed a neutral lipid because it is uncharged. Cholesteryl esters are termed neutral lipids because they are uncharged.

## Fatty Acids Are Named After Corresponding Hydrocarbons

Fatty acids are named after the hydrocarbon with the same number and arrangement of carbon atoms, with -oic being substituted for the final -e in the Genevan system. Saturated fatty acids end in -anoic. Unsaturated fatty acids with double bonds end in -enoic. Octanoic acid is an example of a saturated fatty acid with the formula C8, derived from the hydrocarbon octane. Octadecenoic acid, also known as oleic acid, is an example of an unsaturated fatty acid with the formula C18, derived from the hydrocarbon octadecane.

Carbon atoms in fatty acids are numbered from the carboxyl carbon, which is carbon no. 1.

## Most Naturally Occurring Unsaturated Fatty Acids Have cis Double Bonds

The carbon chains of saturated fatty acids form a zigzag pattern when extended at low temperatures. At higher temperatures, some bonds rotate, causing chain shortening, which explains why biomembranes become thinner with increases in temperature. Since carbon-carbon double bonds do not allow rotation, a type of geometric isomerism occurs in unsaturated fatty acids, termed cis-trans isomerism, which depends on the orientation of atoms or groups around the axes of their double bonds. If the acyl chains are on the same side of the bond, it is cis-; if on opposite sides, it is trans-. The cis configuration causes a 120° bend in the molecule at the double bond, while the trans-configuration does not. Thus, oleic acid has a V shape, whereas its trans isomer elaidic acid remains straight (Figure 21–3). An increase in the number of cis double bonds in a fatty acid leads to a variety of possible spatial configurations of the molecule. Arachidonic acid, with four cis double bonds, is bent into a U shape (Figure 21–3). An increase in the number of cis double bonds in a fatty acid has profound significance for molecular packing in cell membranes and on the positions occupied by fatty acids in complex lipids such as phospholipids. Trans double bonds alter these spatial relationships. Although double bonds in naturally occurring unsaturated fatty acids are almost always in the cis configuration, trans-fatty acids are present in foods such as those derived from ruminant fat, caused by the action of microorganisms in the rumen, and industrial trans fats, which are a by-product of the saturation of fatty acids during the hydrogenation (hardening) of natural oils in the manufacture of margarine. Dietary trans-fatty acids, however, are now known to be detrimental to health, being associated with increased risk of diseases including cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, and cancer. For this reason, the content of industrial trans fats in foods is currently limited to a low level by law in many countries. A plan to eliminate these industrially generated fats completely from the world’s food supply was announced by the World Health Organization in 2018.

## Physical & Physiologic Properties of Fatty Acids Reflect Chain Length & Degree of Unsaturation

The melting points of even-numbered carbon fatty acids increase with chain length. The melting points of even-numbered carbon fatty acids decrease with unsaturation. A triacylglycerol containing three saturated fatty acids of 12 or more carbons is solid at body temperature. A triacylglycerol with polyunsaturated fatty acid residues is liquid to below 0 degrees Celsius. Natural acylglycerols contain a mixture of fatty acids tailored to suit their functional roles. Membrane lipids are more unsaturated than storage lipids. Membrane lipids must be fluid at all environmental temperatures. Lipids in tissues subject to cooling are more unsaturated. Cooling occurs during hibernation or in the extremities of animals.

## Carbons 1 & 3 of Glycerol Are Not Identical

Carbons 1 and 3 of glycerol are not identical when viewed in three dimensions.

The -sn (stereochemical numbering) system is used to unambiguously number the carbon atoms of glycerol.

Enzymes distinguish between the sn-1 and sn-3 positions and are specific for one or the other carbon.

Glycerol kinase phosphorylates glycerol on sn-3, not sn-1.

Phosphorylation by glycerol kinase results in glycerol-3-phosphate, not glycerol-1-phosphate.

# PHOSPHOLIPIDS ARE THE MAIN LIPID CONSTITUENTS OF MEMBRANES

Phospholipids are derivatives of phosphatidic acid, where a glycerol moiety is esterified to a phosphate group and two long-chain fatty acids, forming glycerophospholipids. Phosphatidic acid serves as an intermediate in the synthesis of triacylglycerols and glycerophospholipids. Phosphatidic acid is not present in large quantities in tissues. Sphingolipids, such as sphingomyelin, are also important components of membranes. Sphingomyelin has a phosphate esterified to sphingosine, a complex amino alcohol. Both glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids have two long-chain hydrocarbon tails that are crucial for forming the lipid bilayer in cell membranes. In glycerophospholipids, both tails are fatty acid chains, while in sphingolipids, one tail is a fatty acid and the other is part of the sphingosine molecule. ## Phosphatidylcholines (Lecithins) & Sphingomyelins Are Abundant in Cell Membranes

Glycerophospholipids containing choline, known as phosphatidylcholines or lecithins, are the most abundant phospholipids in cell membranes. Phosphatidylcholines represent a large proportion of the body's store of choline. Choline is important in nervous transmission as acetylcholine. Choline serves as a store of labile methyl groups. Dipalmitoyl lecithin is a major constituent of lung surfactant, preventing surface tension adherence. The absence of dipalmitoyl lecithin in premature infants causes respiratory distress syndrome. Most phospholipids have a saturated acyl radical in the sn-1 position and an unsaturated radical in the sn-2 position of glycerol.

Phosphatidylethanolamine, known as cephalin, is found in cell membranes. Phosphatidylserine is found in most tissues and cell membranes. Phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylserine differ from phosphatidylcholine by having ethanolamine or serine, respectively, in place of choline. Phosphatidylserine plays a role in apoptosis, which is programmed cell death.

Sphingomyelins are located in the outer leaflet of the cell membrane lipid bilayer. Sphingomyelins are particularly abundant in lipid rafts. Sphingomyelins are found in large quantities in the myelin sheath surrounding nerve fibers. Sphingomyelins play a role in cell signaling and apoptosis. Sphingomyelins do not contain glycerol. Hydrolysis of sphingomyelins yields a fatty acid, phosphoric acid, choline, and sphingosine. The combination of sphingosine and fatty acid is known as ceramide. Ceramide is also found in glycosphingolipids.

## Phosphatidylinositol Is a Precursor of Second Messengers

Inositol in phosphatidylinositol is present as the stereoisomer myoinositol.

Phosphorylated phosphatidylinositols, known as phosphoinositides, are minor components of cell membranes but play a crucial role in cell signaling and membrane trafficking.

Phosphoinositides can have 1, 2, or 3 phosphate groups attached to the inositol ring.

Diacylglycerol acts as an internal signal or second messenger.

Inositol trisphosphate acts as an internal signal or second messenger.

## Cardiolipin Is a Major Lipid of Mitochondrial Membranes

Phosphatidic acid is a precursor of phosphatidylglycerol. Phosphatidylglycerol gives rise to cardiolipin.

Cardiolipin is a phospholipid found only in mitochondria. Cardiolipin is essential for mitochondrial function.

Decreased cardiolipin levels cause mitochondrial dysfunction. Alterations in cardiolipin structure cause mitochondrial dysfunction. Alterations in cardiolipin metabolism cause mitochondrial dysfunction.

Mitochondrial dysfunction occurs in aging. Mitochondrial dysfunction occurs in pathologic conditions including heart failure. Mitochondrial dysfunction occurs in pathologic conditions including cancer. Mitochondrial dysfunction occurs in pathologic conditions including Barth syndrome.

Barth syndrome is a rare genetic disease. Barth syndrome causes cardioskeletal myopathy.

# GLYCOLIPIDS (GLYCOSPHINGOLIPIDS) ARE IMPORTANT IN NERVE TISSUES & IN THE CELL MEMBRANE

Glycolipids are lipids with an attached carbohydrate or carbohydrate chain. Glycolipids are widely distributed in every tissue of the body. Glycolipids are particularly abundant in nervous tissue such as the brain. Glycolipids occur particularly in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane. Glycolipids contribute to cell surface carbohydrates that form the glycocalyx.

The major glycolipids found in animal tissues are glycosphingolipids. Glycosphingolipids contain ceramide and one or more sugars. Galactosylceramide is a major glycosphingolipid of brain and other nervous tissue. Galactosylceramide is found in relatively low amounts elsewhere. Galactosylceramide contains a number of characteristic C24 fatty acids, for example, cerebronic acid.

Galactosylceramide can be converted to sulfogalactosylceramide (sulfatide). Sulfogalactosylceramide has a sulfo group attached to the O in the three position of galactose. Sulfogalactosylceramide is present in high amounts in myelin. Glucosylceramide resembles galactosylceramide, but the head group is glucose rather than galactose. Glucosylceramide is the predominant simple glycosphingolipid of extraneural tissues. Glucosylceramide occurs in the brain in small amounts.

Gangliosides are complex glycosphingolipids derived from glucosylceramide. Gangliosides contain one or more molecules of sialic acid. Neuraminic acid (NeuAc) is the principal sialic acid found in human tissues. Gangliosides are present in nervous tissues in high concentration. Gangliosides function in cell-cell recognition and communication. Gangliosides act as receptors for hormones and bacterial toxins such as cholera toxin. The simplest ganglioside found in tissues is GM3. GM3 contains ceramide, one molecule of glucose, one molecule of galactose, and one molecule of NeuAc. In the shorthand nomenclature used, G represents ganglioside; M is a monosialo-containing species; and 3 is a number assigned on the basis of chromatographic migration. GM1 is a more complex ganglioside derived from GM3. GM1 is of considerable biologic interest as it is known to be the receptor in human intestine for cholera toxin. Other gangliosides can contain anywhere from one to five molecules of sialic acid, giving rise to di-, trisialogangliosides, etc.

# STEROIDS PLAY MANY PHYSIOLOGICALLY IMPORTANT ROLES

Cholesterol is the precursor of a large number of important steroids including bile acids, adrenocortical hormones, sex hormones, vitamin D, and cardiac glycosides. Cholesterol has many essential roles in the body beyond its association with atherosclerosis and heart disease.

All steroids have a similar cyclic nucleus resembling phenanthrene, consisting of rings A, B, and C, with a cyclopentane ring (D) attached. The carbon positions on the steroid nucleus are numbered as shown in Figure 21--12. In structural formulas of steroids, a simple hexagonal ring denotes a completely saturated six-carbon ring with all valences satisfied by hydrogen bonds unless shown otherwise; it is not a benzene ring. All double bonds are shown as such.

Methyl groups occur typically at positions 10 and 13 and constitute carbon atoms 19 and 18. A side chain at position 17 is usual, as in cholesterol. If a compound has one or more hydroxyl groups and no carbonyl or carboxyl groups, it is a sterol, and the name terminates in -ol.

## Because of Asymmetry in the Steroid Molecule, Many Stereoisomers Are Possible

Each of the six-carbon rings of the steroid nucleus can exist in the 'chair' or 'boat' conformation. In naturally occurring steroids, virtually all the rings are in the 'chair' form, which is the more stable conformation. The junction between the A and B rings may be cis or trans in naturally occurring steroids. The junction between the B and C rings is trans, as is usually the C/D junction. # LIPID PEROXIDATION IS A SOURCE OF FREE RADICALS

Lipid peroxidation is responsible for the deterioration of foods, known as rancidity. Lipid peroxidation is responsible for damage to tissues in vivo. Lipid peroxidation damage includes cancer, inflammatory diseases, atherosclerosis, and aging. Free radicals are molecules with unpaired valence electrons. Free radicals are highly reactive. ROS are produced during peroxide formation from fatty acids with methylene-interrupted double bonds. Fatty acids with methylene-interrupted double bonds are typically found in naturally occurring polyunsaturated fatty acids. Lipid peroxidation is a chain reaction that can have devastating effects.

Termination of the chain reaction occurs when two radicals react to form a non-radical product, such as ROOR or RR. Antioxidants are used to control and reduce lipid peroxidation. Propyl gallate, butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA), and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) are examples of antioxidants used as food additives. Vitamin E (tocopherol) is a naturally occurring lipid-soluble antioxidant. Urate and vitamin C are water-soluble antioxidants.

Antioxidants are classified into two categories: preventive antioxidants and chain-breaking antioxidants. Preventive antioxidants reduce the rate of chain initiation. Chain-breaking antioxidants interfere with chain propagation. Preventive antioxidants include catalase and peroxidases like glutathione peroxidase, which react with ROOH. Selenium is an essential component of glutathione peroxidase. Selenium regulates the activity of glutathione peroxidase. Chelators of metal ions, such as ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA) and diethylenetriaminepentaacetate (DTPA), act as preventive antioxidants. Peroxidation in vivo is catalyzed by heme compounds and lipoxygenases found in platelets and leukocytes. Oxysterols are formed from cholesterol. Isoprostanes are formed from the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids like arachidonic acid. Oxysterols and isoprostanes are products of auto-oxidation or enzymic oxidation. Oxysterols and isoprostanes are used as markers of oxidative stress in humans. (Figure 21-17) illustrates that lipid peroxidation can be initiated by light. (Figure 21-17) illustrates that lipid peroxidation can be initiated by metal ions. Malondialdehyde is formed by fatty acids with three or more double bonds. Malondialdehyde is used as a measure of lipid peroxidation. Ethane is formed from the terminal carbons of ω3 fatty acids. Pentane is formed from the terminal carbons of ω6 fatty acids. Ethane and pentane are used as measures of lipid peroxidation.

## They Form Membranes, Micelles, Liposomes, & Emulsions

Lipids are generally insoluble in water due to their predominance of nonpolar groups. Fatty acids contain polar groups. Phospholipids contain polar groups. Sphingolipids contain polar groups. Bile salts contain polar groups. Cholesterol contains polar groups. Fatty acids, phospholipids, sphingolipids, bile salts, and cholesterol are amphipathic, meaning they have both hydrophobic and hydrophilic parts. At oil-water interfaces, amphipathic lipids orient themselves with polar groups in the water phase and nonpolar groups in the oil phase. A bilayer of amphipathic lipids forms the basic structure of biological membranes. When present at a critical concentration in an aqueous medium, these lipids form micelles, which are spherical particles with hydrophobic tails inside and hydrophilic heads facing the water. Liposomes are spheres of lipid bilayers that enclose part of the aqueous medium. Liposomes can be formed by sonicating an amphipathic lipid in an aqueous medium. Bile salts aggregate into micelles and form mixed micelles with fat digestion products, facilitating lipid absorption in the intestine. Liposomes have potential clinical uses, such as drug carriers in circulation when combined with tissue-specific antibodies, particularly in cancer therapy. Liposomes are used for gene transfer into vascular cells. Liposomes are used as carriers for topical and transdermal drug and cosmetic delivery. Emulsions are larger particles formed by nonpolar lipids in an aqueous medium. Emulsions are stabilized by emulsifying agents like amphipathic lipids, which form a surface layer separating the nonpolar material from the aqueous phase. Lipids have the common property of being relatively insoluble in water (hydrophobic) but soluble in nonpolar solvents. Amphipathic lipids also contain one or more polar groups, making them suitable as constituents of membranes at lipid-water interfaces.
Lipids of major physiologic significance include fatty acids and their esters, together with cholesterol and other steroids.
Long-chain fatty acids may be saturated, monounsaturated, or polyunsaturated, according to the number of double bonds present. Their fluidity decreases with chain length and increases according to degree of unsaturation.
Eicosanoids are formed from 20-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acids and make up an important group of physiologically and pharmacologically active compounds known as prostaglandins, thromboxanes, leukotrienes, and lipoxins.
The esters of glycerol are quantitatively the most significant lipids, represented by triacylglycerol (“fat”), a major constituent of some lipoprotein classes and the storage form of lipid in adipose tissue. Glycerophospholipids and sphingolipids are amphipathic lipids and have important roles—as major constituents of membranes and the outer layer of lipoproteins, as surfactant in the lung, as precursors of second messengers, and as constituents of nervous tissue.
Glycolipids are also important constituents of nervous tissue such as brain and the outer leaflet of the cell membrane, where they contribute to the carbohydrates on the cell surface.
Cholesterol, an amphipathic lipid, is an important component of membranes. It is the parent molecule from which all other steroids in the body, including major hormones such as the adrenocortical and sex hormones, D vitamins, and bile acids, are synthesized.
Peroxidation of lipids containing polyunsaturated fatty acids leads to generation of free radicals that damage tissues and cause disease.
