# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Fat must be transported between tissues and organs for utilization and storage. Nonpolar lipids, such as triacylglycerol and cholesteryl esters, are insoluble in water. Amphipathic lipids, such as phospholipids and cholesterol, are used to make water-miscible lipoproteins. Lipoproteins solve the problem of transporting lipids in aqueous blood plasma. Excess calories are ingested during the anabolic phase of the feeding cycle in humans. Negative caloric balance follows when the organism draws on its carbohydrate and fat stores. Lipoproteins transport lipids from the intestines as chylomicrons. The liver releases lipids as very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL). Most tissues use these lipids for oxidation. Adipose tissue stores these lipids. Lipid is mobilized from adipose tissue as free fatty acids (FFAs) bound to serum albumin. Abnormalities in lipoprotein metabolism cause hypo- or hyperlipoproteinemias. Diabetes mellitus is the most common condition affecting lipoprotein metabolism. Insulin deficiency in diabetes mellitus causes excessive mobilization of FFA. Insulin deficiency also leads to underutilization of chylomicrons and VLDL. Hypertriacylglycerolemia results from these metabolic changes. Inherited defects primarily cause most pathologic conditions affecting lipid transport. Some inherited defects cause hypercholesterolemia and premature atherosclerosis. Obesity, particularly abdominal obesity, is a risk factor for increased mortality. Obesity is a risk factor for hypertension. Obesity is a risk factor for type 2 diabetes mellitus. Obesity is a risk factor for hyperlipidemia. Obesity is a risk factor for hyperglycemia. Obesity is a risk factor for various endocrine dysfunctions.

# LIPIDS ARE TRANSPORTED IN THE PLASMA AS LIPOPROTEINS

Plasma lipids consist of triacylglycerols (16%). Plasma lipids consist of phospholipids (30%). Plasma lipids consist of cholesterol (14%). Plasma lipids consist of cholesteryl esters (36%). Plasma lipids consist of unesterified long-chain fatty acids (4%).

The density of a lipoprotein decreases as the proportion of lipid to protein increases.

Four major groups of lipoproteins are important physiologically: chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL, and HDL. Four major groups of lipoproteins are important in clinical diagnosis: chylomicrons, VLDL, LDL, and HDL.

Chylomicrons are derived from intestinal absorption of triacylglycerol. Chylomicrons are derived from intestinal absorption of other lipids.

VLDL is derived from the liver. VLDL is used for the export of triacylglycerol.

LDL is responsible for the transport of cholesterol in humans. LDL represents a final stage in the catabolism of VLDL.

HDL is involved in reverse cholesterol transport. HDL is involved in VLDL and chylomicron metabolism.

Triacylglycerol is the predominant lipid in chylomicrons. Triacylglycerol is the predominant lipid in VLDL.

Cholesterol is the predominant lipid in LDL. Phospholipid is the predominant lipid in HDL.

Lipoproteins may be classified according to their electrophoretic properties into α- (HDL), β- (LDL), and pre-β (VLDL)-lipoproteins.

The nonpolar lipid core of lipoproteins mainly consists of triacylglycerol and cholesteryl ester. The nonpolar core is surrounded by a single surface layer of amphipathic phospholipid and cholesterol molecules. Amphipathic phospholipid and cholesterol molecules are oriented so that their polar groups face outward to the aqueous medium. The structure of a plasma lipoprotein is illustrated in Figure 25-1.

## The Distribution of Apolipoproteins Characterizes the Lipoprotein

Each lipoprotein contains one or more apolipoproteins. Apolipoproteins are abbreviated as apo followed by letters such as A, B, C, etc. Some apolipoproteins are integral components of lipoproteins and cannot be removed, like apo B. Others are bound to the surface and can transfer to other lipoproteins, such as apolipoproteins C and E. The major apolipoproteins of HDL, or α-lipoprotein, are apo As. The main apolipoprotein of LDL, or β-lipoprotein, is apo B (B-100), which is also present in VLDL. Chylomicrons contain a truncated form of apo B-100 called apoB-48. Apo B-48 constitutes 48% of the full-sized apo B-100 molecule. Apo B-48 is synthesized in the intestine, while apo B-100 is synthesized in the liver. Apo B-100 is one of the longest single polypeptide chains known, with more than 4500 amino acids and a molecular mass of 540,000 Da. Apo B-48 is produced by introducing a stop signal into the mRNA transcript for apo B-100 through RNA editing. Apolipoproteins C-I, C-II, and C-III are smaller polypeptides with molecular masses ranging from 7000 to 9000 Da and are freely transferable between several lipoproteins. Apo E is also freely transferable and is found in VLDL, HDL, chylomicrons, and chylomicron remnants. Apo E accounts for 5 to 10% of total VLDL apolipoproteins in normal subjects.

Apolipoproteins perform several roles. Apolipoproteins can be structural components of lipoproteins, such as apo B. Apolipoproteins act as enzyme cofactors, for example, apo C-II for lipoprotein lipase and apo A-I for lecithin:cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT). Apolipoproteins can also be enzyme inhibitors, like apo A-II and apo C-III for lipoprotein lipase and apo C-I for cholesteryl ester transfer protein. Apolipoproteins function as ligands for lipoprotein receptors, for example, apo B-100 and apo E for the LDL receptor, and apo E for the LDL-receptor–related protein-1 (LRP-1). Apo A-I interacts with the HDL receptor. Apo A-IV is involved in chylomicron metabolism and may regulate satiety and glucose homeostasis, making it a potential therapeutic target for diabetes and obesity. Apo D is believed to be an important factor in human neurodegenerative disorders. Polymorphism of the gene for Apo-E is associated with the risk of Alzheimer disease; APOE4 increases risk, while APOE2 decreases risk compared to APOE3. This association is related to the effects on β-amyloid formation in the brain.

# FREE FATTY ACIDS ARE RAPIDLY METABOLIZED

Free fatty acids (FFAs), also known as nonesterified fatty acids (NEFAs) or unesterified fatty acids, originate in the plasma from the breakdown of triacylglycerol in adipose tissue. FFAs also arise from the action of lipoprotein lipase on plasma triacylglycerols. FFAs are found in combination with albumin, which is a very effective solubilizer. In the fully fed condition, FFA levels are low. In the starved state, FFA levels may rise up to 0.8 mEq/mL. In uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, FFA levels may rise to as much as 2 mEq/mL.

Tissues remove FFAs from the blood extremely rapidly. Tissues oxidize or esterify FFAs to form triacylglycerol. In starvation, esterified lipids from the circulation or in the tissues are also oxidized. Heart and skeletal muscle cells oxidize lipids, particularly in conditions of starvation. Heart and skeletal muscle cells store considerable amounts of lipid.

FFA uptake by tissues is directly related to the plasma-FFA concentration. The plasma-FFA concentration is determined by the rate of lipolysis in adipose tissue. At the plasma membrane, the fatty acid–albumin complex dissociates. Fatty acids bind to a membrane fatty acid transport protein. The fatty acid transport protein acts as a transmembrane cotransporter with Na+. On entering the cytosol, FFAs are bound by intracellular fatty acid–binding proteins. Intracellular fatty acid–binding proteins transport long-chain fatty acids within cells. The role of these proteins in intracellular transport is similar to that of serum albumin in extracellular transport of long-chain fatty acids.

# TRIACYLGLYCEROL IS TRANSPORTED IN CHYLOMICRONS & VLDL

Chylomicrons are found in chyle and are formed by the lymphatic system draining the intestine. Chylomicrons transport all dietary lipids into the circulation. Small quantities of VLDL are also present in chyle; however, most VLDL in the plasma originates from the liver. VLDL transport triacylglycerol from the liver to the extrahepatic tissues.

The formation mechanisms of chylomicrons by intestinal cells and VLDL by hepatic parenchymal cells are strikingly similar. The similarity may be due to the fact that, apart from the mammary gland, the intestine and liver are the only tissues that secrete particulate lipid. Apolipoprotein B is incorporated into particles with triacylglycerol, cholesterol, and phospholipids in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum. The particles are released from the cell by reverse pinocytosis. Chylomicrons enter the lymphatic system, while VLDL are secreted into the blood.

Newly secreted or 'nascent' chylomicrons and VLDL initially contain only a small amount of apolipoproteins C and E. Chylomicrons and VLDL acquire the full complement from HDL in the circulation (Figures 25–2and 25–3). In abetalipoproteinemia, a rare disease, lipoproteins containing apo B are not formed, leading to the accumulation of lipid droplets in the intestine and liver.

# CHYLOMICRONS & VLDL ARE RAPIDLY CATABOLIZED

The clearance of chylomicrons from the blood is rapid, with a half-time of disappearance under 1 h in humans.

Larger chylomicron particles are catabolized more quickly than smaller ones.

Fatty acids from chylomicron triacylglycerol are delivered mainly to adipose tissue, heart, and muscle, accounting for 80% of the delivery.

Approximately 20% of the fatty acids go to the liver.

The liver does not significantly metabolize native chylomicrons or VLDL.

Native chylomicrons and VLDL are converted to remnant lipoproteins while in circulation.

## Triacylglycerols of Chylomicrons & VLDL Are Hydrolyzed by Lipoprotein Lipase to Form Remnant Lipoproteins

Lipoprotein lipase is an enzyme located on the walls of blood capillaries. Lipoprotein lipase is anchored to the endothelium by negatively charged proteoglycan chains of heparan sulfate. Lipoprotein lipase is found in heart, adipose tissue, spleen, lung, renal medulla, aorta, diaphragm, and lactating mammary gland. Lipoprotein lipase is not active in adult liver. Lipoprotein lipase is not normally found in blood. Following injection of heparin, lipoprotein lipase is released into the circulation. Hepatic lipase is bound to the sinusoidal surface of liver cells. Hepatic lipase is also released by heparin. Hepatic lipase does not react readily with chylomicrons or VLDL. Hepatic lipase is involved in chylomicron remnant and HDL metabolism.

Phospholipids are required as cofactors for lipoprotein lipase activity. Apo C-II is required as a cofactor for lipoprotein lipase activity. Apo A-II acts as an inhibitor of lipoprotein lipase activity. Apo C-III acts as an inhibitor of lipoprotein lipase activity. Hydrolysis takes place while the lipoproteins are attached to the enzyme on the endothelium. Triacylglycerol is hydrolyzed progressively through a diacylglycerol to a monoacylglycerol and finally to FFA plus glycerol. Some of the released FFA return to the circulation, attached to albumin. The bulk of the released FFA is transported into the tissue. The delivery of fatty acids from triacylglycerol is redirected from adipose tissue to the heart in the starved state when the plasma triacylglycerol decreases. The delivery of fatty acids from triacylglycerol is redirected to the mammary gland during lactation for milk fat synthesis. The VLDL receptor plays an important part in the delivery of fatty acids from VLDL triacylglycerol to adipocytes by binding VLDL and bringing it into close contact with lipoprotein lipase. In adipose tissue, insulin enhances lipoprotein lipase synthesis in adipocytes. In adipose tissue, insulin enhances the translocation of lipoprotein lipase to the luminal surface of the capillary endothelium.

Reaction with lipoprotein lipase results in the loss of 70 to 90% of the triacylglycerol of chylomicrons. Reaction with lipoprotein lipase results in the loss of apo C, which returns to HDL. Reaction with lipoprotein lipase does not result in the loss of apo E, which is retained. The resulting chylomicron remnant is about half the diameter of the parent chylomicron. The resulting chylomicron remnant is relatively enriched in cholesterol and cholesteryl esters because of the loss of triacylglycerol. Similar changes occur to VLDL, with the formation of VLDL remnants, also called intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL).

Surplus surface constituents from the action of lipoprotein lipase on chylomicrons and VLDL are another source of preβ-HDL (Figure 25-4).

## The Liver Is Responsible for the Uptake of Remnant Lipoproteins

Chylomicron remnants are taken up by the liver through receptor-mediated endocytosis. The liver hydrolyzes and metabolizes the cholesteryl esters and triacylglycerols in chylomicron remnants. The uptake of chylomicron remnants is mediated by two apo E-dependent receptors: the LDL (apo B-100, E) receptor and LDL receptor-related protein-1 (LRP-1). Hepatic lipase facilitates the uptake of remnants by acting as a ligand. Hepatic lipase also hydrolyzes remnant triacylglycerol and phospholipid.

VLDL is converted to IDL, which can be taken up by the liver directly via the LDL (apo B-100, E) receptor. IDL can also be further metabolized to LDL in the circulation. Each lipoprotein particle, including VLDL, IDL, and LDL, contains only one molecule of apo B-100. Apo B-100 is conserved during the transformations from VLDL to LDL. Each LDL particle is derived from a single precursor VLDL particle. In humans, a relatively large proportion of IDL forms LDL. The relatively large proportion of IDL forming LDL accounts for the increased concentrations of LDL in humans compared with many other mammals.

# LDL IS METABOLIZED VIA THE LDL RECEPTOR

The liver and many extrahepatic tissues express the LDL receptor, which is specific for apo B-100 but not B-48. The LDL receptor also takes up lipoproteins rich in apo E. The LDL receptor is found in many tissues in the body, including the liver, arterial wall, ovary, testes, and adrenocortex. A positive correlation exists between the incidence of atherosclerosis and the plasma concentration of LDL cholesterol. The LDL receptor is defective in familial hypercholesterolemia. Familial hypercholesterolemia is a genetic condition in which blood LDL cholesterol levels are increased. Increased blood LDL cholesterol levels in familial hypercholesterolemia cause premature atherosclerosis.

# HDL TAKES PART IN BOTH LIPOPROTEIN TRIACYLGLYCEROL & CHOLESTEROL METABOLISM

HDL is synthesized and secreted from both the liver and the intestine.

Apo C and apo E are synthesized in the liver and transferred from liver HDL to intestinal HDL when the latter enters the plasma.

A major function of HDL is to act as a repository for the apo C and apo E required in the metabolism of chylomicrons and VLDL.

Nascent HDL consists of discoid phospholipid bilayers containing apo A and free cholesterol.

Nascent HDL is similar to the particles found in the plasma of patients with a deficiency of the plasma enzyme LCAT (lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase).

Nascent HDL is also similar to the particles found in the plasma of patients with obstructive jaundice.

LCAT and the LCAT activator apo A-I bind to the discoidal particles.

The surface phospholipid and free cholesterol are converted into cholesteryl esters and lysolecithin by LCAT.

The nonpolar cholesteryl esters move into the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer.

Lysolecithin is transferred to plasma albumin.

A nonpolar core is generated, forming a spherical, pseudomicellar HDL covered by a surface film of polar lipids and apolipoproteins.

A nonpolar core is generated, forming a spherical, pseudomicellar HDL covered by a surface film of polar lipids and apolipoproteins aids the removal of excess unesterified cholesterol from lipoproteins and tissues.

## Reverse Cholesterol Transport Is a Major Function of HDL

Reverse cholesterol transport involves the removal of cholesterol from tissues and its return to the liver for excretion. The class B scavenger receptor B1 (SR-B1) is an HDL receptor with a dual role in this process. In the liver, SR-B1 binds HDL via apo A-I. In the liver, cholesteryl ester is selectively delivered to the cells, while the particle itself, including apo A-I, is not taken up. In extrahepatic tissues, SR-B1 mediates the acceptance of cholesterol effluxed from the cells by HDL. HDL, generated from discoidal HDL by the action of LCAT, accepts cholesterol from the tissues via the SR-B1. The interchange of HDL and HDL is called the HDL cycle. Free apo A-I is released by these processes and forms preβ-HDL after associating with a minimum amount of phospholipid and cholesterol. Surplus apo A-I is destroyed in the kidney. A second important mechanism for reverse cholesterol transport involves the ATP-binding cassette transporters A1 (ABCA1) and G1 (ABCG1). ABCG1 mediates the transport of cholesterol from cells to HDL. ABCA1 preferentially promotes efflux to poorly lipidated particles such as preβ-HDL or apo A-1, which are then converted to HDL via discoidal HDL.

HDL concentrations vary reciprocally with plasma triacylglycerol concentrations. HDL concentrations vary directly with the activity of lipoprotein lipase. Surplus surface constituents, such as phospholipid and apo A-I, are released during hydrolysis of chylomicrons and VLDL. Surplus surface constituents contribute toward the formation of preβ-HDL and discoidal HDL. HDL concentrations are inversely related to the incidence of atherosclerosis. The inverse relationship possibly reflects the efficiency of reverse cholesterol transport. All plasma lipoproteins are interrelated components of one or more metabolic cycles responsible for the complex process of plasma lipid transport.

# THE LIVER PLAYS A CENTRAL ROLE IN LIPID TRANSPORT & METABOLISM

The liver facilitates the digestion and absorption of lipids by producing bile.

The liver actively synthesizes and oxidizes fatty acids.

The liver synthesizes triacylglycerols and phospholipids.

The liver converts fatty acids to ketone bodies through ketogenesis.

The liver synthesizes and metabolizes plasma lipoproteins.

## Hepatic VLDL Secretion Is Related to Dietary & Hormonal Status

VLDL assembly requires the synthesis of apo B-100 and a source of triacylglycerol. Apo B-100 is synthesized on polyribosomes and translocated to the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) as it is formed. As the protein enters the lumen, it is lipidated with phospholipid with the aid of the microsomal triacylglycerol transfer protein (MTP). MTP facilitates the transfer of triacylglycerol across the ER membrane. Apo B–containing VLDL2 (or precursor VLDL) particles are formed in the ER. Triacylglycerol is derived from lipolysis of cytosolic triacylglycerol lipid droplets followed by reesterification in a pathway requiring phospholipid derivatives and diacylglycerol acyl transferases. Triacylglycerol not used for VLDL1 formation is recycled to the cytosolic droplets. After assembly in the ER, VLDL2 are carried in coat protein II (COPII) vesicles to the golgi. In the golgi, VLDL2 fuse with triacylglycerol-rich lipid droplets to produce VLDL1. Phosphatidic acid is needed for the formation of VLDL1 and/or VLDL2. Phosphatidic acid is produced by the action of phospholipase D after activation by a small GTP-binding protein called ADP-ribosylation factor-1 (ARF-1). Although some VLDL2 particles may be secreted without fusion, most particles that leave the cell are in the form of VLDL1. Nascent VLDL acquire apolipoproteins C and E from HDL in the circulation to become mature VLDL (Figure 25-5).

Triacylglycerol for VLDL formation is synthesized from free fatty acids (FFA). The fatty acids used are derived from two possible sources: de novo synthesis within the liver from acetyl-CoA derived mainly from carbohydrate and uptake of FFA from the circulation. De novo synthesis is predominant in the well-fed condition, when fatty acid synthesis is high and the level of circulating FFAs is low. Triacylglycerol does not normally accumulate in the liver in these conditions; thus, it is transported from the liver in VLDL as rapidly as it is synthesized. FFAs from the circulation are the main source during starvation, the consumption of high-fat diets, or in diabetes mellitus, when hepatic lipogenesis is inhibited. The fed state rather than the starved state enhances both the synthesis of triacylglycerol and the secretion of VLDL by the liver. The consumption of diets high in carbohydrate, particularly if they contain sucrose or fructose, leads to high rates of lipogenesis and esterification of fatty acids. High levels of circulating FFA enhance the synthesis of triacylglycerol and the secretion of VLDL by the liver. Ingestion of ethanol enhances the synthesis of triacylglycerol and the secretion of VLDL by the liver. High concentrations of insulin and low concentrations of glucagon enhance fatty acid synthesis and esterification and inhibit their oxidation.

Insulin suppresses hepatic VLDL secretion by inhibiting both apo B-100 synthesis and the conversion of the smaller VLDL2 into VLDL1 by fusion with bulk triacylglycerol. Brefeldin A, an antibiotic, inhibits the action of ARF-1 and thus inhibits VLDL assembly in the liver. Tolbutamide, a sulfonylurea hypoglycemic drug, decreases the rate of triacylglycerol lipolysis and inhibits VLDL assembly in the liver. Dietary ω3 fatty acids decrease the rate of triacylglycerol lipolysis and inhibit VLDL assembly in the liver. Orotic acid, an intermediate in the synthesis of pyrimidines, decreases the rate of triacylglycerol lipolysis and inhibits VLDL assembly in the liver. A defect in the MTP gene lowers MTP levels and inhibits VLDL assembly in the liver.

## Imbalance in the Rate of Triacylglycerol Formation & Export Causes Fatty Liver

Fatty liver occurs when there is extensive accumulation of triacylglycerol in the liver. Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common liver disorder worldwide. Chronic lipid accumulation in the liver can lead to inflammatory and fibrotic changes. Inflammatory and fibrotic changes can result in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH). NASH can progress to liver diseases including cirrhosis, hepatocarcinoma, and liver failure. Fatty livers fall into two main categories. The first type of fatty liver is associated with raised levels of plasma free fatty acids (FFA). Raised levels of plasma free fatty acids result from mobilization of fat from adipose tissue. Raised levels of plasma free fatty acids can also result from hydrolysis of lipoprotein triacylglycerol by lipoprotein lipase in extrahepatic tissues. The production of VLDL does not keep pace with the increasing influx and esterification of free fatty acids. The production of VLDL does not keep pace with the increasing influx and esterification of free fatty acids allows triacylglycerol to accumulate. This accumulation causes a fatty liver during starvation and the feeding of high-fat diets. The ability to secrete VLDL may also be impaired, for example, in starvation. In uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, fatty infiltration is severe enough to cause visible pallor and enlargement of the liver. In uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, fatty infiltration can possibly lead to liver dysfunction. In twin lamb disease, fatty infiltration is severe enough to cause visible pallor and enlargement of the liver. In twin lamb disease, fatty infiltration can possibly lead to liver dysfunction. In ketosis in cattle, fatty infiltration is severe enough to cause visible pallor and enlargement of the liver. In ketosis in cattle, fatty infiltration can possibly lead to liver dysfunction. The second type of fatty liver is usually due to a metabolic block in the production of plasma lipoproteins. This block allows triacylglycerol to accumulate. The lesion may be due to a block in the synthesis of apolipoprotein. The lesion may be due to an increase in the degradation of apolipoprotein. The lesion may be due to a failure in provision of phospholipids for VLDL synthesis. The lesion may be due to a failure in the secretory mechanism. A deficiency of choline causes one type of fatty liver studied extensively in rats. Choline is called a lipotropic factor. Orotic acid causes fatty liver by interfering with glycosylation of VLDL, thus inhibiting its release. Orotic acid may also impair the recruitment of triacylglycerol to the particles. A deficiency of vitamin E enhances the hepatic necrosis of the choline deficiency type of fatty liver. Added vitamin E or a source of selenium has a protective effect by combating lipid peroxidation. Protein deficiency can cause fatty infiltration of the liver. Essential fatty acid deficiency can cause fatty infiltration of the liver. Vitamin deficiencies, such as linoleic acid, pyridoxine, and pantothenic acid, can cause fatty infiltration of the liver.

## Ethanol Also Causes Fatty Liver

Alcoholic fatty liver is the first stage in alcoholic liver disease (ALD). Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) is caused by alcoholism. Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) ultimately leads to cirrhosis. Fat accumulation in the liver is caused by impaired fatty acid oxidation. Fat accumulation in the liver is caused by increased lipogenesis. Impaired fatty acid oxidation and increased lipogenesis are due to changes in the [NADH]/[NAD+] redox potential in the liver. Changes in the [NADH]/[NAD+] redox potential are caused by the oxidation of ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase. # ADIPOSE TISSUE IS THE MAIN STORE OF TRIACYLGLYCEROL IN THE BODY

Triacylglycerols are stored in adipose tissue in large lipid droplets. Triacylglycerols undergo lipolysis, which is the hydrolysis of these molecules. Lipolysis and reesterification are entirely different pathways. Lipolysis and reesterification involve different reactants and enzymes. Lipolysis and reesterification are regulated separately by nutritional, metabolic, and hormonal factors. The balance between lipolysis and reesterification determines the magnitude of the FFA pool in adipose tissue. The level of FFA circulating in the plasma is determined by the magnitude of the FFA pool in adipose tissue. The factors in adipose tissue that regulate the outflow of FFA influence the metabolism of other tissues. Adipose tissue influences the liver and muscle by regulating the outflow of FFA. Adipose tissue secretes hormones such as leptin and adiponectin, known as adipokines. Adipose tissue is recognized as an endocrine organ. Leptin regulates energy homeostasis by stimulating energy use. Leptin regulates energy homeostasis by limiting food intake. If leptin is lacking, food intake may be uncontrolled. A lack of leptin can cause obesity. Adiponectin modulates glucose and lipid metabolism in muscle and liver. Adiponectin enhances the sensitivity of tissues to insulin.

## The Provision of Glycerol-3-Phosphate Regulates Esterification: Lipolysis Is Controlled by Hormone-Sensitive Lipase

Triacylglycerol is synthesized from acyl-CoA and glycerol-3-phosphate. Glycerol kinase is not expressed in adipose tissue, so glycerol cannot be used for the provision of glycerol-3-phosphate. Glycerol-3-phosphate must be supplied from glucose via glycolysis (Figure 25-6).

Triacylglycerol undergoes hydrolysis by a hormone-sensitive lipase to form free fatty acids (FFA) and glycerol. Hormone-sensitive lipase is distinct from lipoprotein lipase, which catalyzes lipoprotein triacylglycerol hydrolysis before its uptake into extrahepatic tissues. Glycerol enters the blood and is taken up and transported to tissues such as the liver and kidney, which possess an active glycerol kinase. Free fatty acids formed by lipolysis can be reconverted in adipose tissue to acyl-CoA by acyl-CoA synthetase. Acyl-CoA can be reesterified with glycerol-3-phosphate to form triacylglycerol. There is a continuous cycle of lipolysis and reesterification within adipose tissue (Figure 25-6).

When the rate of reesterification is not sufficient to match the rate of lipolysis, free fatty acids accumulate and diffuse into the plasma. Free fatty acids bind to albumin and raise the concentration of plasma-free fatty acids. Hormone-sensitive lipase is activated by ACTH, TSH, glucagon, epinephrine, and norepinephrine.

## Increased Glucose Metabolism Reduces the Output of FFA

When glucose utilization by adipose tissue is increased, the outflow of free fatty acids (FFA) decreases.

The release of glycerol continues even when glucose utilization increases, indicating that the effect of glucose is not due to a reduction in the rate of lipolysis.

The effect of increased glucose utilization is due to the increased provision of glycerol-3-phosphate, which enhances the esterification of FFA.

Glucose can also be oxidized in the pentose phosphate pathway in adipose tissue.

Glucose can be converted to long-chain fatty acids via acyl-CoA in adipose tissue.

Glucose can form acylglycerol via glycerol-3-phosphate in adipose tissue.

As total glucose utilization decreases, a greater proportion of glucose is directed to the production of glycerol-3-phosphate for the esterification of acyl-CoA to form triacylglycerol.

The formation of triacylglycerol helps to minimize the efflux of FFA.

## Adipose Tissue Lipolysis Is Inhibited by Insulin

Insulin inhibits the release of free fatty acids (FFA) from adipose tissue. Insulin causes a decrease in circulating plasma FFA. Insulin enhances lipogenesis and the synthesis of acylglycerol. The effects of insulin are dependent on the presence of glucose. Insulin enhances the uptake of glucose into adipose cells through the GLUT 4 transporter. Insulin increases the activity of the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase. Insulin increases the activity of the enzyme acetyl-CoA carboxylase. Insulin increases the activity of the enzyme glycerol phosphate acyltransferase. The enzymes are regulated by phosphorylation-dephosphorylation mechanisms. Insulin inhibits the activity of hormone-sensitive lipase in adipose tissue. The inhibition of hormone-sensitive lipase by insulin reduces the release of free fatty acids. The inhibition of hormone-sensitive lipase by insulin reduces the release of glycerol. Adipose tissue is more sensitive to insulin than many other tissues. Adipose tissue is a major site of insulin action in vivo.

## Several Hormones Promote Lipolysis

Epinephrine and norepinephrine accelerate the release of free fatty acids (FFA) from adipose tissue by increasing the rate of lipolysis of the triacylglycerol stores. Glucagon, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and growth hormone (GH) also promote lipolysis. Many of these hormones activate hormone-sensitive lipase. Glucocorticoids and thyroid hormones are required for optimal lipolytic processes, acting in a facilitatory or permissive capacity with respect to other lipolytic endocrine factors. Catecholamines, such as epinephrine and norepinephrine, promote lipolysis by stimulating the activity of adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cAMP. cAMP activates cAMP-dependent protein kinase, which in turn activates hormone-sensitive lipase. Methylxanthines, such as caffeine and theophylline, inhibit the enzyme cyclic 3', 5'-nucleotide phosphodiesterase, which degrades cAMP to 5'-AMP. Insulin antagonizes the effect of the lipolytic hormones by inhibiting the synthesis of cAMP at the adenylyl cyclase site, acting through a G protein. Insulin also stimulates phosphodiesterase and lipase phosphatase, which inactivates hormone-sensitive lipase. The sympathetic nervous system, through the release of norepinephrine in adipose tissue, plays a central role in the mobilization of FFA. Denervation of adipose tissue or ganglionic blockade can reduce or abolish increased lipolysis caused by various factors. The effect of growth hormone in promoting lipolysis depends on the synthesis of proteins involved in the formation of cAMP. Glucocorticoids promote lipolysis via synthesis of new lipase protein by a cAMP-independent pathway, which may be inhibited by insulin. Glucocorticoids also promote transcription of genes involved in the cAMP signal cascade. The pituitary gland and the adrenal cortex enhance fat mobilization. Lipolysis is more sensitive to changes in insulin concentration than glucose utilization and esterification. Nicotinic acid and prostaglandin E also act as antilipolytic agents by inhibiting the synthesis of cAMP at the adenylyl cyclase site. The stimulatory effects of epinephrine and norepinephrine on adenylate cyclase are promoted by β-adrenergic blockers and inhibited by thyroid hormone. Adenosine inhibits adenylyl cyclase, and high concentrations of FFA inhibit the enzyme and hormone-sensitive lipase. Lipase phosphatase inactivates hormone-sensitive lipase. The removal of cAMP by phosphodiesterase switches off the lipolytic stimulus. (Figure 25-7)

## Perilipin Regulates the Balance Between Triacylglycerol Storage & Lipolysis in Adipocytes

Perilipin is a protein involved in the formation of lipid droplets in adipocytes. Perilipin inhibits lipolysis in basal conditions by preventing access of the lipase enzymes to the stored triacylglycerols. Hormones that promote triacylglycerol degradation stimulate perilipin. Stimulated perilipin becomes phosphorylated. Phosphorylation changes the conformation of perilipin. The changed conformation of perilipin exposes the lipid droplet surface to hormone-sensitive lipase. Exposing the lipid droplet surface to hormone-sensitive lipase promotes lipolysis. Perilipin enables the storage and breakdown of triacylglycerol to be coordinated according to the metabolic needs of the body.

# BROWN ADIPOSE TISSUE PROMOTES THERMOGENESIS

Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is a specialized form of adipose tissue involved in metabolism and thermogenesis. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is extremely active in some species, such as during arousal from hibernation. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is active in animals exposed to cold for nonshivering thermogenesis. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is active in heat production in newborns. Though not a prominent tissue in humans, brown adipose tissue (BAT) is present in normal individuals. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) is characterized by a well-developed blood supply. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) has a high content of mitochondria and cytochromes. Brown adipose tissue (BAT) has low activity of ATP synthase. The metabolic emphasis in brown adipose tissue (BAT) is on the oxidation of both glucose and fatty acids. Norepinephrine is important in increasing lipolysis in brown adipose tissue (BAT). Norepinephrine increases the synthesis of lipoprotein lipase in brown adipose tissue (BAT). Lipoprotein lipase enhances the utilization of triacylglycerol-rich lipoproteins from the circulation in brown adipose tissue (BAT). Oxidation and phosphorylation are not coupled in the mitochondria of brown adipose tissue (BAT) because of the presence of thermogenic uncoupling protein, thermogenin (also called uncoupling protein 1 [UCP1]). The phosphorylation that does occur in brown adipose tissue (BAT) is at the substrate level, for example, at the succinate thiokinase step and in glycolysis. Oxidation in brown adipose tissue (BAT) produces much heat. Little free energy is trapped in ATP in brown adipose tissue (BAT). Since nonpolar lipids are insoluble in water, for transport between the tissues in the aqueous blood plasma they are combined with amphipathic lipids and proteins to make water-miscible lipoproteins.
Four major groups of lipoproteins are recognized. Chylomicrons transport lipids resulting from digestion and absorption. VLDLs transport triacylglycerol from the liver. LDLs deliver cholesterol to the tissues, and HDLs remove cholesterol from the tissues and return it to the liver for excretion in the process known as reverse cholesterol transport.
Chylomicrons and VLDL are metabolized by hydrolysis of their triacylglycerol, leaving lipoprotein remnants in the circulation. These are taken up by liver, but some of the remnants (IDL) resulting from VLDL form LDL are taken up by extrahepatic tissues as well as the liver via the LDL receptor.
Apolipoproteins constitute the protein moiety of lipoproteins. They act as enzyme activators (eg, apo C-II and apo A-I) or as ligands for cell receptors (eg, apo A-I, apo E, and apo B-100).
Triacylglycerol is the main storage lipid in adipose tissue. Upon mobilization, FFA and glycerol are released. FFAs are an important fuel source.
Brown adipose tissue is the site of “nonshivering thermogenesis.” It is found in hibernating and newborn animals and is present in adult humans. Thermogenesis results from the presence of UCP1 (thermogenin) in the inner mitochondrial membrane.
