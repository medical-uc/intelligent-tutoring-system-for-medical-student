# Overview

Understand that biologic membranes are mainly composed of a lipid bilayer and associated proteins and glycoproteins. The major lipids are phospholipids, cholesterol, and glycosphingolipids.
Appreciate that membranes are asymmetric, dynamic structures that contain a mixture of integral and peripheral proteins.
Describe the key features of the fluid mosaic model of membrane structure.
Understand the concepts of passive diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport, endocytosis, and exocytosis.
Recognize that transporters, ion channels, the Na+ - K+ -ATPase, receptors, and gap junctions are important participants in membrane function.
Be aware that a variety of disorders result from abnormalities of membrane structure and function, including familial hypercholesterolemia, cystic fibrosis, hereditary spherocytosis, among others.

# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Membranes are dynamic, highly fluid structures consisting of a lipid bilayer and a multitude of associated proteins. Plasma membranes form closed compartments around the cytoplasm to define cell boundaries. The plasma membrane has selective permeabilities and acts as a barrier. The plasma membrane maintains differences in composition between the inside and outside of the cell. Selective membrane molecular permeability is generated through the action of specific transporters and ion channels. The plasma membrane exchanges material with the extracellular environment by exocytosis and endocytosis. There are special areas of membrane structure—gap junctions—through which adjacent cells may communicate by exchanging material. The plasma membrane plays key roles in cell–cell interactions and in transmembrane signaling.

Membranes form specialized compartments within the cell. Intracellular membranes help shape many of the morphologically distinguishable cellular structures (organelles). Intracellular membranes shape mitochondria. Intracellular membranes shape endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Intracellular membranes shape Golgi. Intracellular membranes shape secretory granules. Intracellular membranes shape lysosomes. Intracellular membranes shape the nucleus. Membranes localize enzymes. Membranes function as integral elements in excitation-response coupling. Membranes provide sites of energy transduction. Membranes are sites of energy transduction in photosynthesis in plants (chloroplasts). Membranes are sites of energy transduction in oxidative phosphorylation (mitochondria).

Changes in membrane components can affect water balance. Changes in membrane components can affect ion flux. Specific deficiencies or alterations of certain membrane components lead to a variety of diseases. Mutations in genes encoding membrane proteins cause specific deficiencies or alterations of certain membrane components. Normal cellular function depends on normal membranes.

Water makes up about 60% of the lean body mass of the human body.

Water is distributed in two large compartments within the human body.

Intracellular Fluid (ICF) constitutes two-thirds of total body water.

Intracellular Fluid (ICF) provides a specialized environment for the cell to make, store, and utilize energy.

Intracellular Fluid (ICF) provides a specialized environment for the cell to repair itself.

## Extracellular Fluid (ECF)

The extracellular fluid (ECF) contains about one-third of total body water.

The ECF is distributed between the plasma and interstitial compartments.

The ECF is a bidirectional delivery system.

The ECF supplies cells with oxygen.

The ECF delivers various ions and trace minerals to cells.

The ECF provides regulatory molecules like hormones to cells.

Regulatory molecules in the ECF coordinate the functions of widely separated cells.

The ECF removes waste products from cells.

The ECF eliminates toxic or detoxified materials from the cellular environment.

Membranes are sheet-like structures consisting of an asymmetric lipid bilayer with distinct inner and outer surfaces or leaflets. Membranes are protein-studded and form spontaneously in aqueous environments due to the amphipathic nature of lipids and proteins. ## Phospholipids

Phosphoglycerides are the more common phospholipid class present in membranes. Phosphoglycerides consist of a glycerol-phosphate backbone. Two fatty acids are attached to the glycerol-phosphate backbone in ester linkages. An alcohol is also attached to the glycerol-phosphate backbone. The fatty acid constituents are usually even-numbered carbon molecules. The fatty acids most commonly contain 16 or 18 carbons. The fatty acids are unbranched. The fatty acids can be saturated or unsaturated with one or more double bonds. Phosphatidic acid is the simplest phosphoglyceride. Phosphatidic acid is a 1,2-diacylglycerol 3-phosphate. Phosphatidic acid is a key intermediate in the formation of other phosphoglycerides. In most phosphoglycerides present in membranes, the 3-phosphate is esterified to an alcohol. The alcohol can be choline, ethanolamine, glycerol, inositol, or serine. Phosphatidylcholine is generally the major phosphoglyceride by mass in the membranes of human cells.

Sphingomyelin comprises the second major class of phospholipids. Sphingomyelin contains a sphingosine rather than a glycerol backbone. A fatty acid is attached by an amide linkage to the amino group of sphingosine, forming ceramide. When the primary hydroxyl group of sphingosine is esterified to phosphorylcholine, sphingomyelin is formed. Sphingomyelin is prominent in myelin sheaths.

## Glycosphingolipids

Glycosphingolipids (GSLs) are sugar-containing lipids built on a backbone of ceramide.

GSLs include galactosyl-ceramides and glucosyl-ceramides, known as cerebrosides.

GSLs also include gangliosides.

GSLs are mainly located in the plasma membranes of cells.

In the plasma membranes, GSLs display their sugar components to the exterior of the cell.

## Sterols

Cholesterol is the most common sterol in the membranes of animal cells. The majority of cholesterol resides within plasma membranes. Smaller amounts of cholesterol are found within mitochondrial, Golgi complex, and nuclear membranes. Cholesterol intercalates among the phospholipids of the membrane. The hydrophilic hydroxyl group of cholesterol is at the aqueous interface. The remainder of the cholesterol molecule is buried within the lipid bilayer leaflet. Cholesterol is not present in plants from a nutritional viewpoint. Lipids can be separated from one another and quantified by techniques such as column chromatography. Lipids can be separated from one another and quantified by thin-layer chromatography. Lipids can be separated from one another and quantified by gas-liquid chromatography. The structures of lipids can be established by mass spectrometry. The structures of lipids can be established by other techniques.

## Membrane Lipids Form Bilayers

Phospholipids are amphipathic molecules with hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions. In water, phospholipids spontaneously form micelles to satisfy their solubility requirements. Within micelles, the hydrophobic regions of phospholipids are shielded from water. Within micelles, the hydrophilic polar groups are exposed to the aqueous environment. Micelles are typically small, around 200 nm. The size of micelles limits their potential to form membranes. Detergents often form micelles. (Figure 40–4and Figure 21–18)

Phospholipids can also form bimolecular lipid bilayers. Bimolecular lipid bilayers are key structures in biological membranes. Bilayers are sheets where the hydrophobic regions of phospholipids are sequestered from water. Bilayers are sheets where the hydrophilic regions of phospholipids are exposed to water. Bilayers can fold to form enclosed vesicles with no edges. The lipid bilayer is impermeable to most water-soluble molecules. The impermeability of the lipid bilayer is due to the hydrophobic core. The self-assembly of lipid bilayers is driven by the hydrophobic effect. The hydrophobic effect increases the entropy of surrounding solvent molecules by releasing immobilized water. ## Membrane Proteins Are Associated With the Lipid Bilayer

Membrane phospholipids act as a solvent for membrane proteins. The alpha-helical structure of proteins minimizes the hydrophilic character of the peptide bonds. Proteins can be amphipathic. Proteins form an integral part of the membrane. Hydrophilic regions of membrane proteins protrude at the inside and outside faces of the membrane. Hydrophobic regions of membrane proteins traverse the hydrophobic core of the bilayer. Hydrophobic regions of membrane proteins that traverse membranes contain substantial numbers of hydrophobic amino acids. Hydrophobic regions of membrane proteins almost invariably have a high alpha-helical content. A stretch of approximately 20 amino acids in an alpha-helical configuration will span the lipid bilayer for most membranes.

It is possible to calculate whether a particular sequence of amino acids in a protein is consistent with a transmembrane location. Hydrophobic amino acids have positive values in a table listing hydrophobicities. Polar amino acids have negative values in the table. The total free energy values for transferring successive sequences of 20 amino acids in the protein are plotted. This yields a so-called hydropathy plot. Values of over 20 kcal mol^{-1} are consistent with a hydrophobic sequence being a transmembrane segment.

Some proteins are anchored to one leaflet of the bilayer by covalent linkages to certain lipids. Protein lipidation is termed protein lipidation. Lipidation can occur at protein termini, either N- or C-. Lipidation can occur internally. Common protein lipidation events include C-terminal protein isoprenylation, cholesterylation, and glycophosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchoring. N-terminal protein myristoylation is common. Internal cysteine S-prenylation is common. Internal cysteine S-acylation is common. Lipidation occurs on a specific subset of proteins. Lipidation typically plays key roles in their biology.

## Different Membranes Have Different Protein Compositions

The sarcoplasmic reticulum of muscle cells contains less than a dozen very abundant proteins. The plasma membranes contain hundreds of different proteins. Proteins are the major functional molecules in membranes. Proteins in membranes include enzymes, pumps and transporters, channels, structural components, antigens, and receptors for various molecules. Each type of membrane has a different complement of proteins. There is no such thing as a typical membrane structure. The complexity of membranes allows investigators to define different structural and functional categories of intracellular lipid-bounded compartments based on protein and lipid composition.

## Membranes Are Dynamic Structures

Membranes and their components are dynamic structures. Membrane lipids and proteins undergo turnover. Different lipids have different turnover rates. The turnover rates of individual species of membrane proteins may vary widely. In some instances, the membrane itself can turn over more rapidly than any of its constituents. The membrane itself can turn over more rapidly than any of its constituents is discussed in more detail in the section on endocytosis.

Lipids and certain proteins exhibit lateral diffusion in the plane of their membranes. Many nonmobile proteins do not exhibit lateral diffusion. Nonmobile proteins are anchored to the underlying actin cytoskeleton. The transverse movement of lipids across the membrane, known as flip-flop, is extremely slow. Flip-flop does not occur at an appreciable rate in the case of membrane proteins.

## Membranes Are Asymmetric Structures

Proteins have unique orientations in membranes. The outside surfaces of membranes are different from the inside surfaces. The carbohydrates attached to membrane proteins are located externally. The external location of carbohydrates contributes to inside-outside asymmetry. Specific proteins are located exclusively on the outsides of membranes. Specific proteins are located exclusively on the insides of membranes.

Regional heterogeneities in membranes exist at the villous borders of mucosal cells, which are almost macroscopically visible. Gap junctions occupy smaller regions of the membrane, generating smaller local asymmetries. Tight junctions occupy smaller regions of the membrane, generating smaller local asymmetries. Synapses occupy smaller regions of the membrane, generating smaller local asymmetries.

There is inside-outside asymmetry of the phospholipids. Phosphatidylcholine is located mainly in the outer leaflet. Sphingomyelin is located mainly in the outer leaflet. Phosphatidylserine is preferentially located in the inner leaflet. Phosphatidylethanolamine is preferentially located in the inner leaflet. Limited transverse mobility of membrane phospholipids is necessary for lipid asymmetry. In synthetic bilayers, phospholipids exhibit an extraordinarily slow rate of flip-flop. The half-life of asymmetry in synthetic bilayers is on the order of several weeks.

The mechanisms involved in lipid asymmetry are not well understood. The enzymes involved in phospholipid synthesis are located on the cytoplasmic side of microsomal membrane vesicles. Translocases, known as flippases, transfer certain phospholipids, such as phosphatidylcholine, from the inner to the outer leaflet. Specific proteins that bind individual phospholipids are present in the two leaflets. Specific proteins contribute to the asymmetric distribution of lipid molecules. Phospholipid exchange proteins recognize certain phospholipids. Phospholipid exchange proteins transfer phospholipids between membranes, such as from the ER to mitochondrial and peroxisomal membranes.

The entry of lipids into membranes has not been studied as intensively as the entry of proteins. Many membrane lipids are synthesized in the ER. Lipids are transported from the ER in vesicles. Vesicles transfer the contained lipids to the recipient membrane. Lipids can also enter via direct contact of one membrane with another. Direct contact of membranes is facilitated by specific proteins. Transport via phospholipid exchange proteins is another pathway. Phospholipid exchange proteins exchange lipids without causing net transfer.

GSLs exhibit asymmetry with their sugar moieties protruding outward from the plasma membrane. GSLs are absent from the inner face of the plasma membrane. Glycoproteins exhibit asymmetry with their sugar moieties protruding outward from the plasma membrane. Glycoproteins are absent from the inner face of the plasma membrane.

## Membranes Contain Integral & Peripheral Proteins

Membrane proteins can be classified into two types: integral and peripheral. Most membrane proteins are integral. Integral proteins interact extensively with phospholipids. Integral proteins require detergents for solubilization. Integral proteins are usually globular and amphipathic. Integral proteins consist of two hydrophilic ends separated by a hydrophobic region that traverses the hydrophobic core of the bilayer. Some integral membrane proteins, such as transporter molecules, ion channels, various receptors, and G proteins, span the bilayer many times. Other simple membrane proteins, like glycophorin A, span the membrane only once. Integral proteins are asymmetrically distributed across the membrane bilayer. The asymmetric orientation of integral proteins is established during their insertion into the lipid bilayer in the ER. The insertion of proteins into membranes and membrane assembly are discussed in Chapter 48.

Peripheral proteins do not interact directly with the hydrophobic cores of phospholipids in the bilayer. Peripheral proteins do not require detergents for their release. Peripheral proteins are bound to the hydrophilic regions of specific integral proteins and the head groups of phospholipids. Peripheral proteins can be released by treatment with salt solutions of high ionic strength. Ankyrin, a peripheral protein, is bound to the inner aspect of the integral protein 'band 3' in the erythrocyte membrane. Spectrin, a cytoskeletal structure within the erythrocyte, is bound to ankyrin. Spectrin plays a crucial role in maintaining the biconcave shape of the erythrocyte.

# ARTIFICIAL MEMBRANES BOTH ALLOW INVESTIGATORS TO MODEL MEMBRANE FUNCTION AND PROVIDE METHODS FOR THE EFFICIENT FORMATION OF LIPID NANOPARTICLES THAT ARE POTENT DRUG DELIVERY SYSTEMS

Artificial membrane systems can be prepared using appropriate techniques. Artificial membrane systems typically consist of mixtures of one or more phospholipids. The phospholipids can be of natural or synthetic origin. Mild sonication is used to induce the formation of spherical vesicles in these systems. The lipids in these vesicles form a bilayer. The structures created by the lipids are known as liposomes. Liposomes are the first therapeutic nanoparticles.

The lipid content of artificial membranes can be varied. Varying the lipid content allows for the systematic examination of the effects of different lipid compositions on certain functions. Purified membrane proteins or enzymes can be incorporated into these vesicles. Incorporating purified membrane proteins or enzymes allows for assessing the factors required for reconstituting their function. The environment of these systems can be rigidly controlled and systematically varied. Ion concentrations and ligands can be varied in these systems. Liposomes can be formed to entrap certain compounds within the vesicle. Liposomes can entrap drugs and isolated genes within the vesicle. Liposomes allow for the delivery of therapeutic 'genetic payloads' to specific cells, tissues, and organs. The 'protected environment' of liposomes makes DNA less sensitive to attack by hydrolytic enzymes. The 'protected environment' of liposomes makes RNA less sensitive to attack by hydrolytic enzymes. The 'protected environment' of liposomes makes proteins less sensitive to attack by hydrolytic enzymes. The biomedical implications of these approaches include gene therapy and messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccines, such as anti-COVID vaccines.

# THE FLUID MOSAIC MODEL OF MEMBRANE STRUCTURE

The fluid mosaic model of membrane structure was proposed in 1972 by Singer and Nicolson. The model likens integral membrane proteins to 'icebergs' floating in a sea of fluid phospholipid molecules. Early evidence for the model came from observations that fluorescently labeled integral membrane proteins rapidly and randomly redistributed within the plasma membrane of hybrid cells formed by the fusion of two different parent cells, one labeled and one not. Phospholipids undergo even more rapid lateral diffusion and redistribution within the plane of the membrane. One molecule of phospholipid can move several micrometers per second within the plane of the membrane.

The fluidity of membranes is largely dependent on the lipid composition of the membrane. In a lipid bilayer, the hydrophobic chains of fatty acids can be highly aligned. Highly aligned hydrophobic chains of fatty acids provide a stiff structure. As temperature increases, the hydrophobic side chains transition from an ordered state to a disordered one. Unsaturated bonds in the cis configuration increase membrane fluidity by decreasing the compactness of side chain packing without reducing hydrophobicity. Phospholipids in cellular membranes generally contain at least one unsaturated fatty acid with at least one cis double bond.

Cholesterol modifies the fluidity of membranes.

Membrane fluidity significantly affects its functions. Increased membrane fluidity increases permeability to water and small hydrophilic molecules. The lateral mobility of integral proteins increases with increased membrane fluidity. Changes in lipid fluidity have little effect on the activity of proteins with active sites in hydrophilic regions. Changes in fluidity can significantly alter transport rates for proteins that span the membrane. The insulin receptor is an example where increased fluidity enhances its ability to bind insulin. At normal body temperature (37°C), the lipid bilayer is in a fluid state. Bacteria can modify their membrane lipid composition to adapt to temperature changes.

## Lipid Rafts, Caveolae, & Tight Junctions Are Specialized Features of Plasma Membranes

Lipid rafts are specialized areas of the exoplasmic (outer) leaflet of the lipid bilayer enriched in cholesterol, sphingolipids, and certain proteins (Figure 40--8). Lipid rafts are hypothesized to be involved in signal transduction and other processes. Clustering certain components of signaling systems closely together in lipid rafts may increase the efficiency of their function.

Caveolae may derive from lipid rafts. Caveolae contain the protein caveolin-1. Caveolin-1 may be involved in the formation of caveolae from rafts. Caveolae are observable by electron microscopy as flask, or tube-shaped indentations of the cell membrane into the cytosol (Figure 40--9). Caveolae likely play a role in endocytosis. Caveolae contain various components of the signal transduction system, such as the insulin receptor and some G proteins. Caveolae contain the folate receptor and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS). Caveolae and lipid rafts are active areas of research. Ideas concerning caveolae and their roles in various biologic processes are rapidly evolving.

## Passive Diffusion Involving Transporters & Ion Channels Moves Many Small Molecules Across Membranes

Molecules can passively traverse the bilayer down electrochemical gradients by simple diffusion. Molecules can passively traverse the bilayer down electrochemical gradients by facilitated diffusion. Active transport requires energy because it moves solutes against an electrochemical gradient. Simple diffusion is the passive flow of a solute from a higher to a lower concentration due to random thermal movement. Facilitated diffusion is passive transport of a solute from a higher to a lower concentration mediated by a specific protein transporter. Active transport involves vectorial movement of a solute across a membrane against a concentration gradient. Active transport requires energy, often derived from the hydrolysis of ATP. Active transport involves a specific transporter (pump).

Some solutes such as gases can enter the cell by diffusing down an electrochemical gradient across the membrane without requiring metabolic energy. Simple diffusion across the membrane is limited by the thermal agitation of the molecule. Simple diffusion across the membrane is limited by the concentration gradient across the membrane. Simple diffusion across the membrane is limited by the solubility of the solute in the hydrophobic core of the membrane bilayer. Solubility is inversely proportional to the number of hydrogen bonds that must be broken for a solute to incorporate into the hydrophobic bilayer. Electrolytes, which are poorly soluble in lipid, do not form hydrogen bonds with water. Electrolytes acquire a shell of water from hydration by electrostatic interaction. The size of the hydration shell is directly proportional to the charge density of the electrolyte. Electrolytes with a large charge density have a larger shell of hydration. Electrolytes with a large charge density have a slower diffusion rate.

The inside of the cell usually has a net negative charge. Increased hydrostatic pressure increases the rate of collisions between molecules and the membrane. Increased hydrostatic pressure increases the force of collisions between molecules and the membrane. Increased temperature increases particle motion. Increased temperature increases the frequency of collisions between external particles and the membrane.

## Facilitated Diffusion Is Mediated by a Variety of Specific Transporters

Some specific solutes diffuse across membranes more rapidly than expected due to their size, charge, or partition coefficient. Specific transporters are involved in the rapid diffusion of some solutes across membranes. Facilitated diffusion is a uniport system. Facilitated diffusion can be saturated, indicating a finite number of sites for solute diffusion. Facilitated diffusion systems are often stereospecific. Facilitated diffusion systems are driven by the transmembrane electrochemical gradient.

The 'ping-pong' mechanism helps explain facilitated diffusion. In this model, the carrier protein exists in two principal conformations. In the 'ping' state, the carrier is exposed to high solute concentrations. Solute molecules bind to specific sites on the carrier in the 'ping' state. Binding induces a conformational change in the carrier protein. The conformational change exposes the carrier to a lower concentration of solute in the 'pong' state. The 'ping-pong' mechanism is completely reversible. Net flux across the membrane depends on the concentration gradient (Figure 40–13).

Estrogens increase amino acid transport in the uterus.

There are at least five different carrier systems for amino acids in animal cells. Each carrier system is specific for a group of closely related amino acids.

## Ion Channels Are Transmembrane Proteins That Allow the Selective Entry of Various Ions

Natural membranes contain transmembrane channels composed of proteins that form selective ion channels. Cation-conductive channels typically have an average diameter of about 5 to 8 nm. The permeability of a channel depends on the size, hydration extent, and charge density of the ion. The domains are connected by variable-length intra- and extracellular loops. The voltage sensor of the channel is formed through the interaction of domains I to IV.

These carbonyls displace bound water from the ion, restricting its size to appropriate dimensions for passage through the channel. All ion channels are basically made up of transmembrane subunits that come together to form a central pore through which ions pass selectively. The membranes of nerve cells contain well-studied ion channels responsible for the generation of action potentials. Some of these channels are controlled by neurotransmitters, allowing regulation of channel activity. Ion channels are open transiently and are “gated.” Gates can be controlled by opening or closing. In ligand-gated channels, a specific molecule binds to a receptor and opens the channel. Voltage-gated channels open (or close) in response to changes in membrane potential. Mechanically gated channels respond to mechanical stimuli such as pressure and touch. Some properties of ion channels are listed in Table 40--3 and Table 40--4 (Table 40-4: (no caption)).

## Some Properties of Ion Channels

Ion channels are composed of transmembrane protein subunits.

Ion channels are highly selective for one ion. A few ion channels are nonselective.

Ion channels allow impermeable ions to cross membranes. Ions cross membranes at rates approaching diffusion limits.

Ion channels are regulated in their activities.

The main types of ion channels are voltage-gated, ligand-gated, and mechanically gated.

Ion channels are usually highly conserved across species.

Mutations in genes encoding ion channels can cause specific diseases.

The activities of ion channels are affected by certain drugs.

## Ionophores Are Molecules That Act as Membrane Shuttles for Various Ions

Certain microbes synthesize small cyclic organic molecules called ionophores, such as valinomycin. Ionophores function as shuttles for the movement of ions across membranes. Ionophores contain hydrophilic centers that are surrounded by peripheral hydrophobic regions. Specific ions bind within the hydrophilic center of the ionophore. The ionophore then diffuses through the membrane, delivering the ion to the cytosol. Gramicidin is a polypeptide antibiotic that acts as an ionophore. Gramicidin folds up to form hollow channels through which ions can traverse the membrane.

Microbial toxins, such as diphtheria toxin, can produce large pores in cellular membranes. Large pores provide macromolecules with direct access to the internal milieu. Activated serum complement components can also produce large pores in cellular membranes. ATP leakage results in cell lysis.

## Aquaporins Are Proteins That Form Water Channels in Certain Membranes

In certain cells, such as red cells and cells of the collecting ductules of the kidney, the movement of water by simple diffusion is augmented by movement through water channels.

Water channels are composed of tetrameric transmembrane proteins named aquaporins.

At least 10 distinct aquaporins (AP-1 to AP-10) have been identified.

Crystallographic and other studies have revealed how these channels permit passage of water but exclude passage of ions and protons.

The pores are too narrow to permit passage of ions.

Mutations in the gene encoding AP-2 have been shown to be the cause of one type of nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.

Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus is a condition in which there is an inability to concentrate urine.

# TRANSMISSION OF NERVE IMPULSES INVOLVES ION CHANNELS & PUMPS

The membrane of neuronal cells maintains an asymmetry of inside-outside voltage and is electrically excitable due to the presence of voltage-gated channels. The voltage difference rapidly collapses, leading to depolarization of that segment of the membrane. Ion pumps in the membrane quickly restore the gradient.

When large areas of the membrane are depolarized, the electrochemical disturbance propagates in a wave-like form down the membrane, generating a nerve impulse. Myelin sheets, formed by Schwann cells, wrap around nerve fibers and provide an electrical insulator that surrounds most of the nerve. Myelin greatly speeds up the propagation of the wave by allowing ions to flow in and out of the membrane only at the nodes of Ranvier. The myelin membrane has a very high-lipid content, which accounts for its excellent insulating property. Relatively few proteins are found in the myelin membrane; those present hold together multiple membrane bilayers to form the hydrophobic insulating structure that is impermeable to ions and water.

Certain diseases, such as multiple sclerosis and the Guillain-Barré syndrome, are characterized by demyelination and impaired nerve conduction.

Glucose transport in the small intestine involves different aspects of the principles of transport. Similar mechanisms are used to transport other sugars as well as amino acids across the apical lumen in polarized cells such as are found in the intestine and kidney. The transcellular movement of glucose involves one additional component, a uniport that allows the glucose accumulated within the cell to move across the basolateral membrane. This movement involves a glucose uniporter (GLUT2).

# CELLS TRANSPORT CERTAIN MACROMOLECULES ACROSS THE PLASMA MEMBRANE BY ENDOCYTOSIS & EXOCYTOSIS

The process by which cells take up large molecules is called endocytosis. Some molecules hydrolyzed inside the cell yield nutrients, such as polysaccharides, proteins, and polynucleotides. Endocytosis provides a mechanism for regulating the content of certain membrane components, including hormone receptors. Endocytosis can be used to study how cells function. DNA from one cell type can be used to transfect a different cell and alter the latter's function or phenotype. A specific gene is often employed in these experiments to study and analyze the regulation of that gene. DNA transfection depends on endocytosis for the entry of DNA into the cell. Cells release macromolecules by exocytosis. Endocytosis and exocytosis both involve vesicle formation with or from the plasma membrane.

## Endocytosis Involves Ingestion of Parts of the Plasma Membrane

Endocytosis involves the ingestion of parts of the plasma membrane in almost all eukaryotic cells. Endocytotic vesicles are formed when segments of the plasma membrane invaginate. Endocytotic vesicles enclose extracellular fluid and its contents. The vesicle pinches off as the plasma membranes fuse. The fusion of plasma membranes seals the neck of the vesicle at the site of invagination. The generated vesicle has a bilayer lipid membrane. The generated vesicle fuses with other membrane structures. The fusion of the vesicle facilitates the transport of its contents to other cellular compartments or back to the cell exterior. Most endocytotic vesicles fuse with primary lysosomes to form secondary lysosomes. Secondary lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes for intracellular disposal. The macromolecular contents are digested to yield amino acids, simple sugars, or nucleotides. Amino acids, simple sugars, or nucleotides are transported out of the vesicles for reuse by the cell. Endocytosis requires energy, usually from ATP hydrolysis. Endocytosis requires calcium ions. Endocytosis requires cytoskeletal elements in the cell.

There are two general types of endocytosis: phagocytosis and pinocytosis. Phagocytosis occurs in specialized cells like macrophages and granulocytes. Phagocytosis involves the ingestion of large particles such as viruses, bacteria, cells, or cellular debris. Macrophages can ingest 25% of their volume per hour. Macrophages internalize 3% of their plasma membrane each minute. Macrophages internalize the entire membrane every 30 minutes.

Pinocytosis, or 'cell drinking,' is a property of all cells. Pinocytosis leads to the uptake of fluid and its contents. Fluid-phase pinocytosis is a nonselective process. In fluid-phase pinocytosis, solute uptake by small vesicle formation is proportionate to its concentration in the extracellular fluid. Vesicle formation in pinocytosis is highly active. Fibroblasts internalize their plasma membrane at about one-third the rate of macrophages. The cell's surface area and volume remain relatively unchanged during pinocytosis. Membrane replacement by exocytosis or recycling is necessary to balance endocytosis.

Absorptive pinocytosis involves the uptake of specific macromolecules with binding sites on the plasma membrane. Receptor-mediated endocytosis involves the uptake of specific macromolecules with binding sites on the plasma membrane. High-affinity receptors allow selective concentration of ligands from the medium. High-affinity receptors minimize uptake of unbound macromolecules. High-affinity receptors increase the rate of specific molecule entry into the cell. Vesicles formed during absorptive pinocytosis are derived from invaginations called coated pits. Coated pits are lined with clathrin. Clathrin has a triskelion structure. Each limb of clathrin is composed of one light and one heavy chain. Clathrin polymerization into vesicles is directed by assembly particles made of four adapter proteins. Adapter proteins interact with specific amino acid sequences in the receptors, ensuring uptake selectivity. Dynamin is necessary for the pinching off of clathrin-coated vesicles from the cell surface. Dynamin binds and hydrolyzes GTP. Coated pits can constitute up to 2% of the surface of some cells.

The cholesterol-containing low-density lipoprotein (LDL) molecule and its receptor are internalized via coated pits containing the LDL receptor. Endocytotic vesicles containing the LDL-bound LDL receptor complex fuse with lysosomes in the cell. The receptor is released and recycled back to the cell surface. The apoprotein of LDL is degraded and the cholesteryl esters metabolized. The synthesis of the LDL receptor is regulated by metabolic products, such as cholesterol, released during LDL degradation. Disorders of the LDL receptor and its internalization are medically significant.

Absorptive pinocytosis of extracellular glycoproteins requires specific carbohydrate recognition signals. Specific carbohydrate recognition signals are bound by membrane receptor molecules similar to the LDL receptor. A galactosyl receptor on the surface of hepatocytes facilitates the absorptive pinocytosis of asialoglycoproteins from the circulation. Acid hydrolases taken up by absorptive pinocytosis in fibroblasts are recognized by their mannose-6-phosphate moieties. Mannose-6-phosphate moieties play a role in intracellular targeting to lysosomes.

Receptor-mediated endocytosis can be problematic. Viruses causing diseases like hepatitis, poliomyelitis, AIDS, and COVID-19 use receptor-mediated endocytosis to enter cells. Iron toxicity begins with excessive uptake due to endocytosis. (Figure 40-18)

# VARIOUS SIGNALS CAN BE TRANSMITTED ACROSS MEMBRANES

Specific biochemical signals such as neurotransmitters, hormones, and immunoglobulins bind to integral transmembrane receptor proteins via their exposed extracellular domains. # GAP JUNCTIONS ALLOW DIRECT FLOW OF MOLECULES FROM ONE CELL TO ANOTHER

Gap junctions are structures that permit direct transfer of small molecules (up to ∼1200 Da) from one cell to its neighbor. Gap junctions are composed of a family of proteins called connexins that form a bihexagonal structure consisting of 12 such proteins. Six connexins form a connexin hemichannel and join to a similar structure in a neighboring cell to make a complete, membrane-spanning connexon channel (Figure 40-20). One gap junction contains several connexons. Different connexins are found in different tissues. Mutations in genes encoding connexins have been found to be associated with a number of conditions, including cardiovascular abnormalities, one type of deafness, and the X-linked form of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (a demyelinating neurologic disorder).

# EXTRACELLULAR VESICLES (EXOSOMES) REPRESENT A NOVEL, & PREVIOUSLY UNDERAPPRECIATED MECHANISM OF CELL-CELL COMMUNICATION

In the last decade, a class of small, heterogeneous, secreted vesicles, broadly termed extracellular vesicles, have been identified and characterized. Extracellular vesicles have been implicated as a new and important mediator of cell-cell communication. Extracellular vesicles contribute importantly to both normal and pathologic physiology. Extracellular vesicles are enclosed by a lipid bilayer. Extracellular vesicles are somewhat heterogeneous in size, ranging from 30 to 2000 nm in diameter. Extracellular vesicles are generated by at least two distinct mechanisms. Microvesicles are generated by budding from the plasma membrane of a source cell. Exosomes are generated from the multivesicular body (MVB), a component of the endocytic membrane trafficking system. Exosomes are secreted from the source cell on fusion of the MVB with the plasma membrane. The released extracellular vesicles (exosomes and microvesicles) ultimately fuse to their target cell to deliver a distinct 'payload'. Given the recent discovery of extracellular vesicles, the exact names and terms used to describe these vesicles, their cargos, and relevant source and target cells vary in the biomedical literature. The terms 'microvesicle' and 'exosome' are often lumped together as simply 'exosomes'.

Vesicle content varies from source cell to source cell. Vesicle content has been reported to be different from the same source cell grown under different conditions. Vesicle payloads can include a variety of cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins. Vesicle payloads can include membrane-bound proteins ranging from channels to receptors. Vesicle payloads can include major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. Vesicle payloads can include lipid raft-interacting proteins. Vesicle payloads can include DNA, mRNA, large and small noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs). Vesicle payloads can include small proteins and bioactive small molecules. More recent studies have identified cellular secretion of non-membrane enveloped proteins and multiprotein complexes (nanoparticles). Given the rich and broad diversity of secreted vesicles, exosomes and non-membrane-bounded proteins have been implicated in a very broad range of biology and diseases. Extracellular vesicles appear to target specific recipient cells. The potential value of exosomes as therapeutic delivery systems is receiving significant interest and attention in the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industries. ## Cystic Fibrosis Is Due to Mutations in the Gene Encoding CFTR, a Chloride Transporter

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a recessive genetic disorder prevalent among whites in North America and certain parts of northern Europe.

Cystic fibrosis is characterized by chronic bacterial infections of the airways and sinuses.

Cystic fibrosis causes fat maldigestion due to pancreatic exocrine insufficiency.

Cystic fibrosis leads to infertility in males due to abnormal development of the vas deferens.

Cystic fibrosis results in elevated levels of chloride in sweat, greater than 60 mmol/L.

Mutations in the gene encoding the cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator protein (CFTR) are responsible for cystic fibrosis.

Membranes are complex dynamic structures composed of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrate-containing molecules.
The basic structure of all membranes is the lipid bilayer. This bilayer is formed by two sheets of phospholipids in which the hydrophilic polar head groups are directed away from each other and are exposed to the aqueous environment on the outer and inner surfaces of the membrane. The hydrophobic nonpolar tails of these molecules are oriented toward each other, in the direction of the center of the membrane.
Membranes are very dynamic structures. Membrane constituents, lipids, and certain proteins, show rapid lateral diffusion. Flip-flop is very slow for lipids and almost nonexistent for proteins.
The fluid mosaic model forms a useful basis for thinking about membrane structure and function.
Membrane proteins are classified as integral if they are firmly embedded in the bilayer and as peripheral if they are attached to the outer or inner membrane surface.
The 20 or so membranes in a mammalian cell have different compositions and functions and they define essential compartments, or specialized environments, within the cell that have specific functions.
Certain hydrophobic molecules freely diffuse across membranes, but the movement of others is restricted because of their size and/or charge.
Various passive and active (usually ATP-dependent) mechanisms are employed to maintain gradients of many different molecules across different membranes.
Certain solutes, for example, glucose, enter cells by facilitated diffusion along a downhill gradient from high to low concentration using specific carrier proteins (transporters).
The major ATP-driven pumps are classified as P (phosphorylated), F (energy factors), V (vacuolar), and ABC transporters.
Large molecules can enter or leave cells through mechanisms such as endocytosis or exocytosis. These processes often require binding of the molecule to a receptor, which affords specificity to the process.
Extracellular vesicles, termed exosomes, also allow direct movement of macromolecules from cell to cell via small vesicles. Exosome payloads can include specific lipids, proteins (receptors, channels, signaling proteins), DNA, RNAs, and small bioactive molecules.
Mutations that affect the structure of membrane proteins may cause diseases.
