## DNA Contains Four Distinct Deoxynucleotides

The chemical nature of the monomeric deoxynucleotide units of DNA includes deoxyadenylate, deoxyguanylate, deoxycytidylate, and thymidylate. The monomeric deoxynucleotide units are described in detail in Chapter 32. The monomeric units of DNA are held together by 3',5'-phosphodiester bonds. A single strand of DNA is shown in Figure 34–1. The genetic code, or the informational content of DNA, is determined by the sequence of these monomers. The monomers are purine and pyrimidine deoxyribonucleotides. The DNA polymer has a polarity. One end of the DNA polymer has a 5'-hydroxyl or phosphate terminus. The other end of the DNA polymer has a 3'-phosphate or hydroxyl terminus. The polarity of the DNA polymer is crucial for the function of DNA. The genetic information in DNA is stored in the order of the monomeric units within the polymers. A mechanism for reproducing or replicating this information with high fidelity is necessary. The observation by Chargaff that the concentration of deoxyadenosine (A) nucleotides equals that of thymidine (T) nucleotides (A = T) was instrumental in the development of the double-stranded DNA model by Watson, Crick, and Wilkins in the early 1950s. The observation by Chargaff that the concentration of deoxyguanosine (G) nucleotides equals that of deoxycytidine (C) nucleotides (G = C) was instrumental in the development of the double-stranded DNA model by Watson, Crick, and Wilkins in the early 1950s. The double-stranded DNA model is depicted in Figure 34–2. The two strands of the double-stranded helix are held together by hydrogen bonds between the purine and pyrimidine bases. The two strands of the double-stranded helix are held together by van der Waals and hydrophobic interactions between the stacked adjacent base pairs. The pairings between the purine and pyrimidine nucleotides are specific. Adenine (A) pairs with thymine (T). Guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C). These pairings are often referred to as Watson-Crick base pairs. The common form of DNA is right-handed, meaning the base residues spiral in a clockwise direction when viewed from above. The double-stranded DNA molecule has antiparallel strands. One strand runs in the 5' to 3' direction. The other strand runs in the 3' to 5' direction. Within a gene, the genetic information is stored in the sequence of nucleotides on the template strand. The template strand is the strand copied during RNA synthesis. The opposite strand is considered the coding strand because it matches the RNA transcript sequence, with uracil in place of thymine. The two strands of DNA are held together by hydrogen bonds and wind around a central axis in the form of a double helix. In the test tube, double-stranded DNA can exist in at least six forms: A-DNA, B-DNA, C-DNA, D-DNA, E-DNA, and Z-DNA. The B form is typically found under physiologic conditions. A single turn of B form DNA contains 10 base pairs and spans a distance of 3.4 nm (34 Å). The width of the double helix in B-DNA is 2 nm (20 Å).

## The Denaturation of DNA Is Used to Analyze Its Structure

Three hydrogen bonds hold the deoxyguanosine nucleotide to the deoxycytidine nucleotide. The A-T pair is held together by two hydrogen bonds. The four DNA nucleotide bases, deoxyguanosine, deoxyadenine, deoxythymidine, and deoxycytidine, are flat and planar. The four DNA nucleotide bases allow for close stacking within duplex DNA. The atoms within the aromatic, heterocyclic bases are highly polarizable. Many atoms within the bases contain partial charges. The stacked bases form van der Waals and electrostatic interactions. The van der Waals and electrostatic interactions are collectively referred to as base-stacking forces or base-stacking interactions. Base-stacking interactions between adjacent G-C base pairs are stronger than those between A-T base pairs. G-C-rich DNA sequences are more resistant to denaturation than A-T-rich regions. Denaturation is the separation of DNA strands, also termed 'melting'.

## DNA Can be Reversibly Denatured & Specifically Renatured, Both in the Test Tube & in Living Cells

In the laboratory, the double-stranded structure of DNA can be separated into its two component strands by increasing temperature. In the laboratory, the double-stranded structure of DNA can be separated into its two component strands by decreasing solution salt concentrations. In the laboratory, the double-stranded structure of DNA can be separated into its two component strands by adding chaotropic agents that form competing H-bonds with the individual deoxynucleotide bases. The denaturation of DNA into two single strands is accompanied by an increase in optical absorbance in the ultraviolet light spectrum at 260 nm. The increase in optical absorbance in the ultraviolet light spectrum at 260 nm is known as hyperchromicity of denaturation. Native double-stranded DNA in solution is an extremely viscous material due to the combined strength of base stacking and H-bonding between complementary bases. Upon denaturation, DNA solutions lose their viscosity.

DNA rich in G-C pairs melts at a higher temperature than DNA rich in A-T pairs due to differences in hydrogen bond content and base stacking. Chaotropes such as urea and formamide can efficiently form H-bonds with the nucleotide bases. Chaotrope addition allows the strands of DNA or complementary DNA-RNA, and intramolecular RNA-RNA hybrids to be separated at much lower temperatures. Chaotrope addition minimizes phosphodiester bond breakage and chemical damage to nucleotides during extended incubation in solution.

In living cells, DNA denaturation and renaturation occur naturally during DNA replication, DNA recombination, DNA repair, and DNA gene transcription. DNA denaturation and renaturation are mediated by specific nucleic acid binding proteins. DNA denaturation and renaturation are mediated by various enzymes. DNA denaturation and renaturation utilize thermal and/or chemical energy supplied via ATP hydrolysis.

## DNA Exists in Relaxed & Supercoiled Forms

In some organisms such as bacteria, bacteriophages, many DNA-containing animal viruses, and organelles like mitochondria, the ends of DNA molecules are joined to form a closed circle with no covalently free ends. Closed circles exist in relaxed or supercoiled forms. Supercoils are introduced when a closed circle is twisted around its own axis. Supercoils are introduced when a linear piece of duplex DNA, whose ends are fixed, is twisted. The process of introducing supercoils requires energy and puts the molecule under torsional stress. The greater the number of supercoils, the greater the stress or torsion. Negative supercoils are formed when the molecule is twisted in the direction opposite from the clockwise turns of the right-handed double helix found in B-DNA. Such DNA is said to be underwound. The energy required to achieve this state is stored in the supercoils. The transition to another form that requires energy is facilitated by the underwinding. One such transition is strand separation, which is a prerequisite for DNA replication and transcription. Supercoiled DNA is a preferred form in biologic systems. Enzymes that catalyze topologic changes of DNA are called topoisomerases. Topoisomerases can relax or insert supercoils using ATP as an energy source. Homologs of this enzyme exist in all organisms and are important targets for cancer chemotherapy. Supercoils can also form within linear DNAs if particular segments of DNA are constrained by interacting tightly with nuclear proteins that establish two boundary sites defining a topologic domain. Even higher orders of altered DNA structure exist and are of functional significance.

# THE CHEMICAL NATURE OF RNA DIFFERS FROM THAT OF DNA

RNA is a polymer of purine and pyrimidine ribonucleotides linked by 3',5'-phosphodiester bonds. RNA contains ribose as its sugar moiety. DNA contains 2'-deoxyribose as its sugar moiety. RNA contains adenine, guanine, cytosine, and uracil as its pyrimidine components. RNA does not contain thymine. DNA contains thymine. RNA can exist as a single strand. DNA typically exists as a double-stranded helical molecule. RNA can fold back on itself to form double-stranded characteristics through complementary base pairing. RNA pairs guanine with cytosine. RNA pairs adenine with uracil. RNA's guanine content does not necessarily equal its cytosine content. RNA's adenine content does not necessarily equal its uracil content. RNA can be hydrolyzed by alkali to form 2', 3' cyclic diesters of the mononucleotides. DNA cannot form 2', 3' cyclic diesters due to the absence of a 2'-hydroxyl group. The alkali lability of RNA is useful for diagnostic and analytical purposes. The primary structure of RNA is its sequence of purine and pyrimidine nucleotides. RNA's primary structure is complementary to the template strand of the gene from which it was transcribed. An RNA molecule can bind specifically to its template DNA strand through base-pairing rules. An RNA molecule does not bind with the coding strand of its gene. The sequence of the RNA molecule, except for U replacing T, is the same as that of the coding strand of the gene (Figure 34–8).

## Messenger RNA

mRNA is the most heterogeneous RNA class in terms of abundance, size, and stability. Specific mRNAs in brewer's yeast can range from 100s per cell to less than 0.1 per mRNA per cell in a genetically homogeneous population. Specific transcriptional and posttranscriptional mechanisms contribute to the large dynamic range in mRNA content. All members of this RNA class function as messengers conveying the information in a gene to the protein-synthesizing machinery. Each mRNA serves as a template for the polymerization of a specific sequence of amino acids to form a specific protein molecule, which is the ultimate gene product (Figure 34–9).

Eukaryotic mRNAs have unique chemical characteristics. The 5' terminus of mRNA is capped by a 7-methylguanosine triphosphate linked to an adjacent 2'-O-methyl ribonucleoside at its 5'-hydroxyl through the three phosphates (Figure 34-10). The cap is involved in the recognition of mRNA by the translation machinery. The cap helps stabilize the mRNA by preventing the nucleolytic attack by 5'-exoribonucleases. The protein-synthesizing machinery begins translating the mRNA into proteins downstream of the 5' or capped terminus. At the 3'-hydroxyl terminus of almost all eukaryotic mRNA molecules, there is an attached, nongenetically encoded polymer of adenylate residues 20 to 250 nucleotides in length. The poly(A) tail at the 3'-end of mRNAs maintains the intracellular stability of the specific mRNA by preventing the attack of 3'-exoribonucleases. The poly(A) tail facilitates translation. Both the mRNA cap and poly(A) tail are added posttranscriptionally by nontemplate-directed enzymes to mRNA precursor molecules (pre-mRNA). mRNA represents 2 to 5% of total eukaryotic cellular RNA.

## Ribosomal RNA

A ribosome is a cytoplasmic nucleoprotein structure that serves as the machine for the synthesis of proteins from the mRNA templates. The ribosome facilitates the interaction between mRNA and tRNA molecules to translate the information transcribed from the gene during mRNA synthesis into a specific protein. During periods of active protein synthesis, many ribosomes can be associated with any single mRNA molecule to form an assembly called the polysome (see Figure 37-7).

## Small RNA

A large number of discrete, highly conserved small RNA species are found in eukaryotic cells. Some small RNA species are quite stable. Most small RNA molecules are complexed with proteins to form ribonucleoproteins. Ribonucleoproteins are distributed in the nucleus, the cytoplasm, or both. Small RNA species range in size from 20 to 1000 nucleotides. Small RNA species are present in 100,000 to 1,000,000 copies per cell. Collectively, small RNA species represent less than or equal to 5% of cellular RNA.

## Large & Small Noncoding Regulatory RNAs: Micro-RNAs (miRNAs), Silencing RNAs (siRNAs), Long Noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), and Circular RNAs (circRNAs)

Regulatory nonprotein coding RNAs (ncRNAs) have been identified and characterized in the last decade of eukaryotic regulatory biology. NcRNAs exist in two general size classes: large (50-1000 nucleotides) and small (20-22 nucleotides).

Small ncRNAs, termed miRNAs and siRNAs, typically inhibit gene expression at the level of specific protein production by targeting mRNAs. miRNAs are generated by specific nucleolytic processing of the products of distinct genes or transcription units. miRNA precursors are 5'-capped and 3'-polyadenylated. miRNA precursors usually range in size from about 500 to 1000 nucleotides.

siRNAs are generated by the specific nucleolytic processing of large dsRNAs. Large dsRNAs can be produced from endogenous RNAs or introduced into the cell by RNA viruses. Both siRNAs and miRNAs hybridize to their targeted mRNAs via RNA-RNA hybridization. Hundreds of distinct miRNAs and siRNAs have been described in humans. Estimates suggest thousands of human miRNA-encoding genes exist.

miRNAs are potential agents for therapeutic drug development due to their genetic specificity. siRNAs are potential agents for therapeutic drug development due to their genetic specificity. siRNAs are used in the laboratory to decrease or 'knockdown' specific protein levels by directing mRNA degradation. siRNAs serve as an alternative to gene-knockout technology. Several siRNA-based therapeutic clinical trials are in progress to test the efficacy of these molecules as drugs for treating human disease.

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) and long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are two classes of larger noncoding RNAs that have been recently identified and characterized. circRNAs are produced by RNA splicing-type reactions from a wide range of precursor RNAs. circRNAs are produced from mRNA precursors. circRNAs are produced from nonprotein coding lncRNA precursors. circRNAs have been detected in all eukaryotes tested. circRNAs seem particularly abundant in metazoans. circRNAs seem particularly abundant in cells of the nervous system.

LncRNAs do not code for protein. LncRNAs range in size from about 300 to thousands of nucleotides. LncRNAs are typically transcribed from large regions of eukaryotic genomes that do not encode proteins. More than 90% of all eukaryotic genomic DNA may be transcribed at some level. Noncoding RNAs make up a significant portion of this transcription. Noncoding RNAs play roles in structural aspects of chromatin. Noncoding RNAs play roles in the regulation of mRNA gene transcription by RNA polymerase II.

Bacteria contain small, heterogeneous regulatory RNAs termed sRNAs. Bacterial sRNAs range in size from 50 to 500 nucleotides. Bacterial sRNAs control the expression or activity of a large array of distinct genes. sRNAs often repress protein synthesis by binding to specific mRNA. sRNAs sometimes activate protein synthesis by binding to specific mRNA.

# SPECIFIC NUCLEASES DIGEST NUCLEIC ACIDS

Enzymes capable of degrading nucleic acids have been recognized for many years. Enzymes capable of degrading nucleic acids are called nucleases. Nucleases that exhibit specificity for DNA are referred to as deoxyribonucleases. Nucleases that specifically hydrolyze RNA are ribonucleases. Some nucleases can degrade both DNA and RNA. Within the classes of nucleases, there are enzymes capable of cleaving internal phosphodiester bonds. Enzymes capable of cleaving internal phosphodiester bonds produce either 3'-hydroxyl and 5'-phosphoryl terminals or 5'-hydroxyl and 3'-phosphoryl terminals. These enzymes are referred to as endonucleases. Some endonucleases can hydrolyze both strands of a double-stranded molecule. Some endonucleases can only cleave single strands of nucleic acids. Some nucleases can hydrolyze only unpaired single strands. Some nucleases can hydrolyze single strands participating in the formation of a double-stranded molecule. There exist classes of endonucleases that recognize specific sequences in DNA. Restriction endonucleases, also termed restriction enzymes, are a class of these DNA cleaving enzymes. Restriction enzymes bind specific, usually contiguous DNA base pairs, typically 4, 5, 6, or 8 bp, and cleave both strands of DNA, usually within the binding/recognition sequence element. The CRISPR-Cas family of enzymes is another class of enzymes. CRISPR-Cas enzymes are ribonucleoprotein complexes that utilize a 'guide RNA' of specific nucleotide sequence to target a nuclease to cleave distinct DNA or RNA sequences. Both classes of DNA-cleaving enzyme are described in greater detail in Chapter 39.

Some nucleases are capable of hydrolyzing a nucleotide only when it is present at a terminal of a molecule. Nucleases capable of hydrolyzing a nucleotide only when it is present at a terminal of a molecule are referred to as exonucleases. Exonucleases act in one direction, either 3' to 5' or 5' to 3', only. In bacteria, a 3' to 5' exonuclease is an integral part of the DNA replication machinery. A 3' to 5' exonuclease serves to edit or proofread the most recently added deoxynucleotide for base-pairing errors.

Nucleases are essential tools in modern molecular genetics and molecular medicine. Nucleases have roles in nucleic acid metabolism in living cells. Nucleases are described in greater detail in Chapter 39.

\- DNA consists of four bases—A, G, C, and T—that are held in linear array by phosphodiester bonds through the 3' and 5' positions of adjacent deoxyribose moieties.
DNA is organized into two strands by the pairing of bases A to T and G to C on complementary strands. These strands form a double helix around a central axis.
The exact sequence of these 3 billion nucleotides defines the uniqueness of each individual.
DNA provides a template, both for its own replication and thus maintenance of the genotype, and for the transcription of the roughly 25,000 protein coding human genes as well as a large array of nonprotein coding regulatory ncRNAs.
RNA exists in several different cellular nucleoprotein structures, most of which are directly or indirectly involved in protein synthesis or its regulation. The linear array of nucleotides in RNA consists of A, G, C, and U, and the sugar moiety is ribose.
The major forms of RNA include mRNA, rRNA, tRNA, snRNAs, and regulatory ncRNAs. Certain RNA molecules act as catalysts (ribozymes).
