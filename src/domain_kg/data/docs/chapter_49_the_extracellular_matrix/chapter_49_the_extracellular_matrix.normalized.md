# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Most mammalian cells are located in tissues surrounded by a complex extracellular matrix (ECM), often referred to as 'connective tissue'. The ECM protects organs and provides elasticity where required, such as in blood vessels, lungs, and skin. The ECM contains three major classes of biomolecules: structural proteins, specialized proteins, and proteoglycans. Structural proteins in the ECM include collagen, elastin, and fibrillin. Specialized proteins in the ECM include fibronectin and laminin. Fibronectin and laminin form a mesh of fibers embedded in proteoglycans. The ECM is involved in many processes, both normal and pathologic. The ECM plays important roles in development, inflammatory states, and the spread of cancer cells. Certain components of the ECM are involved in rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis. Genetic disturbances in the synthesis of collagen, a major ECM component, cause diseases such as osteogenesis imperfecta and some types of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Specific components of proteoglycans, known as glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), are affected in genetic disorders called mucopolysaccharidoses. Changes occur in the ECM during the aging process. The chapter describes the basic biochemistry of the three major classes of biomolecules found in the ECM. The chapter also illustrates the biomedical significance of these biomolecules. Major biochemical features of two specialized forms of ECM—bone and cartilage—are briefly considered. The chapter considers a number of diseases involving bone and cartilage.

# COLLAGEN IS THE MOST ABUNDANT PROTEIN IN THE ANIMAL WORLD

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the animal world, constituting approximately 25% of the protein in mammals. Collagen provides an extracellular framework for all metazoan animals and exists in virtually every animal tissue. Twenty-nine types of collagens, made up of over 30 distinct polypeptide chains, have been identified in human tissues. Each polypeptide chain is encoded by a separate gene. Some proteins, such as the C1q component of the complement system and pulmonary surfactant proteins SPA and SPD, have collagen-like domains in their structures and are sometimes referred to as 'noncollagen collagens.'

Type I collagen is found in noncartilaginous connective tissues, including bone, tendon, and skin. Type II collagen is present in cartilage and the vitreous humor. Type III collagen is located in extensible connective tissues, such as skin, lung, and the vascular system. Type IV collagen is part of basement membranes. Type V collagen is a minor component in tissues containing collagen I. Type VI collagen is found in muscle and most connective tissues. Type VII collagen is located at the dermal-epidermal junction. Type VIII collagen is found in endothelium and other tissues. Type IX collagen is present in tissues containing collagen II. Type X collagen is found in hypertrophic cartilage. Type XI collagen is also present in tissues containing collagen II. Type XII collagen is found in tissues containing collagen I. Type XIII collagen is distributed in many tissues, including neuromuscular junctions and skin. Type XIV collagen is also found in tissues containing collagen I. Type XV collagen is associated with collagens close to basement membranes in many tissues, including the eye, muscle, and microvessels. Type XVI collagen is found in many tissues. Type XVII collagen is located in epithelia and skin hemidesmosomes. Type XVIII collagen is associated with collagens close to basement membranes and is a close structural homologue of Type XV collagen. Type XIX collagen is rare and found in basement membranes and rhabdomyosarcoma cells. Type XX collagen is present in many tissues, particularly the corneal epithelium. Type XXI collagen is also found in many tissues. Type XXII collagen is located at tissue junctions, including cartilage-synovial fluid and hair follicle-dermis. Type XXIII collagen is limited in tissues, mainly existing as transmembrane and shed forms. Type XXIV collagen is found in developing cornea and bone. Type XXV collagen is located in the brain. Type XXVI collagen is found in the testis and ovary. Type XXVII collagen is present in embryonic cartilage and other developing tissues, as well as in cartilage in adults. Type XXVIII collagen is part of the basement membrane around Schwann cells. Type XXIX collagen is found in the epidermis.

# COLLAGENS HAVE A TRIPLE HELIX STRUCTURE

All collagen types have a triple helical structure made up of three polypeptide chain subunits (\alpha chains). In some collagens, the entire molecule is a triple helix. In others, only a fraction of the structure may be in this form. Mature collagen type I has a structure where each polypeptide subunit is twisted into a left-handed helix of three residues per turn forming an \alpha chain. Three of these are then wound into a right-handed triple- or superhelix, forming a rod-like molecule 1.4 nm in diameter and about 300-nm long (Figure 49-1).

[FIGURE:fig-49-1]

Molecular features of collagen structure. Each individual polypeptide chain is twisted into a left-handed helix of three residues (Gly-X-Y) per turn (alpha chain), and three of these chains are then wound into a right-handed superhelix (triple helix). The triple helices are then assembled into a quarter-staggered alignment to form fibrils. This arrangement leads to areas where there is complete overlap of the molecules alternating with areas where there is a gap, giving the fibrils a regular banded appearance.

Glycine residues occur at every third position of the triple helical portion of the alpha chain. Glycine is the only amino acid small enough to be accommodated in the limited space available in the central core of the triple helix. The repeating structure (Gly-X-Y)n is an absolute requirement for the formation of the triple helix. X and Y can be any other amino acids. The X positions are often proline. The Y positions are often hydroxyproline. Proline and hydroxyproline confer rigidity on the collagen molecule. Hydroxyproline is formed by the posttranslational hydroxylation of peptide-bound proline residues. Prolyl hydroxylase catalyzes the hydroxylation of proline residues. The cofactors of prolyl hydroxylase are ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and alpha-ketoglutarate. Lysines in the Y position may be posttranslationally modified to hydroxylysine. Lysyl hydroxylase modifies lysines to hydroxylysine. Lysyl hydroxylase has similar cofactors to prolyl hydroxylase. Some hydroxylysines may be further modified by the addition of galactose or galactosyl-glucose through an O-glycosidic linkage. The glycosylation site is unique to collagen.

Some collagen types form long rod-like fibers in tissues. Long rod-like fibers are assembled by lateral association of these triple helical units into fibrils (10-300 nm in diameter) in a 'quarter-staggered' alignment such that each is displaced longitudinally from its neighbor by slightly less than one-quarter of its length (see Figure 49-1). Fibrils, in turn, associate into thicker fibers (1-20 \( \mu \) m in diameter). Because the quarter staggered alignment results in regularly spaced gaps between the triple helical molecules in the array, fibers have a banded appearance in connective tissues. In some tissues, for example, tendons, fibers associate into even larger bundles, which may have a diameter of up to 500 \( \mu \) m. Collagen fibers are further stabilized by the formation of covalent cross-links, both within and between the triple helical units. Covalent cross-links form through the action of lysyl oxidase, a copper-dependent enzyme that oxidatively deaminates the \(ε\) -amino groups of certain lysine and hydroxylysine residues, yielding reactive aldehydes. Such aldehydes can form aldol condensation products with other lysine- or hydroxylysine-derived aldehydes, or form Schiff bases with the \(ε\) -amino groups of unoxidized lysines or hydroxylysines. These reactions, after further chemical rearrangements, result in the stable covalent cross-links that are important for the tensile strength of the fibers. Histidine may also be involved in certain cross-links.

The main fibril-forming collagens in skin and bone are types I and II. The main fibril-forming collagens in cartilage are types I and II. Other collagens also adopt the triple helix structure. There are many nonfibril-forming collagens. The structures and functions of nonfibril-forming collagens are described briefly in the following section.

## Some Collagen Types Do Not Form Fibrils

Several collagen types do not form fibrils in tissues. Several collagen types are characterized by interruptions of the triple helix with stretches of protein lacking Gly-X-Y repeat sequences. Areas of globular structure are interspersed in the triple helical structure of these collagen types. Network-like collagens, such as type IV, form networks in basement membranes. Fibril-associated collagens with interrupted triple helices (FACITs) have interruptions in the triple helical domains. Beaded filaments consist of long chains of collagen molecules that have a regular beaded appearance. Collagen VII forms the main part of anchoring fibrils in epithelial tissues. Transmembrane collagens have short intracellular N-terminal domains and extracellular domains with long interrupted triple helices. Multiplexins are collagens with multiple triple helix domains and interruptions. (Figure 49-2)

## Collagen Undergoes Extensive Posttranslational Modifications

Newly synthesized collagen undergoes extensive posttranslational modification. The modification of collagen occurs before it becomes part of a mature extracellular collagen fiber. Table 49--2 provides details on the posttranslational modifications of collagen.

Collagen is synthesized on ribosomes in a precursor form, preprocollagen. Preprocollagen contains a leader or signal sequence that directs the polypeptide chain into the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER).

As it enters the ER, this leader sequence is removed enzymatically.

Hydroxylation of proline and lysine residues takes place at this site. Glycosylation of hydroxylysines takes place at this site.

The procollagen molecule contains polypeptide extensions at its amino-terminal end. The procollagen molecule contains polypeptide extensions at its carboxyl-terminal end. The polypeptide extensions in procollagen are 20 to 35 kDa in size. The polypeptide extensions in procollagen are not present in mature collagen.

Both extension peptides contain cysteine residues.

The amino terminal propeptide forms only intrachain disulfide bonds.

The carboxyl-terminal propeptides form intrachain disulfide bonds. The carboxyl-terminal propeptides form interchain disulfide bonds.

Formation of these disulfide bonds assists in the registration of the three collagen molecules. The registration of the three collagen molecules helps form the triple helix, winding from the carboxyl-terminal end.

After the formation of the triple helix, no further hydroxylation of proline can take place. After the formation of the triple helix, no further hydroxylation of lysine can take place. After the formation of the triple helix, no further glycosylation of hydroxylysines can take place.

Self-assembly is a cardinal principle in the biosynthesis of collagen.

## Extracellular

Cleavage of amino-terminal propeptides is performed by extracellular enzymes called procollagen aminoproteinase. Cleavage of carboxyl-terminal propeptides is performed by extracellular enzymes called procollagen carboxyproteinase. Procollagen aminoproteinase removes the extension peptides at the amino-terminal ends. Procollagen carboxyproteinase removes the extension peptides at the carboxyl-terminal ends. The removal of propeptides forms the monomeric units of collagen, termed tropocollagen. Cleavage of the propeptides may occur within crypts or folds in the cell membrane. Once the propeptides are removed, the tropocollagen molecules spontaneously assemble into collagen fibers.

The same cells that secrete collagen also secrete fibronectin, a large glycoprotein present on cell surfaces, in the ECM, and in blood. Fibronectin binds collagen fibers during aggregation. Fibronectin alters the kinetics of fiber formation in the pericellular matrix. Associated with fibronectin and procollagen in this matrix are the proteoglycans heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate. Type IX collagen, a minor collagen type from cartilage, contains an attached glycosaminoglycan chain. Interactions between fibronectin, proteoglycans, and collagen may serve to regulate the formation of collagen fibers. Interactions between fibronectin, proteoglycans, and collagen may determine their orientation in tissues.

Once formed, collagen is relatively metabolically stable. Collagen breakdown is increased during starvation. Collagen breakdown is increased during various inflammatory states. Excessive production of collagen occurs in conditions such as hepatic cirrhosis.

Oxidative deamination of η-amino groups of lysyl and hydroxylysyl residues to aldehydes is part of the process of forming intra- and interchain cross-links. These cross-links are formed via Schiff bases and aldol condensation products. Lysyl oxidase is the enzyme responsible for the formation of these inter- and intrachain cross-links.

## A Number of Genetic & Deficiency Diseases Result From Abnormalities in the Synthesis of Collagen

More than 30 genes encode the collagens. These genes are designated according to the procollagen type and their constituent alpha chains, called pro alpha chains. Collagens can be homotrimeric, containing three identical pro alpha chains. Collagens can be heterotrimeric, where the pro alpha chains are different. Type I collagen is heterotrimeric, containing two pro alpha 1(I) and one pro alpha 2(I) chains. Type II collagen is homotrimeric, having three pro alpha 1(II) chains. Collagen genes have the prefix COL followed by the type in Arabic numerals, then an A and the number of the pro alpha chain they encode. COL1A1 is the gene for the pro alpha 1 chain of type I collagen. COL1A2 is the gene for the pro alpha 2 chain of type I collagen. COL2A1 is the gene for the pro alpha 1 chain of type II collagen.

The pathway of collagen biosynthesis is complex. The pathway of collagen biosynthesis involves at least eight enzyme-catalyzed posttranslational steps. # ELASTIN CONFERS EXTENSIBILITY & RECOIL

Elastin is a connective tissue protein that confers extensibility in tissues. Elastin is a connective tissue protein that confers elastic recoil in tissues. Elastin is present in large amounts in the lung. Elastin is present in large amounts in large arterial blood vessels. Elastin is present in large amounts in some elastic ligaments. Smaller quantities of elastin are found in skin. Smaller quantities of elastin are found in ear cartilage. Smaller quantities of elastin are found in several other tissues. Only one genetic type of elastin is known. Variants of elastin arise by alternative RNA splicing. Elastin is synthesized as a soluble monomer of approximately 70 kDa called tropoelastin. Some prolines in tropoelastin are hydroxylated to hydroxyproline by prolyl hydroxylase. Hydroxylysine is not present in elastin. Glycosylated hydroxylysine is not present in elastin. Tropoelastin is not synthesized in a proform with extension peptides. Elastin does not contain repeat Gly-X-Y sequences. Elastin does not have a triple helical structure. Elastin does not contain carbohydrate moieties. After secretion from the cell, certain lysyl residues of tropoelastin are oxidatively deaminated to aldehydes by lysyl oxidase. The major cross-links formed in elastin are the desmosines. Desmosines result from the condensation of three lysine-derived aldehydes with an unmodified lysine to form a tetrafunctional cross-link unique to elastin. Once cross-linked, elastin is highly insoluble. Once cross-linked, elastin is extremely stable. Once cross-linked, elastin has a very low turnover rate. The random coil conformations in elastin's structure permit the protein to stretch. The random coil conformations in elastin's structure permit the protein to recoil during the performance of its physiologic functions. Deletions in the elastin gene have been found in approximately 90% of subjects with Williams-Beuren syndrome. The mutations affect the synthesis of elastin and probably play a causative role in the supravalvular aortic stenosis often found in this condition. Fragmentation or a decrease of elastin is found in conditions such as pulmonary emphysema. Fragmentation or a decrease of elastin is found in conditions such as cutis laxa. # FIBRILLINS ARE STRUCTURAL COMPONENTS OF MICROFIBRILS

Microfibrils are fine fiber-like strands 10 to 12 nm in diameter. Microfibrils provide a scaffold for the deposition of elastin in the ECM. Fibrillins are large glycoproteins about 350 kDa. Fibrillins are major structural components of microfibrils. Fibrillins are secreted into the ECM by fibroblasts. Fibrillins become incorporated into the insoluble microfibrils after proteolytic cleavage. Fibrillin-1 is the main fibrillin present in humans. Fibrillin-2 has been identified in humans. Fibrillin-3 has been identified in humans. Fibrillin-2 is important in the deposition of microfibrils early in development. Microfibril-associated glycoproteins (MAGPs) are associated with microfibrils. Fibulins are associated with microfibrils. Members of the ADAMTS family are associated with microfibrils. Fibrillin microfibrils are found in elastic fibers. Fibrillin microfibrils are found in elastin-free bundles in the eye. Fibrillin microfibrils are found in elastin-free bundles in the kidney. Fibrillin microfibrils are found in elastin-free bundles in tendons.

## Marfan Syndrome Is Caused by Mutations in the Gene for Fibrillin-1

Marfan syndrome is a relatively prevalent inherited disease affecting connective tissue. Marfan syndrome is inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. Marfan syndrome affects the eyes, causing dislocation of the lens, known as ectopia lentis. Marfan syndrome affects the skeletal system, most patients are tall and exhibit long digits, known as arachnodactyly, and hyperextensibility of the joints. Marfan syndrome affects the cardiovascular system, causing weakness of the aortic media, leading to dilation of the ascending aorta. Abraham Lincoln may have had Marfan syndrome. Most cases of Marfan syndrome are caused by mutations in the gene for fibrillin-1. Mutations in the fibrillin-1 gene result in abnormal fibrillin and/or lower amounts being deposited in the ECM.

Mutations in the fibrillin-1 gene have also been identified as the cause of acromicric dysplasia. Acromicric dysplasia is characterized by short stature, skin thickening, and stiff joints. Congenital contractual arachnodactyly is associated with a mutation in the gene for fibrillin-2.

# FIBRONECTIN IS INVOLVED IN CELL ADHESION & MIGRATION

Fibronectin is a major glycoprotein of the ECM. Fibronectin is also found in a soluble form in plasma. Fibronectin consists of two identical subunits, each about 230 kDa. The subunits are joined by two disulfide bridges near their carboxyl terminals. The gene encoding fibronectin is very large, containing about 50 exons. The RNA produced by its transcription is subject to considerable alternative splicing. As many as 20 different mRNAs have been detected in various tissues.

Fibronectin contains three types of repeating motifs: I, II, and III. The motifs are organized into functional domains, at least seven in total. Functions of these domains include binding fibronectin, heparin, fibrin, collagen, and cell surfaces. Fibronectin contains an Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) sequence that binds to cells via a transmembrane receptor protein. The receptor protein belongs to the integrin class of proteins. The RGD sequence is shared by other proteins in the ECM that bind to integrins on cell plasma membranes. The presence of the RGD sequence in synthetic peptides enables them to inhibit the binding of fibronectin to cells.

The fibronectin integrin receptor interacts indirectly with actin microfilaments present in the cytosol. Large protein complexes form focal adhesions that anchor cells in the ECM and relay signals influencing cell behavior. The interaction of fibronectin with its receptor provides a route for communication between the outside and inside of the cell. Fibronectin is involved in cell migration by providing a binding site for cells, helping them steer through the ECM. The amount of fibronectin around many transformed cells is sharply reduced, partly explaining their faulty interaction with the ECM.

# LAMININ IS A MAJOR PROTEIN COMPONENT OF BASAL LAMINAS

Basal laminas are specialized areas of the ECM that surround epithelial and some other cells, such as muscle cells. Laminin is a glycoprotein with a molecular weight of about 850 kDa. Laminin has a length of 70 nm. Each chain of laminin has genetic variants. Laminin forms networks in basal laminas. Laminin networks are attached to type IV collagen by nidogen. Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are unbranched polysaccharides. Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are made up of repeating disaccharides. One component of these disaccharides is always an amino sugar, either D-glucosamine or D-galactosamine. The other component is a uronic acid, either D-glucuronic acid (GlcUA) or its 5'-epimer, L-iduronic acid (IdUA). Keratan sulfate does not contain a uronic acid. There are at least seven types of GAGs: hyaluronic acid (hyaluronan), chondroitin sulfate, keratan sulfates I and II, heparin, heparan sulfate, and dermatan sulfate. With the exception of hyaluronic acid, all GAGs contain sulfate groups, either as O-esters or as N-sulfate. Hyaluronic acid does not contain sulfate groups. Hyaluronic acid can exist as a polysaccharide in the ECM without covalent attachment to protein. Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and proteoglycans are major components of the ECM. Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and proteoglycans have important biologic roles. Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and proteoglycans are involved in disease processes. Interest in glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and proteoglycans has increased in recent years.

## Attachment to Core Proteins

The linkage between GAGs and their core proteins is generally one of three types.

An O-glycosidic bond exists between xylose (Xyl) and a serine residue on the protein, unique to proteoglycans.

An O-glycosidic bond exists between xylose (Xyl) and a serine residue on the protein, unique to proteoglycans. This linkage is formed by transferring a Xyl residue to serine from UDP-xylose.

Two residues of galactose (Gal) are added to the Xyl residue, forming a link trisaccharide, Gal-Gal-Xyl.

Further chain growth of the GAG occurs on the terminal Gal.

An O-glycosidic bond exists between N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and serine (Ser) or threonine (Thr), present in keratan sulfate.

An O-glycosidic bond exists between N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and serine (Ser) or threonine (Thr), present in keratan sulfate. This bond is formed by donating a GalNAc residue to Ser or Thr, using UDP-GalNAc as the donor.

An N-glycosylamine bond exists between N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and the amide nitrogen of asparagine (Asn), as found in N-linked glycoproteins.

The synthesis of the core proteins occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum.

Formation of at least some of the above linkages also occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum.

Most of the later steps in the biosynthesis of GAG chains and their subsequent modifications occur in the Golgi apparatus.

## Chain Elongation

Nucleotide sugars are used in the synthesis of the oligosaccharide chains of GAGs. Golgi-located glycosyltransferases are used in the synthesis of the oligosaccharide chains of GAGs. The 'one enzyme, one linkage' relationship is applicable in the synthesis of GAGs. The 'one enzyme, one linkage' relationship is similar to certain types of linkages found in glycoproteins. The enzyme systems involved in chain elongation are capable of high-fidelity reproduction of complex GAGs.

## Chain Termination

Sulfation results in chain termination.

Sulfation particularly affects certain positions of the sugars.

The progression of the growing GAG chain away from the membrane site causes chain termination.

The membrane site is where catalysis occurs.

## Further Modifications

After the formation of the GAG chain, numerous chemical modifications occur.

Chemical modifications after the formation of the GAG chain include the introduction of sulfate groups onto GalNAc and other moieties.

Modifications also involve the epimerization of GlcUA to IdUA residues.

The enzymes that catalyze sulfation are called sulfotransferases.

Sulfotransferases use 3'-phosphoadenosine-5'-phosphosulfate (PAPS) as the sulfate donor.

PAPS is also known as active sulfate.

Sulfotransferases are located in the Golgi.

Sulfotransferases are highly specific.

Distinct sulfotransferases catalyze sulfation at different positions on the acceptor sugars, such as carbons 2, 3, 4, and 6.

An epimerase catalyzes the conversion of glucuronyl to iduronyl residues.

## Proteoglycans Are Important in the Structural Organization of the Extracellular Matrix

Proteoglycans are found in every tissue of the body. Proteoglycans are mainly located in the extracellular matrix or ground substance. The ground substance is transparent and colorless. The ground substance fills spaces between cells. The ground substance largely consists of proteoglycans. Proteoglycans are associated with each other. Proteoglycans are associated with collagen. Proteoglycans are associated with elastin. Some proteoglycans bind to collagen. Some proteoglycans bind to elastin. The interactions determine the structural organization of the matrix. Some proteoglycans, such as decorin, can bind growth factors. Binding of growth factors by proteoglycans modulates their effects on cells. Some proteoglycans interact with adhesive proteins. Proteoglycans interact with fibronectin. Proteoglycans interact with laminin. The glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in proteoglycans are polyanions. GAGs bind polycations. The binding ability attracts water into the ECM by osmotic pressure. Water attraction by GAGs contributes to the ECM's turgor. GAGs can gel at relatively low concentrations. The polysaccharide chains of GAGs are long and extended. The extended nature of GAGs allows proteoglycans to act as sieves. Proteoglycans restrict the passage of large macromolecules into the ECM. Proteoglycans allow relatively free diffusion of small molecules. Proteoglycans form huge macromolecular aggregates. The aggregates occupy a large volume of the matrix relative to proteins.

## Hyaluronic Acid

Hyaluronic acid consists of an unbranched chain of repeating disaccharide units containing glucuronic acid (GlcUA) and GlcNAc.

Hyaluronic acid is present in bacteria and is found in the ECM of nearly all animal tissues.

Hyaluronic acid is especially high in concentration in highly hydrated types such as skin and umbilical cord.

Hyaluronic acid is also found in bone, cartilage, joints (synovial fluid), vitreous humor in the eye, and embryonic tissues.

Hyaluronic acid plays an important role in permitting cell migration during morphogenesis and wound repair.

The ability of hyaluronic acid to attract water into the ECM triggers loosening of the matrix, aiding cell migration.

High concentrations of hyaluronic acid together with chondroitin sulfates in cartilage contribute to its compressibility.

## Chondroitin Sulfates (Chondroitin 4-Sulfate & Chondroitin 6-Sulfate)

Proteoglycans linked to chondroitin sulfate by the Xyl-Ser O-glycosidic bond are prominent components of cartilage. The repeating disaccharide in chondroitin sulfate is similar to that found in hyaluronic acid. The repeating disaccharide in chondroitin sulfate contains GlcUA. The repeating disaccharide in chondroitin sulfate contains GalNAc, which replaces GlcNAc. The GalNAc in chondroitin sulfate is substituted with sulfate at either its 4' or 6' position. Approximately one sulfate is present per disaccharide unit in chondroitin sulfate.

Chondroitin sulfates have an important role in maintaining the structure of the ECM. Chondroitin sulfates are located at sites of calcification in endochondral bone. Chondroitin sulfates are found in cartilage. Chondroitin sulfates are found in high amounts in the ECM of the central nervous system. In addition to their structural function, chondroitin sulfates are thought to act as signaling molecules. Chondroitin sulfates are thought to prevent the repair of nerve endings after injury.

## Keratan Sulfates I & II

The keratan sulfates consist of repeating Gal-GlcNAc disaccharide units with sulfate attached to the 6' position of GlcNAc or occasionally of Gal. Keratan sulfate I was originally isolated from the cornea. Keratan sulfate II was isolated from cartilage. The classification of keratan sulfates I and II is based on their different linkages to the core protein. In the eye, keratan sulfates lie between collagen fibrils. Keratan sulfates play a critical role in corneal transparency. Changes in proteoglycan composition found in corneal scars disappear when the cornea heals.

## Heparin

The repeating disaccharide heparin contains glucosamine (GlcN). The repeating disaccharide heparin contains either D-glucuronic acid (GlcUA) or L-iduronic acid (IdUA) (Figure 49-9). Most of the amino groups of the GlcN residues are N-sulfated. A few of the amino groups of the GlcN residues are acetylated (GlcNAc). The GlcN carries a sulfate attached to carbon 6.

## Heparan Sulfate

Heparan sulfate is present in a proteoglycan found on many extracellular cell surfaces. Heparan sulfate contains GlcN with fewer N-sulfates than heparin. Heparan sulfate has a predominant uronic acid of GlcUA. Heparan sulfates are associated with the plasma membrane of cells. The core proteins of heparan sulfates span the plasma membrane. Heparan sulfates may act as receptors. Heparan sulfates may participate in the mediation of cell growth. Heparan sulfates may participate in cell-cell communication. Heparan sulfate mediates the attachment of cells to their substratum in culture. Heparan sulfate is found in the basement membrane of the kidney. Heparan sulfate is found with type IV collagen in the kidney basement membrane. Heparan sulfate is found with laminin in the kidney basement membrane. Heparan sulfate plays a major role in determining the charge selectiveness of glomerular filtration.

## Dermatan Sulfate

Dermatan sulfate is widely distributed in animal tissues. Dermatan sulfate's structure is similar to that of chondroitin sulfate. Formation of the IdUA in dermatan sulfate occurs by 5'-epimerization of GlcUA. This process is similar to that in heparin and heparan sulfate. The degree of sulfation regulates the epimerization in dermatan sulfate. Because sulfation is incomplete, dermatan sulfate contains both IdUA-GalNAc and GlcUA-GalNAc disaccharides. Dermatan sulfate is the main glycosaminoglycan in skin. Dermatan sulfate may play a part in blood coagulation. Dermatan sulfate may also be involved in wound repair. Dermatan sulfate may contribute to resistance to infection. Proteoglycans are found in intracellular locations such as the nucleus. In the nucleus, proteoglycans are thought to have a regulatory role in functions such as cell proliferation. Proteoglycans also participate in the transport of molecules between the nucleus and the cytosol. Glycosaminoglycans act as structural components of the extracellular matrix. Glycosaminoglycans have specific interactions with proteins such as collagen, elastin, fibronectin, laminin, and growth factors. As polyanions, glycosaminoglycans bind polycations and cations. Glycosaminoglycans contribute to the characteristic turgor of various tissues. Glycosaminoglycans act as sieves in the extracellular matrix. Hyaluronic acid facilitates cell migration. Hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate play roles in the compressibility of cartilage in weight bearing. Dermatan sulfate plays a role in corneal transparency. Dermatan sulfate has a structural role in the sclera. Heparin acts as an anticoagulant. Heparan sulfate is a component of plasma membranes. Heparan sulfate may act as receptors and participate in cell adhesion and cell–cell interactions. Heparan sulfate determines the charge selectiveness of the renal glomerulus. Heparan sulfate is a component of synaptic and other vesicles. Heparan sulfate has a role in nuclear functions such as cell proliferation and transport of molecules between the nucleus and the cytosol.

Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) are synthesized and degraded. In adult tissues, glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) generally exhibit relatively slow turnover. The half-lives of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in adult tissues range from days to weeks.

# BONE IS AFFECTED BY MANY METABOLIC & GENETIC DISORDERS

Osteogenesis imperfecta is characterized by abnormal fragility of bones. The sclera of the eye in osteogenesis imperfecta is often abnormally thin and translucent. The sclera of the eye in osteogenesis imperfecta appears blue due to a deficiency of connective tissue. Eight types (I-VIII) of osteogenesis imperfecta have been recognized. Types I to IV of osteogenesis imperfecta are caused by mutations in the COL1A1 or COL1A2 genes or both. Type I osteogenesis imperfecta is mild. Type II osteogenesis imperfecta is severe. Infants born with type II osteogenesis imperfecta usually do not survive. Types III and IV of osteogenesis imperfecta are progressive and/or deforming. Over 100 mutations in the COL1A1 and COL1A2 genes have been documented, including partial gene deletions and duplications. Some mutations in the COL1A1 and COL1A2 genes affect RNA splicing. The most frequent type of mutation in the COL1A1 and COL1A2 genes results in the replacement of glycine by another bulkier amino acid. The replacement of glycine by another bulkier amino acid affects the formation of the collagen triple helix. Mutations in the COL1A1 and COL1A2 genes generally result in decreased expression of collagen. Mutations in the COL1A1 and COL1A2 genes result in structurally abnormal pro chains that assemble into abnormal fibrils, weakening the overall structure of bone. When one abnormal chain is present, folding may be prevented. Prevention of folding results in enzymatic degradation of all of the chains. This process is called 'procollagen suicide'. 'Procollagen suicide' is an example of a dominant negative mutation. Types V to VIII of osteogenesis imperfecta are less common. Types V to VIII of osteogenesis imperfecta are caused by mutations in the genes for noncollagen proteins.

Osteopetrosis is characterized by increased bone density. Osteopetrosis is a rare condition due to mutations in the gene for carbonic anhydrase II. Osteopetrosis is characterized by an inability to resorb bone.

Osteoporosis is a generalized progressive reduction in bone tissue mass per unit volume. Osteoporosis is caused by an imbalance between bone resorption and deposition. Primary type 1 osteoporosis commonly occurs in women after menopause. Primary type 1 osteoporosis is mainly due to lack of estrogen. Lack of estrogen leads to increased bone resorption in primary type 1 osteoporosis. Lack of estrogen leads to decreased bone mineralization in primary type 1 osteoporosis. Primary type 2 or senile osteoporosis occurs in both sexes post 75 years. Primary type 2 or senile osteoporosis is more prevalent in women with a ratio of 2:1 female to male. The ratio of mineral to organic elements remains unchanged in the remaining normal bone. Fractures, such as those of the head of the femur, occur very easily in osteoporosis. Fractures in osteoporosis represent a huge burden to both affected patients and society in general.

In the Pfeiffer, Jackson-Weiss, and Crouzon syndromes, there is premature fusion of some bones in the skull, known as craniosynostosis. Thanatophoric dysplasia is the most common neonatal lethal skeletal dysplasia. (Table 49-10)

# THE MAJOR COMPONENTS OF CARTILAGE ARE TYPE II COLLAGEN & CERTAIN PROTEOGLYCANS

There are three types of cartilage. Hyaline (articular) cartilage is the major type. Elastic cartilage is the second type. Fibroelastic cartilage is the third type. Hyaline cartilage contains type II collagen as its major protein component. Elastic cartilage contains elastin. Fibroelastic cartilage contains type I collagen. Cartilage contains proteoglycans, which play an important role in its compressibility. Aggrecan is the major proteoglycan in cartilage. Aggrecan has a complex structure containing several glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), including hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate, and keratan sulfate. Aggrecan also contains both link and core proteins. The core protein of aggrecan has three domains: A, B, and C. Hyaluronic acid binds noncovalently to domain A of the core protein. The link protein stabilizes the hyaluronate-core protein interactions. Keratan sulfate chains are located in domain B. Chondroitin sulfate chains are located in domain C. Both keratan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate are bound covalently to the core protein. The core protein also contains both O- and N-linked oligosaccharide chains. The core proteins of proteoglycans DS-PG I and DS-PG II are homologous to those of CS-PG I and CS-PG II found in bone. Osteoblasts lack the epimerase required to convert glucuronic acid to iduronic acid, which might explain the difference. Cartilage is an avascular tissue. Cartilage obtains most of its nutrients from synovial fluid. Cartilage exhibits slow but continuous turnover. Various proteases, such as collagenases and stromelysin, synthesized by chondrocytes can degrade collagen and other proteins in cartilage. Chondronectin is involved in the attachment of type II collagen to chondrocytes (cartilage cells).

The Principal Proteins Found in Cartilage

Collagen type II is 90-98% of total hyaline cartilage collagen and is composed of three α1(II) chains.
Collagens V, VI, IX, X, XI include type IX, which cross-links to type II collagen, and type XI, which may help control the diameter of type II fibrils.
Cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP) is an important structural component of cartilage and regulates cell movement and attachment.
Aggrecan is the major proteoglycan of cartilage.

# CHONDRODYSPLASIAS ARE HEREDITARY DISORDERS AFFECTING CARTILAGE

Chondrodysplasias are hereditary disorders affecting cartilage. Chondrodysplasias are manifested by short-limbed dwarfism. Chondrodysplasias are manifested by numerous skeletal deformities. Some chondrodysplasias are due to mutations in the COL2A1 gene. Mutations in the COL2A1 gene lead to abnormal forms of type II collagen. Stickler syndrome is an example of a chondrodysplasia. Stickler syndrome is manifested by degeneration of the joint cartilage. Stickler syndrome is manifested by degeneration of the vitreous body of the eye.

Achondroplasia is the best known of the chondrodysplasias. Achondroplasia is the most common cause of short-limbed dwarfism. Individuals with achondroplasia have short limbs. Individuals with achondroplasia have normal trunk size. Individuals with achondroplasia have macrocephaly. Individuals with achondroplasia have other skeletal abnormalities. Achondroplasia is often inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. Many cases of achondroplasia are due to new mutations. Achondroplasia is due to mutations in the gene encoding fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3). Mutations in achondroplasia result in substitution of arginine for glycine (residue number 380) in the transmembrane domain of FGFR3. Mutations in achondroplasia render FGFR3 inactive. No such mutation was found in unaffected individuals.

Mutations in the FGFR3 gene can result in hypochondroplasia. Mutations in the FGFR3 gene can result in thanatophoric dysplasia (types I and II). Mutations in the FGFR3 gene can lead to the SADDAN phenotype. The SADDAN phenotype includes severe achondroplasia with developmental delay and acanthosis nigricans. Acanthosis nigricans is a brown to black hyperpigmentation of the skin.

Other skeletal dysplasias are due to mutations in genes encoding FGF receptors. Certain craniosynostosis syndromes are due to mutations in genes encoding FGF receptors. Diastrophic dysplasia is due to mutation in a sulfate transporter.

\- The major components of the ECM are the structural proteins collagen, elastin, and fibrillin-1, a number of specialized proteins (eg, fibronectin and laminin), and various proteoglycans.
Collagen is the most abundant protein in the animal kingdom; 29 types have been identified. All collagens contain greater or lesser stretches of triple helix and the repeating structure (Gly-X-Y) n.
The biosynthesis of collagen is complex, featuring many posttranslational events, including hydroxylation of proline and lysine.
Diseases associated with impaired synthesis of collagen include scurvy, osteogenesis imperfecta, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and Menkes disease.
Elastin confers extensibility and elastic recoil on tissues. Elastin lacks hydroxylysine, Gly-X-Y sequences, triple helical structure, and sugars, but contains desmosine and isodesmosine cross-links not found in collagen.
Fibrillin-1 is located in microfibrils. Mutations in the gene encoding fibrillin-1 cause Marfan syndrome.
GAGs are made up of repeating disaccharides containing a uronic acid (glucuronic or iduronic) or hexose (galactose) and a hexosamine (galactosamine or glucosamine). Sulfate is also frequently present.
The major GAGs are hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulfate, keratan sulfates I and II, heparin, heparan sulfate, and dermatan sulfate.
GAGs are synthesized by the sequential actions of specific enzymes (glycosyltransferases, epimerases, sulfotransferases, etc) and are degraded by the sequential action of lysosomal hydrolases. Genetic deficiencies of the latter result in mucopolysaccharidoses.
GAGs occur in tissues bound to various proteins (link proteins and core proteins), constituting proteoglycans. These structures are often of very high molecular weight and serve many functions in tissues.
Many components of the ECM bind to integrins, proteins found on the cell surface, and this constitutes one pathway by which the cell exterior can communicate with the interior.
Bone and cartilage are specialized forms of the ECM. Collagen I and hydroxyapatite are the major constituents of bone. Collagen II and certain proteoglycans are major constituents of cartilage.
A number of heritable diseases of bone and of cartilage are caused by mutations in the genes for collagen and proteins involved in bone mineralization and cartilage formation.
