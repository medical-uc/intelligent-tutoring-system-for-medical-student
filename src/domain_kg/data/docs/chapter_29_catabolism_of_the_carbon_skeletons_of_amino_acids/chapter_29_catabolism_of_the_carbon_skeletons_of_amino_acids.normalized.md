# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

Chapter 28 addresses the metabolic fates of the resulting hydrocarbon skeletons of each of the protein amino acids. Chapter 28 addresses the enzymes and intermediates involved in the metabolic fates of the protein amino acids. Chapter 28 addresses several associated metabolic diseases or “inborn errors of metabolism.”

Most disorders of amino acid catabolism are comparatively rare. If left untreated, disorders of amino acid catabolism can result in irreversible brain damage and early mortality. Prenatal or early postnatal detection of metabolic disorders is essential. Timely initiation of treatment for metabolic disorders is essential. The ability to detect the activities of enzymes in cultured amniotic fluid cells facilitates prenatal diagnosis by amniocentesis. In the United States, all states conduct screening tests of newborns for up to 40 metabolic diseases. Screening tests for metabolic diseases include disorders associated with defects in the catabolism of amino acids. The most reliable screening tests use tandem mass spectrometry to detect catabolites suggestive of a given metabolic defect in a few drops of neonate blood. Screening tests for metabolic diseases implicate the absence or lowered activity of one or more specific enzymes.

Mutations of a gene can result in the failure to synthesize the encoded enzyme. Mutations of associated regulatory regions of DNA can result in the failure to synthesize the encoded enzyme. Mutations of a gene can result in the synthesis of a partially nonfunctional enzyme. Mutations of associated regulatory regions of DNA can result in the synthesis of a partially nonfunctional enzyme. Mutations of a gene can result in the synthesis of a completely nonfunctional enzyme. Mutations of associated regulatory regions of DNA can result in the synthesis of a completely nonfunctional enzyme. Mutations that affect enzyme activity can have severe metabolic consequences. Mutations that compromise the three-dimensional structure of an enzyme can have severe metabolic consequences. Mutations that disrupt the catalytic sites of an enzyme can have severe metabolic consequences. Mutations that disrupt the regulatory sites of an enzyme can have severe metabolic consequences. Low catalytic efficiency of a mutant enzyme can result from impaired positioning of residues involved in catalysis. Low catalytic efficiency of a mutant enzyme can result from impaired positioning of residues involved in binding a substrate. Low catalytic efficiency of a mutant enzyme can result from impaired positioning of residues involved in binding a coenzyme. Low catalytic efficiency of a mutant enzyme can result from impaired positioning of residues involved in binding a metal ion. Mutations may impair the ability of certain enzymes to respond appropriately to the signals that modulate their activity. Mutations may alter an enzyme's affinity for an allosteric regulator of activity.

Different mutations can have similar effects on any of the above factors. At a molecular level, these represent distinct molecular diseases. Various mutations may give rise to the same clinical signs and symptoms. Remediation of metabolic disorders of amino acid metabolism consists primarily of feeding diets low in the amino acid whose catabolism is impaired. Ultimately, genetic engineering may be able to permanently correct a given metabolic defect.

# TRANSAMINATION TYPICALLY INITIATES AMINO ACID CATABOLISM

Transamination, catalyzed by a transaminase, is the first catabolic reaction of most protein amino acids.

Proline does not participate in transamination.

Hydroxyproline does not participate in transamination.

Threonine does not participate in transamination.

Lysine does not participate in transamination.

The hydrocarbon skeletons that remain after transamination are degraded to amphibolic intermediates.

## Asparagine & Aspartate Form Oxaloacetate

Asparagine and aspartate form oxaloacetate through reactions involving asparaginase (EC 3.5.1.1) and a transaminase. Asparaginase catalyzes the conversion of asparagine and water into aspartate and ammonium. Transaminase catalyzes the conversion of aspartate and pyruvate into alanine and oxaloacetate. All four carbons of asparagine participate in forming oxaloacetate. All four carbons of aspartate participate in forming oxaloacetate.

## Glutamine & Glutamate Form α-Ketoglutarate

Glutaminase (EC 3.5.1.2) catalyzes the reaction of glutamine and water to form glutamate and ammonium.

A transaminase catalyzes the reaction of glutamate and pyruvate to form alanine and α-ketoglutarate.

Glutamate and aspartate are substrates for the same transaminase.

Metabolic defects in transaminases, which fulfill central amphibolic functions, may be incompatible with life.

No known metabolic defect is associated with the pathways that convert asparagine and glutamine to amphibolic intermediates.

## Tyrosine

Tyrosine is catabolized to amphibolic intermediates through a series of reactions. Tyrosine is transaminated to p-hydroxyphenylpyruvate. P-hydroxyphenylpyruvate is converted to homogentisate. Homogentisate is transformed into maleylacetoacetate. Maleylacetoacetate is changed into fumarylacetoacetate. Fumarylacetoacetate is broken down into fumarate and acetoacetate. Fumarate and acetoacetate are ultimately converted into acetyl-CoA and acetate.

Several metabolic disorders are associated with the tyrosine catabolic pathway. Type I tyrosinemia, also known as tyrosinosis, is caused by a defect in fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase. Therapy for type I tyrosinemia involves a diet low in tyrosine and phenylalanine. Untreated type I tyrosinemia can lead to death from liver failure. Type II tyrosinemia, or Richner-Hanhart syndrome, results from a defect in tyrosine aminotransferase. Neonatal tyrosinemia is due to lowered activity of p-hydroxyphenylpyruvate hydroxylase. Therapy for type II tyrosinemia and neonatal tyrosinemia involves a diet low in protein.

Alkaptonuria provided the basis for Sir Archibald Garrod's early 20th-century ideas on heritable metabolic disorders. # METABOLIC DISORDERS OF BRANCHED-CHAIN AMINO ACID CATABOLISM

Maple syrup urine disease (branched-chain ketonuria, or MSUD) causes the urine to have a maple syrup or burnt sugar odor. The urinary keto acids in MSUD derive principally from leucine. Signs and symptoms of MSUD often include ketoacidosis, neurologic derangements, mental retardation, and a maple syrup odor of urine. The mechanism of toxicity in MSUD is unknown. Early diagnosis by enzymatic analysis is essential in MSUD to avoid brain damage and early mortality. Dietary protein is replaced by an amino acid mixture that lacks leucine, isoleucine, and valine in MSUD. The molecular genetics of MSUD are heterogeneous. Genetic subtypes of MSUD are recognized based on the locus affected. Type II MSUD arises from mutations in the E2 gene. Type III MSUD arises from mutations in the E3 gene. In intermittent branched-chain ketonuria, symptoms occur later in life. In isovaleric acidemia, ingestion of protein-rich foods elevates isovalerate. The impaired enzyme in isovaleric acidemia is isovaleryl-CoA dehydrogenase, EC 1.3.8.4. Vomiting, acidosis, and coma can follow ingestion of excess protein in isovaleric acidemia. Excess amino acids are catabolized to amphibolic intermediates that serve as sources of energy or for the biosynthesis of carbohydrates and lipids.
Transamination is the most common initial reaction of amino acid catabolism.
Metabolic diseases associated with glycine catabolism include glycinuria and primary hyperoxaluria.
Two distinct pathways convert cysteine to pyruvate. Metabolic disorders of cysteine catabolism include cystine-lysinuria, cystine storage disease, and the homocystinurias.
Threonine catabolism merges with that of glycine after threonine aldolase cleaves threonine to glycine and acetaldehyde.
Following transamination, the carbon skeleton of tyrosine is degraded to fumarate and acetoacetate. Metabolic diseases of tyrosine catabolism include tyrosinosis, Richner-Hanhart syndrome, neonatal tyrosinemia, and alkaptonuria.
Metabolic disorders of phenylalanine catabolism include PKU and several hyperphenylalaninemias.
Neither nitrogen of lysine participates in transamination. The same net effect is, however, achieved by the intermediate formation of saccharopine. Metabolic diseases of lysine catabolism include periodic and persistent forms of hyperlysinemia-ammonemia.
The catabolism of leucine, valine, and isoleucine presents many analogies to fatty acid catabolism. Metabolic disorders of branched-chain amino acid catabolism include hypervalinemia, maple syrup urine disease, intermittent branched-chain ketonuria, isovaleric acidemia, and methylmalonic aciduria.
