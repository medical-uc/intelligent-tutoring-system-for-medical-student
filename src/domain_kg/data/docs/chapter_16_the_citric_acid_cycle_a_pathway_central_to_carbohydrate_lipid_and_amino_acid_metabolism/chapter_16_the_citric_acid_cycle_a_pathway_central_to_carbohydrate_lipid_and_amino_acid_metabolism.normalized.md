# Overview

The Citric Acid Cycle is a central pathway in carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid metabolism.

Owen P. McGuinness is the author of the chapter on the Citric Acid Cycle.

# BIOMEDICAL IMPORTANCE

The citric acid cycle is a sequence of reactions in mitochondria. The citric acid cycle is also known as the Krebs cycle. The citric acid cycle is also known as the tricarboxylic acid cycle.

The citric acid cycle couples the oxidation of acetyl-CoA to the reduction of coenzymes. The coenzymes are reoxidized in the electron transport chain. The electron transport chain is linked to the formation of ATP.

The citric acid cycle is the final common pathway for the oxidation of carbohydrate, lipid, and protein. Glucose, fatty acids, and most amino acids are metabolized to acetyl-CoA or intermediates of the cycle.

The citric acid cycle has a central role in gluconeogenesis. The citric acid cycle has a central role in lipogenesis. The citric acid cycle has a central role in the interconversion of amino acids.

Many processes related to the citric acid cycle occur in most tissues. The liver is the only tissue in which all processes related to the citric acid cycle occur to a significant extent.

Large numbers of hepatic cells being damaged has profound repercussions. Large numbers of hepatic cells being replaced by connective tissue has profound repercussions.

Genetic defects of citric acid cycle enzymes are associated with severe neurologic damage. Severe neurologic damage is due to impaired ATP formation in the central nervous system.

Hyperammonemia leads to loss of consciousness. Hyperammonemia leads to coma. Hyperammonemia leads to convulsions.

Impaired activity of the citric acid cycle leads to reduced formation of ATP.

# THE CITRIC ACID CYCLE PROVIDES SUBSTRATES FOR THE RESPIRATORY CHAIN

The citric acid cycle begins with the reaction between the acetyl moiety of acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate, forming citrate. Citrate is a six-carbon tricarboxylic acid. Oxaloacetate is regenerated at the end of the cycle. Only a small amount of oxaloacetate is needed for the oxidation of a large quantity of acetyl-CoA. Oxaloacetate plays a catalytic role in the citric acid cycle.

The citric acid cycle provides the main pathway for ATP formation linked to the oxidation of metabolic fuels. Coenzymes are reduced during the oxidation of acetyl-CoA. The reduced coenzymes are then reoxidized in the respiratory chain. The reoxidation is linked to the formation of ATP through oxidative phosphorylation. The process is aerobic and requires oxygen as the final oxidant of the reduced coenzymes. The enzymes of the citric acid cycle are located in the mitochondrial matrix. Some enzymes are free, while others are attached to the inner mitochondrial membrane and the crista membrane. The enzymes and coenzymes of the respiratory chain are also found in the mitochondrial matrix.

To sustain the citric acid cycle, the number of carbons entering and leaving must be equal. Acetyl-CoA, a two-carbon molecule, combines with oxaloacetate, a four-carbon molecule, to form citrate, a six-carbon molecule. After one turn of the cycle, citrate is converted back to oxaloacetate. If a metabolic pathway adds carbon to the cycle that is not through acetyl-CoA, such as the conversion of pyruvate to oxaloacetate for gluconeogenesis, then there must be an outlet pathway to bring an equal amount of carbon out of the cycle. The entry of carbon into the cycle is called anaplerosis. The exiting of carbon from the cycle is called cataplerosis. Anaplerosis must equal cataplerosis to sustain the citric acid cycle.

The citric acid cycle is the major catabolic pathway for acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA enters the cycle by forming citrate. Reoxidation of the coenzymes in the respiratory chain leads to the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP. For one turn of the cycle, nine ATP are generated via oxidative phosphorylation. One ATP (or GTP) arises at substrate level from the conversion of succinyl-CoA to succinate (Figure 16-1).

# REACTIONS OF THE CITRIC ACID CYCLE GENERATE REDUCING EQUIVALENTS & CO₂

The initial reaction between acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate to form citrate is catalyzed by citrate synthase. Citrate synthase forms a carbon-carbon bond between the methyl carbon of acetyl-CoA and the carbonyl carbon of oxaloacetate. The thioester bond of the resultant citryl-CoA is hydrolyzed, releasing citrate and CoASH. The reaction is exothermic. The coenzyme A released can be recycled in the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Fluoroacetate can inhibit this process. Malonate can inhibit this process. Arsenite can inhibit this process.

Citrate is isomerized to isocitrate by the enzyme aconitase. Aconitase catalyzes the reaction in two steps: dehydration to cis-aconitate and rehydration to isocitrate. Citrate is a symmetrical molecule. Aconitase reacts with citrate asymmetrically. This asymmetric behavior is due to channeling, where the product of citrate synthase is transferred directly to the active site of aconitase without entering free solution. Fatty acid synthesis uses an anaplerotic pathway to add oxaloacetate to the cycle, leading to extra citrate and isocitrate. Excess isocitrate can inhibit aconitase. Channeling ensures that citrate is only available in free solution when aconitase is inhibited by isocitrate accumulation. This allows citrate to be exported out of the cycle for fatty acid synthesis.

Fluoroacetate is a poison found in some plants. Fluoroacetate is toxic to grazing animals. Fluoroacetyl-CoA condenses with oxaloacetate to form fluorocitrate. Fluorocitrate inhibits aconitase. Inhibition of aconitase causes citrate to accumulate.

Isocitrate undergoes dehydrogenation catalyzed by isocitrate dehydrogenase to form oxalosuccinate. There are three isoenzymes of isocitrate dehydrogenase.

\u03b1-Ketoglutarate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation catalyzed by a multienzyme complex similar to that involved in the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate. The \u03b1-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex requires the same cofactors as the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex: thiamin diphosphate, lipoate, NAD\u002b, flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), and CoA. \u03b1-Ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex results in the formation of succinyl-CoA. The equilibrium of this reaction favors succinyl-CoA formation, making it physiologically unidirectional. Arsenite inhibits this reaction, causing \u03b1-ketoglutarate to accumulate. High concentrations of ammonia, seen in liver disease, also inhibit \u03b1-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase.

Succinyl-CoA is converted to succinate by the enzyme succinate thiokinase (succinyl-CoA synthetase). Succinyl-CoA is the only example of substrate-level phosphorylation in the citric acid cycle. Tissues in which gluconeogenesis occurs, such as the liver and kidney, contain two isoenzymes of succinate thiokinase. One isoenzyme of succinate thiokinase is specific for GDP. The other isoenzyme of succinate thiokinase is specific for ADP. The GTP formed is used for the decarboxylation of oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate in gluconeogenesis. Nongluconeogenic tissues have only the isoenzyme that phosphorylates ADP.

When ketone bodies are being metabolized in extrahepatic tissues, an alternative reaction is catalyzed by succinyl-CoA–acetoacetate-CoA transferase (thiophorase). The reaction involves the transfer of CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate. The transfer of CoA results in the formation of acetoacetyl-CoA and succinate.

The first dehydrogenation reaction, forming fumarate, is catalyzed by succinate dehydrogenase. Succinate dehydrogenase is bound to the inner surface of the inner mitochondrial membrane. Succinate dehydrogenase contains FAD and iron-sulfur (Fe-S) protein. Succinate dehydrogenase directly reduces ubiquinone in the electron transport chain. Fumarase catalyzes the addition of water across the double bond of fumarate, yielding malate. Although the equilibrium of this reaction strongly favors malate, the net flux is to oxaloacetate because oxaloacetate is rapidly being used. Oxaloacetate is used for multiple reactions: forming citrate, leaving the mitochondria for gluconeogenesis, or undergoing transamination to form aspartate. NADH is reoxidized to NAD by the respiratory chain.

In the citric acid cycle, two carbon atoms are lost as CO₂ in one turn of the cycle. Two carbon atoms are not derived from the acetyl-CoA that has immediately entered the cycle. Instead, they are derived from the portion of the citrate molecule that was derived from oxaloacetate. After one turn of the cycle, the regenerated oxaloacetate is labeled. Labeled CO₂ is evolved during the second turn of the cycle. Because succinate is a symmetrical compound, 'randomization' of label occurs at this step. All four carbon atoms of oxaloacetate appear labeled after one turn of the cycle. During gluconeogenesis, some of the label in oxaloacetate is incorporated into glucose and glycogen.

## The Citric Acid Cycle Takes Part in Gluconeogenesis, Transamination, & Deamination

All the intermediates of the citric acid cycle are potentially glucogenic because they can give rise to oxaloacetate. Oxaloacetate leads to the production of glucose in the liver and kidney. The key enzyme for transferring out of the cycle into gluconeogenesis is phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase catalyzes the decarboxylation of oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate. GTP acts as the phosphate donor in the reaction catalyzed by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase. The GTP required for this reaction is provided by the GDP-dependent isoenzyme of succinate thiokinase. The mechanism ensures that oxaloacetate is not withdrawn from the cycle for gluconeogenesis unless GTP is supplied. The mechanism prevents the depletion of citric acid cycle intermediates and reduced ATP generation.

Net transfer into the citric acid cycle, known as anaplerosis, occurs through several reactions. One important anaplerotic reaction is the formation of oxaloacetate by the carboxylation of pyruvate, catalyzed by pyruvate carboxylase. The formation of oxaloacetate by the carboxylation of pyruvate maintains an adequate concentration of oxaloacetate for the condensation reaction with acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA acts as an allosteric activator of pyruvate carboxylase. Acetyl-CoA acts as an inhibitor of pyruvate dehydrogenase, ensuring a supply of oxaloacetate. Lactate, an important substrate for gluconeogenesis, enters the cycle by being oxidized to pyruvate. Pyruvate is then carboxylated to oxaloacetate. Transamination of aspartate directly forms oxaloacetate. Various compounds can yield propionyl CoA, which can be carboxylated and isomerized to succinyl CoA, making them important anaplerotic substrates.

Aminotransferase reactions form pyruvate from alanine. Aminotransferase reactions form oxaloacetate from aspartate. Aminotransferase reactions form \alpha -ketoglutarate from glutamate. Aminotransferase reactions are reversible. The citric acid cycle serves as a source of carbon skeletons for the synthesis of these amino acids. Other amino acids contribute to gluconeogenesis by providing carbon skeletons that become citric acid cycle intermediates. Alanine, cysteine, glycine, hydroxyproline, serine, threonine, and tryptophan yield pyruvate. Arginine, histidine, glutamine, and proline yield \alpha -ketoglutarate. Isoleucine, methionine, and valine yield succinyl-CoA. Tyrosine and phenylalanine yield fumarate.

A cataplerotic route is necessary for complete oxidation. Oxaloacetate must leave the cycle to be phosphorylated and carboxylated to phosphoenolpyruvate at the expense of GTP. Phosphoenolpyruvate is then dephosphorylated to form pyruvate by pyruvate kinase. Pyruvate can then undergo oxidative decarboxylation to acetyl-CoA by pyruvate dehydrogenase.

In ruminants, the main metabolic fuel is short-chain fatty acids formed by bacterial fermentation. Propionate is the major glucogenic product of rumen fermentation. Propionate enters the cycle at succinyl-CoA via the methylmalonyl-CoA pathway. Propionate forms glucose through gluconeogenesis.

## The Citric Acid Cycle Takes Part in Fatty Acid Synthesis

Acetyl-CoA is the major substrate for long-chain fatty acid synthesis in nonruminants. In ruminants, acetyl-CoA is derived directly from acetate. Pyruvate dehydrogenase is a mitochondrial enzyme, and fatty acid synthesis occurs in the cytosol. The mitochondrial membrane is impermeable to acetyl-CoA. Citrate is transported from the mitochondrion to the cytosol to make acetyl-CoA available in the cytosol. Citrate lyase catalyzes the cleavage of citrate in the cytosol. Citrate transport out of the mitochondrion is regulated by the inhibition of aconitase by its product. The inhibition of aconitase by its product ensures that citrate is used for fatty acid synthesis only when there is an adequate amount to ensure continued activity of the cycle. The regulation of mitochondrial transport systems involved in cataplerosis and anaplerosis pathways is poorly understood, but they likely play a critical regulatory role in these pathways.

Oxaloacetate released by citrate lyase cannot reenter the mitochondrion. Oxaloacetate is reduced to malate at the expense of NADH. Malate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation to pyruvate, reducing NADP+ to NADPH. The malic enzyme catalyzes this reaction, which is the source of half the NADPH required for fatty acid synthesis. The pentose phosphate pathway provides the remainder of the NADPH required for fatty acid synthesis. Pyruvate enters the mitochondrion and is carboxylated to oxaloacetate by pyruvate carboxylase. This reaction is ATP-dependent and uses the coenzyme biotin. (Figure 16–4)

## Regulation of the Citric Acid Cycle Depends Primarily on a Supply of Oxidized Cofactors

In most tissues, the primary role of the citric acid cycle is in energy-yielding metabolism. Respiratory control via the respiratory chain and oxidative phosphorylation regulates citric acid cycle activity. The availability of ADP is ultimately dependent on the utilization of ATP in chemical and physical work. As ATP usage increases, such as during muscle contraction, this generates ADP to help drive the cycle. If ATP demand is low, the cycle slows down to match the demand. Individual enzymes of the cycle are regulated. Regulatory events help to couple rapid changes in energy demand with citric acid cycle flux. In the brain, which is largely dependent on glucose to supply acetyl-CoA, control of the citric acid cycle may occur at pyruvate dehydrogenase. Citrate synthase is allosterically inhibited by ATP and long-chain fatty acyl-CoA. Mitochondrial NAD-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase is allosterically activated by ADP. Activation by ADP is counteracted by ATP and NADH. Succinate dehydrogenase is inhibited by oxaloacetate. The concentration of oxaloacetate controls the rate of citrate formation. Hyperammonemia leads to loss of consciousness, coma, and convulsions, and may be fatal. Hyperammonemia occurs in advanced liver disease and a number of (rare) genetic diseases of amino acid metabolism. Hyperammonemia lowers concentrations of all citric acid cycle intermediates. Hyperammonemia decreases citric acid cycle flux. Hyperammonemia decreases the generation of ATP. The equilibrium of glutamate dehydrogenase is finely poised. Ammonium ion concentration is elevated in liver disease. Ammonia possibly inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase. Inhibitions by ammonia further decrease citric acid cycle flux.

The citric acid cycle is the final pathway for the oxidation of carbohydrate, lipid, and protein. Their common end-metabolite, acetyl-CoA, reacts with oxaloacetate to form citrate.
The reduced coenzymes are oxidized by the respiratory chain linked to formation of ATP. Thus, the cycle is the major pathway for the formation of ATP. It is located in the matrix of mitochondria adjacent to the enzymes of the respiratory chain and oxidative phosphorylation.
The citric acid cycle is amphibolic since, in addition to oxidation, it is important in the provision of carbon skeletons for gluconeogenesis, acetyl-CoA for fatty acid synthesis, and interconversion of amino acids. For these processes to be sustained, they rely on a balance of anaplerosis and cataplerosis in the citric acid cycle.
