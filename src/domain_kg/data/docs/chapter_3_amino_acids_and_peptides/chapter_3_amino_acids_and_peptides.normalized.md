The R groups of amino acids can be either hydrophilic or hydrophobic. The properties influence the location of amino acids in a protein’s mature folded conformation. Hydrophobic R-groups are indicated by the symbol ф (Phi, uppercase), while hydrophilic R-groups are indicated by the symbol ζ (zeta, lowercase).

Some proteins contain additional amino acids that arise through posttranslational modification. Examples of such modifications include the conversion of peptidyl proline and peptidyl lysine to 4-hydroxyproline and 5-hydroxylysine, the conversion of peptidyl glutamate to γ-carboxyglutamate, and the methylation, formylation, acetylation, prenylation, and phosphorylation of certain aminoacyl residues. Posttranslational modifications increase the diversity of chemical functional groups in proteins and often alter their solubility, stability, catalytic activity, and interaction with other biomolecules.

## Selenocysteine, the 21st Protein L-α-Amino Acid

Selenocysteine is an L-α-amino acid present in proteins from every domain of life. Humans contain approximately two dozen selenoproteins. Selenoproteins include certain peroxidases and reductases. Selenoprotein P circulates in the plasma. The iodothyronine deiodinases are responsible for converting the prohormone thyroxine (T) to the thyroid hormone 3,3′,5-triiodothyronine (T). Peptidy selenocysteine is inserted directly into a growing polypeptide during translation. Peptidy selenocysteine is commonly termed the '21st amino acid'. Unlike the other 20 protein amino acids, the incorporation of selenocysteine is specified by a large and complex genetic element. The genetic element is for the unusual transfer RNA (tRNA) called tRNASec. The tRNASec utilizes the UGA anticodon that normally signals STOP. The protein synthetic apparatus recognizes a UGA codon as coding for selenocysteine when accompanied by a stem-loop structure. The stem-loop structure is the selenocysteine insertion element in the untranslated region of the mRNA.

## Stereochemistry of the Protein Amino Acids

With the sole exception of glycine, the α-carbon of every amino acid is chiral. All protein amino acids share the absolute configuration of L-glyceraldehyde and are defined as L-α-amino acids. Some protein amino acids are dextrorotatory and some are levorotatory. Almost all protein amino acids are (S). L-Cysteine is (R) because the atomic mass of the sulfur atom on C3 exceeds that of the amino group on C2. The failure to use (R) or (S) to express absolute stereochemistry is not a mere historical aberration. In mammals, the biochemical reactions of L-α-amino acids, their precursors, and their catabolites are catalyzed by enzymes that act exclusively on L-isomers, irrespective of their absolute configuration.

## L-α-Amino Acids Serve Additional Metabolic Roles

L-α-Amino acids fulfill vital metabolic roles in addition to serving as the “building blocks” of proteins. Ornithine is a key intermediate in the urea cycle. Citrulline is a key intermediate in the urea cycle. S-adenosyl-methionine serves as a methyl-group donor for many enzyme-catalyzed reactions. Tyrosine is a precursor of thyroid hormone. Tyrosine is metabolized to produce epinephrine. Tyrosine is metabolized to produce norepinephrine. Tyrosine is metabolized to produce dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA). Phenylalanine is metabolized to produce epinephrine. Phenylalanine is metabolized to produce norepinephrine. Phenylalanine is metabolized to produce dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA). Glutamate is a neurotransmitter. Glutamate is a precursor of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA).

## Certain Plant L-α-Amino Acids Can Adversely Impact Human Health

The consumption of plants that contain certain nonprotein amino acids can adversely impact human health. Neurolathyrism, a profound neurologic disorder characterized by progressive and irreversible spastic paralysis of the legs, is caused by dietary intake of the seeds and seed products of the legume Lathyrus. Lathyrism occurs widely during famines, when Lathyrus seeds may become a major part of the diet. L-α-amino acids serve as important precursors of the toxins responsible, which include β-N-oxalyl-L-α,β-diaminopropionic acid (β-ODAP Table 3–2) and β-N-oxalyl L-alanine (BOAA) in “grass pea,” that is, Lathyrus sativus. The osteolathyrogen γ-glutamyl-β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN) is present in “sweet pea.” The seeds of some species also contain α,γ-diaminobutyric acid, an analogue of ornithine that inhibits the hepatic urea cycle enzyme ornithine transcarbamylase, leading to ammonia toxicity. L-β-methylaminoalanine, a neurotoxic amino acid present in Cycad seeds, has been implicated as a risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis-Parkinson dementia complex in natives of Guam who consume fruit bats that feed on cycad fruit or flour made from cycad seeds.

## p Ka Values Express the Strengths of Weak Acids & Bases

The use of a single, continuous scale enables the relative strengths of all groups capable of dissociably binding protons to be compared.

The terms “weak acid” and “weak base” constitute arbitrary labels since in any protonic equilibrium both acidic and basic forms must participate.

In the laboratory, altering the charge on amino acids and their derivatives by varying the pH facilitates the physical separation of amino acids, peptides, and proteins.

## At Its Isoelectric pH (pI), an Amino Acid Bears No Net Charge

Zwitterions are an example of an isoelectric species. An isoelectric species is a molecule with an equal number of positive and negative charges. A molecule with an equal number of positive and negative charges is electrically neutral.

The isoelectric pH (pI) of alanine is calculated as (2.35 + 9.69) / 2. The isoelectric pH (pI) of alanine equals 6.02.

For polyprotic acids, identifying the isoionic species is necessary. Identifying the isoionic species is necessary to calculate the pI.

For aspartic acid, the pI is calculated as (2.09 + 3.96) / 2. The pI calculation for aspartic acid equals 3.02.

In clinical laboratories, knowledge of the pI guides the selection of conditions. The conditions for electrophoretic separations are selected based on the pI.

At pH 7.0, electrophoresis will separate two molecules with pI values of 6.0 and 8.0, because the molecule with a pI of 6.0 will have a net negative charge, and the molecule with a pI of 8.0 will have a net positive charge.

Chromatographic separations on ionic supports consider these principles. Diethylaminoethyl (DEAE) cellulose is an example of an ionic support used in chromatographic separations.

## p Ka Values Vary With the Environment

The environment of a dissociable group affects its pK. An adjacent oppositely charged group can stabilize a developing charge. An adjacent similarly charged group can destabilize a developing charge.

## The Solubility of Amino Acids Reflects Their Ionic Character

Amino acids are readily soluble in polar solvents such as water and ethanol due to the charges from their dissociable functional groups. Amino acids are insoluble in nonpolar solvents like benzene, hexane, or ether. Amino acids do not absorb visible light, making them colorless. Tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan absorb ultraviolet light at high wavelengths, specifically between 250-290 nm. Tryptophan absorbs ultraviolet light about 10 times more efficiently than phenylalanine or tyrosine. Tryptophan is the major contributor to the ability of most proteins to absorb light at around 280 nm (Figure 3-6).

# THE α-R GROUPS DETERMINE THE PROPERTIES OF AMINO ACIDS

Each functional group of an amino acid exhibits all of its characteristic chemical reactions. Carboxylic acid groups form esters, amides, and acid anhydrides. Amino groups undergo acylation, amidation, and esterification. —OH and —SH groups undergo oxidation and esterification. Glycine, the smallest amino acid, often occurs where peptides bend sharply because it can be accommodated in places inaccessible to other amino acids. Alanine, valine, leucine, and isoleucine have hydrophobic R groups and occur primarily in the interior of cytosolic proteins. Phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan have aromatic R groups and occur primarily in the interior of cytosolic proteins. Charged R groups of basic and acidic amino acids stabilize specific protein conformations via ionic interactions, or salt bridges. Ionic interactions stabilize specific protein conformations via ionic interactions, or salt bridges. These interactions function in 'charge relay' systems during enzymatic catalysis and electron transport in respiring mitochondria. Histidine plays unique roles in enzymatic catalysis. At a distinctly acidic pH, selenocysteine should be the better nucleophile. Serine's primary alcohol functions as the active site nucleophile in trypsin and other serine proteases. Threonine's secondary alcohol group is not known to serve this role in catalysis. The —OH groups of serine, tyrosine, and threonine frequently serve as the points of covalent attachment for phosphoryl groups that regulate protein function.

## Amino Acid Sequence Determines Primary Structure

Amino acids are linked together by peptide bonds. The number and order of the amino acid residues in a polypeptide constitute its primary structure. Peptides are named as derivatives of the carboxy terminal aminoacyl residue. The 'ine' ending on the carboxy-terminal residue indicates that its α-carboxyl group is not involved in a peptide bond. Three-letter abbreviations linked by straight lines represent an unambiguous primary structure. Lines are omitted when using single-letter abbreviations. Peptides are written with the residue that bears the free α-amino group at the left. Prefixes like tri- or octa- denote peptides with three or eight residues, respectively. Amino acids present in peptides, called aminoacy, are referred to by replacing the 'ine' or 'ic' suffixes of free amino acids with 'yl' (eg, alanyl, aspartyl, tyrosyl). For example, Lys-Leu-Tyr-Gln is called lysyl-leucyl-tyrosyl-glutamine.

## The Peptide Bond Has Partial Double-Bond Character

The peptide bond connecting a carbonyl carbon to the α-nitrogen has partial double-bond character. The partial double-bond character prevents rotation about this bond. The O, C, N, and H atoms of a peptide bond are coplanar. The semirigidity of the peptide bond influences the folding of peptides and proteins into their mature 3D structures. Free rotation can occur about the bonds connecting the α-carbon with the α-nitrogen and with the α-carbonyl carbon (Figure 3-8).

## Noncovalent Forces Constrain Peptide Conformations

Folding of a peptide probably begins coincident with its biosynthesis. The mature, physiologically active conformation reflects the collective contributions of noncovalent interactions. Hydrogen bonding is an example of a noncovalent interaction. Hydrophobic interactions are another example of noncovalent interactions. The minimization of steric hindrance between residues contributes to the peptide's conformation. Common repeating conformations include α-helices. Common repeating conformations include β-pleated sheets.

## Peptides Are Polyelectrolytes

The peptide bond is uncharged at any pH of physiologic interest.

Formation of peptides from amino acids is accompanied by a net loss of one positive and one negative charge per peptide bond formed.

Peptides' terminal carboxyl and amino groups and, where present, their acidic or basic R groups render peptides in water charged.

The net charge on a peptide depends on the pH of its environment and on the pK values of its dissociating groups.

Both D-amino acids and non–α-amino acids occur in nature, but proteins are synthesized using only L-α-amino acids. D-Amino acids do, however, serve metabolic roles, not only in bacteria but also in humans.
Metabolic functions of the L-α-Amino acids include the biosynthesis of urea, heme, nucleic acids, hormones such as epinephrine, and neurotransmitters such as DOPA.
Certain of the L-α-amino acids present in plants and plant seeds can have deleterious effects on human health, for example, in lathyrism.
Amino acids are classified as basic, acidic, aromatic, aliphatic, or sulfur-containing based on the composition and properties of their R groups.
The partial double-bond character of the bond that links the carbonyl carbon and the nitrogen of a peptide renders the four atoms of the peptide bond coplanar, which restricts the number of possible peptide conformations.
Peptides are often named as derivatives of the carboxyl terminal residue. The primary structure of a peptide is its amino acid sequence, starting from the amino-terminal residue, the direction in which peptides actually are synthesized in vivo.
All amino acids possess at least two weakly acidic functional groups, R—NH + and R—COOH. Many also possess additional weakly acidic functional groups such as the phenolic —OH of tyrosine or the —SH, guanidino, or imidazole moieties of cysteine, histidine, and arginine, respectively.
The pK values of all functional groups of an amino acid or of a peptide dictate its net charge at a given pH.
pI, the isoelectric pH, is the pH at which a molecule bears no net charge.
The pK values of amino acids in proteins can be influenced by their surroundings, and hence may from those of the corresponding free amino acids.
